# Channel 3 — Public Domain Dark Short Fiction Adaptations
*A founding strategy document — May 2026*

A future channel. Not launching this week, not launching this month. The strategic decision and the research discipline starts now; the channel itself launches once Final Hours and Success Coach are stable, Hetzner is operational, and the freelance review pipeline is humming. Roughly 4-6 months from now.

This is the document that captures the decision and shapes the interim research so the eventual launch is fast, deliberate, and well-positioned rather than reactive.

---

## The thesis

Take public domain dark short fiction — Saki, M.R. James, Ambrose Bierce, occasionally Poe — and adapt single stories into 12-20 minute photoreal cinematic short films. One story per video. Standalone narrative works, not algorithmically-optimised content. Festival-eligible. Genuine artistic ambition.

Three reasons this works.

**The lane is empty in a way that's almost unbelievable.** A YouTube search for *Sredni Vashtar* (one of Saki's most famous stories) returns three videos: a 905-view audio upload of an Alfred Hitchcock TV episode from eight years ago, a 504-view book-summary video, and a 79-view "visual audiobook" from a month ago. *Oh, Whistle and I'll Come to You* (M.R. James's most famous tale): the best dedicated adaptation has 158 views. These are *legendary* short stories that have been on university literature syllabi for a century, and nobody is producing serious cinematic AI adaptations of them. The vacuum is real.

**The moat already partially covers this work.** Final Hours' rulebook has been pressure-tested on Edwardian period clothing, period interiors, candlelit and gas-lit scenes, ship railings, instrument detail. Saki wrote between 1900 and 1916; M.R. James from the 1890s through the 1920s — the *exact* period Final Hours has been accumulating rules against. Channel 3 launches with maybe 80% of its rulebook inherited day one. New channels normally start cold; this one starts warm.

**Festival circuits are professionalising fast and the category is being defined now.** Reply AI Film Festival premieres in Venice during the Biennale window. Runway AIF galas in NYC and LA. LifeArt offers IMDb-qualifying status. AI Zone, Red Rocks, AI International Film Festival running monthly. None of these have yet anointed the canonical "literary AI adaptation" filmmaker. The category is wide open for someone with craft, taste, and consistent output to actually own.

---

## What this channel is, in three sentences

Cinematic AI short film adaptations of public domain dark short fiction. One story per film, 12-20 minutes, photoreal painterly cinematic, AI-narrated in a voice register matched to each author's prose, designed to be watched as standalone works rather than algorithmic content. Submitted to AI film festivals quarterly; published on YouTube as the public-facing home.

## The strongest 8 candidate stories

Ranked by pipeline-fit (how much of the rulebook and visual moat carries over from Final Hours) and by adaptation cinematic potential. All authors public domain. All stories around 4,000-7,000 words, ideal for 12-20 minute adaptation.

**Sredni Vashtar (Saki, 1911).** A frail boy under the suffocating guardianship of a cruel aunt secretly worships a polecat-ferret kept in the garden shed as a dark god, and prays for vengeance. Devastating final image. Edwardian English domestic plus rural shed exterior. Pipeline fit: near perfect — period clothing, gas-lit interiors, controlled outdoor scene. The strongest opening video for the channel.

**The Open Window (Saki, 1914).** A nervous houseguest is told by a 15-year-old girl that the open window of the country house parlour is a memorial to her uncles and brother lost three years ago in a moor accident — until the men return through it. The twist is one of the most economical in English literature. Edwardian country house parlour, exterior moor view. Pipeline fit: high.

**The Music on the Hill (Saki, 1911).** A young wife moves with her bookish husband to a country estate where the old pagan god Pan still rules the landscape; she mocks the warnings and is hunted to death by stags. Genuinely chilling Edwardian rural pagan dread. Pipeline fit: high — landscape work, rural buildings, period dress.

**Oh, Whistle, and I'll Come to You, My Lad (M.R. James, 1904).** An antiquarian academic on a golfing holiday at a remote coastal hotel uncovers an ancient whistle in old graveyard ruins; blows it; is visited at night by something terrible. Edwardian seaside hotel, ruins, beach. The canonical M.R. James ghost story. Pipeline fit: high.

**The Mezzotint (M.R. James, 1904).** A museum curator notices that a mezzotint engraving of an old country house changes when no one is looking — a figure approaches the house across the lawn over successive nights, takes a child, returns. Edwardian academic interior plus the picture's depicted scene. Pipeline fit: very high — the entire story is paintings, candle-lit studies, slow revelation.

**Casting the Runes (M.R. James, 1911).** A vengeful occultist scholar passes a paper inscribed with runes to an enemy at a public meeting; whoever holds the paper is hunted to death by an invisible elemental in three months. Edwardian London, train carriage, lectures. Pipeline fit: high.

**Lost Hearts (M.R. James, 1895).** An orphan boy is brought to the lonely manor of his eccentric scholar uncle; the previous children the uncle "adopted" never came home. Children's ghosts visible only briefly. Victorian manor house, English garden, child protagonist. Pipeline fit: medium — child characters require careful canon work but Final Hours has Anne Boleyn's children-in-the-tower as partial precedent.

**An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge (Ambrose Bierce, 1890).** A Confederate sympathiser is hanged from a railway bridge during the American Civil War; the story chronicles his apparent escape in vivid detail across a long final paragraph that reveals it was the last instant of his dying mind. One of the most famous short stories in American literature. American Civil War setting — new visual era for the pipeline, requires fresh rulebook accumulation. Pipeline fit: lower (period drift) but story strength: enormous.

## Why these specific eight

All eight share three features that matter for AI cinematic adaptation specifically:

**Interior-heavy or controlled-exterior settings.** No battle scenes, no crowds in motion, no fast action. Saki and James wrote for the literary tradition of dread building inside drawing rooms and gardens. The "atmospheric over action" rule the Final Hours rulebook learned from Pompeii is the same rule these stories want.

**One-to-three named characters per story.** Canon work scales. *Sredni Vashtar* has a boy, an aunt, and a ferret; *The Mezzotint* has one curator and a sequence of static painting-scenes; *Owl Creek* has one condemned man in three timeframes. Each can be canonised tightly. Compare to the 14-character drift problem six_minutes hit with vague modern character descriptions — these stories' character economy makes that problem much smaller.

**Period exactness already partially in the moat.** Edwardian English domestic clothing, gas-light, panelled interiors, period instruments, candlelit studies — Final Hours has already pressure-tested all of these and banked the rules. Channel 3 inherits without paying the rule-accumulation tax that Success Coach is currently paying for modern content.

The two exceptions — *Lost Hearts* (Victorian, slightly earlier) and *Owl Creek* (American Civil War, completely new visual era) — are intentionally in the list to test whether the pipeline can handle adjacent and distant periods cleanly. *Lost Hearts* is a small step from Edwardian and worth attempting. *Owl Creek* is a big step and probably belongs at video 8-10 of the channel, not video 1.

## What "research intensively while we build" looks like, operationally

The four months between now and channel launch shouldn't be passive. Specific things worth doing in the background without distracting from Final Hours and Success Coach:

**Read the candidate stories properly.** Not summaries — the actual texts. They're all 4,000-7,000 words; reading the full eight is a six-hour project. Make notes on each: which beats are inherently cinematic, which require visual reframing, which characters could appear across multiple stories if useful (M.R. James in particular has recurring antiquarian protagonist types that could become canon templates).

**Bank "literary adaptation" notes to the Final Hours rulebook when they apply universally.** As Final Hours videos hit new period-clothing or interior-rendering challenges through 2026, ask each time: does this rule also apply to Channel 3's source material? If yes, it lives in universal/shared rulebook. If only Final Hours, it stays Final Hours-specific. The discipline of asking the question is what compounds value across the eventual three channels.

**Watch what wins at the AI film festivals.** Reply AIFF and Runway AIF are publishing finalist reels. LifeArt is screening winners. Watch them. Note which aesthetic registers, which lengths, which narrative structures get jury attention. The category is being defined now; the channel needs to enter knowing what "good" looks like at this specific moment.

**Track the technology improvements that benefit Channel 3 specifically.** The avatar problem we parked is the central technical risk for adapting literary fiction where characters are central. IP-Adapter integration, LoRA training, video models with shot-consistency — each of these gets the channel closer to viable. Bank monthly check-ins. By launch day, the avatar question should be resolved or have a clean workaround.

**Reserve channel naming and branding.** Doesn't need to be done today, but worth thinking about. Names that signal *literary adaptation*, *short film*, *dark fiction*, *the canon of strange stories* — not "AI Stories" or "AI Films" which trap the channel inside a technology framing rather than a craft framing. Strong working names worth banking and stress-testing: *The Lantern Hour*, *Chamber Pieces*, *The Vault of Quiet Horrors*, *The Old Library*, *A Strange Library*. None of these are committed; they're seeds to think about over coming weeks.

**Skim the public domain frontier.** The list of eight stories is a starting point, not the universe. Other authors worth investigating as the channel develops: Edith Wharton's ghost stories (*Afterward*, *Bewitched* — Edwardian, female protagonists), Charlotte Perkins Gilman (*The Yellow Wallpaper* — Victorian domestic horror), Algernon Blackwood (some works public domain, magnificent atmospheric horror), Robert W. Chambers (*The King in Yellow* fragments), William Hope Hodgson's Carnacki tales (Edwardian occult investigator, episodic potential). When the channel has shipped its first 10 videos and has audience signal about what register wins, the second tranche of candidates becomes very valuable.

## Festival positioning

The clearest entry strategy: aim three Channel 3 films per year at the three highest-prestige AI festivals. Specifically:

**Reply AIFF (Venice premiere, June submission deadline annually).** Highest-prestige AI film festival currently. One Channel 3 film per year submitted here, timed to be the strongest work of the year. Reply finalists premiere during the Venice Biennale; even being shortlisted is a credential.

**Runway AIF (NYC and LA galas, rolling deadlines).** Strong industry attendance, technology-savvy jury. Different submission window from Reply, so this can be a separate film. Good for more experimental work.

**LifeArt AI Festival (Hollywood/Mumbai/Athens, IMDb-qualifying).** The one that turns a published film into a real IMDb credit. Worth one submission annually with whichever film best fits their aesthetic.

Three festival submissions per year × $50-200 per submission = $200-600 annual festival budget. Modest cost; meaningful credentialing if any films are shortlisted.

The remaining 6-10 films per year go to YouTube as the channel's primary distribution. YouTube is the audience-building layer; festivals are the prestige layer. Both compound.

## What the launch looks like, when it happens

Concrete shape for when the channel goes live (estimated late 2026 or early 2027):

**Day one publish.** *Sredni Vashtar*. The strongest single story in the lineup, the cleanest pipeline-fit, the most cinematic in execution. The video that establishes what the channel is.

**Months one-two.** *The Open Window* and *The Mezzotint*. Two more strong Saki and M.R. James stories. By video three the audience has a clear sense of the channel's range.

**Festival cycle starts at month three.** First submission goes out — probably *Sredni Vashtar* to one or two early-deadline festivals. The video has had time to settle on YouTube; the festival cycle begins separately.

**Cluster discipline applies.** Even though each film is standalone, three Saki films in the first quarter would help YouTube algorithmically categorise the channel. Don't mix authors randomly in early videos. Saki cluster first, then M.R. James cluster, then Bierce or others.

**Quality bar is hard.** This is the festival channel. The reshoot tolerance is much lower than Final Hours' (which is YouTube-optimised and accepts honest first-version imperfection). A Channel 3 video should be reshot until it would not embarrass you on a festival screen.

## Honest constraints and risks

**Avatar-equivalent challenges for literary characters.** The same problem that broke Coach Alex generation breaks here: rendering a specific named character (the boy in *Sredni Vashtar*, the academic in *Whistle*) consistently across many shots. This must be solved (IP-Adapter or LoRA) before Channel 3 launches. Until then, the channel literally cannot ship.

**Period-specific rendering still has open failure modes.** Final Hours has accumulated rules but hasn't pressure-tested everything Channel 3 will touch — specifically Edwardian *children* (different proportions than adults, rendering challenges), Victorian/Edwardian *interior books and pictures* in detail (M.R. James's stories often turn on a specific painting or manuscript that must read well visually), and *animals* (*Sredni Vashtar*'s ferret is a load-bearing visual asset). The rulebook needs additional rules accumulated through Final Hours videos that touch these areas before Channel 3 can attempt them cleanly.

**Audio register difference.** Final Hours' Victor voice carries dread; Success Coach is auditioning Reed for warmer modern register. Channel 3 needs a third register — literary narrator, calm and measured, slightly old-fashioned diction, comfortable holding a long story without losing momentum. Inworld voice audition becomes part of the pre-launch checklist.

**The festival circuit may rearrange before launch.** Some of the festivals listed today may dissolve; new ones may launch. The strategy of "aim for three prestige festivals annually" survives regardless of which specific three exist. Track but don't commit.

**Risk of becoming a literary-Wikipedia channel.** The temptation when adapting "famous stories" is to lean on the source's reputation rather than make the films themselves outstanding. Channel 3 is not "I read Saki at you with pictures." It's cinematic adaptation that stands as its own work — assuming the viewer has never heard of the story. Honest standard to hold against.

## Trigger for launch

Five conditions, all of which must be true before Channel 3 goes live:

1. Hetzner is operational and humming. Renders happening unattended overnight.
2. Freelance stills reviewer is hired and trained. Failure-category taxonomy is mature.
3. Avatar problem (IP-Adapter or LoRA) is solved or has a clean workaround.
4. Final Hours has shipped 8-12 videos and has retention data on what register works.
5. Success Coach has shipped 3-5 videos and is no longer the daily-attention drain.

When all five are true, the next month is Channel 3 setup. The four months between now and then are research and rulebook accumulation, not action.

## Why this is the right Channel 3, not something else

Two other Channel 3 candidates have come up across recent thinking — the calming-music/sleep-content idea, and the long-form compilation idea. Neither holds up against this one. Sleep content is a wholly different audience attention model and wastes the pipeline's cost asymmetry on visual quality that doesn't matter. Compilations dilute Final Hours' brand without creating new audience value.

The literary-adaptation channel is the right Channel 3 because:

It uses the pipeline's actual strength (high-fidelity cinematic shot composition with character consistency) on content that *rewards* that quality (literary fiction where craft is the whole point).

It enters a genuinely empty algorithmic and festival space.

It inherits 80%+ of its rulebook from Final Hours, paying minimal accumulation tax.

It opens prestige credentialing pathways (festivals, IMDb-qualifying status) that Final Hours and Success Coach don't.

It positions Peter as the rarest profile in AI filmmaking — system operator with literary craft taste, building credentialled work that compounds over years rather than chasing weekly upload schedules.

That last point matters most. Final Hours is the moat; Success Coach is the audience-building wedge; Channel 3 is the artistic and prestige horizon. Together the three channels define the full shape of the operation.

Banked decision: this is Channel 3. Research starts now; launch follows the architecture readiness, not an arbitrary date.
