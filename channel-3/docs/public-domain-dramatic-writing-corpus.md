# The Public Domain Dramatic Writing Corpus
*A strategic asset, comprehensive — May 2026*

A reference document. The full case for treating public-domain dramatic writing — plays, scenarios, unproduced manuscripts, short fiction with strong dramatic structure — as the long-horizon strategic source for AI cinematic adaptation. Written for Peter, banked as the working brief for Channel 3's evolution from "Saki and James short fiction" into something more ambitious: an AI filmmaker building a body of work from the canon of dramatic literature.

This is not a launch document. Channel 3 still opens with *Sredni Vashtar* and the short-fiction strategy. But the dramatic corpus is the *direction the channel grows*, and the architectural decisions made now should anticipate that.

---

## The thesis, stated plainly

The world's libraries hold thousands of plays — many of them masterpieces, many of them by writers whose names appear on university syllabi but whose work has never been filmed, or filmed only once, decades ago, by a regional theatre company. The dramatic canon stretches from Sophocles to Strindberg to Susan Glaspell. Most of it is in the public domain. None of it has been adapted by AI cinema in any serious way.

This is not a small lane. It is the entire history of Western dramatic literature, sitting unworked, while every AI filmmaker on YouTube generates derivative original work because they haven't realised the canon is freely available.

The pipeline being built now is genuinely the right instrument to produce these adaptations. A 90-minute Ibsen play has the same structural shape as the Project → Act → Scene → Shot hierarchy. The work of *converting Ibsen to schema* is hours, not weeks. The work of *producing the adaptation* is the pipeline doing what it's already designed to do.

The strategic position is rare: the AI filmmaker who has spent the narrated-fiction phase (Saki, Chekhov's stories, M.R. James) building credentials, rulebook, audience, and festival presence — then pivots into dialogue-driven dramatic adaptation the day lip-sync stabilises — owns a defensible category that traditional production cannot enter at the same cost or speed. The transition is invisible to the audience (the channel just adds dialogue capability); the architectural lead is total.

## The honest scope — what's actually public domain

Copyright rules vary by jurisdiction. For pragmatic purposes, two regimes matter:

**United States.** Works published before 1930 are now public domain (as of January 2026, with 1929 having entered last year). The 95-year rule from publication date. Works between 1930 and 1963 entered public domain if their copyright was not renewed — and the renewal rate was low for older theatrical works. Stanford's Copyright Renewal Database is the operational tool for verifying any specific work.

**European Union and most Commonwealth (UK, Australia, Canada).** Life of the author plus 70 years. As of 2026, anything by an author who died on or before 1955 is public domain. This is more permissive than the US for many authors who lived past 1929.

**Translation copyright is separate from original-work copyright.** A play by Ibsen (died 1906) is unambiguously public domain. A 1965 English translation of the same play is *not*. Operational solution: use pre-1929 translations (William Archer for Ibsen, Constance Garnett for Chekhov, Edwin Björkman for Strindberg) which are themselves public domain. Alternative: commission new translations or generate them with AI from the public domain originals, both of which you'd own outright.

**Verification practice for any specific work.** Five-minute check: confirm author death date (EU rule), confirm first publication date (US rule), check Stanford renewal database if US-published 1930-1963, identify which translation you're using and verify its copyright separately. When stakes are high (festival submission, monetisation, distribution deal), £200-500 to a copyright lawyer for written verification is cheap insurance.

What follows is curated for works that are clearly in the public domain in major jurisdictions as of 2026 and that fit the pipeline architecture genuinely well. Specific copyright verification still needed for any work before adaptation, but the broad strokes are stable.

## The catalogue, era by era

### Ancient Greek and Roman drama

Universally public domain. Translations from the 19th and early 20th century also public domain (Gilbert Murray's Euripides translations, Lewis Campbell's Sophocles).

**Sophocles.** *Oedipus Rex*, *Antigone*, *Electra*, *Oedipus at Colonus*, *Ajax*. The bedrock of Western dramatic structure. *Antigone* in particular: single location, ethical conflict, devastating ending, three central characters. Festival-perfect.

**Euripides.** *Medea*, *The Bacchae*, *The Trojan Women*, *Hippolytus*, *Electra*. *The Bacchae* is one of the most disturbing plays ever written; *Medea* is psychological horror in pre-Hellenic dress. Both adapt to AI cinema brilliantly because the visual register (Bronze Age palaces, masks, ritual) actually benefits from the slightly otherworldly quality AI generation has.

**Aeschylus.** *The Oresteia* trilogy (*Agamemnon*, *The Libation Bearers*, *The Eumenides*), *Prometheus Bound*, *The Persians*. Older, more ritualised, but structurally dense.

**Aristophanes** (comedy). *Lysistrata*, *The Clouds*, *The Frogs*. Genuinely funny in good translation. Less likely festival material but interesting cluster opportunity.

**Plautus, Terence** (Roman comedy). Influence-everything works. Structurally simple. Decent secondary corpus.

Pipeline fit: medium. Ancient costumes and architecture require fresh rulebook accumulation; the visual era doesn't inherit from Final Hours. But the structural simplicity (small cast, unity of place and time, chorus as narrator-equivalent) maps cleanly onto the schema. Possibly the *richest* festival opportunity because traditional film rarely tackles Greek tragedy seriously and AI's slight otherworldliness suits it.

### Shakespeare and the Elizabethans

All of Shakespeare. Public domain everywhere, every translation, every variant text. Thirty-eight plays. A career's worth of source material alone.

The lesser-known peers are equally rich. **Christopher Marlowe** — *Doctor Faustus*, *Edward II*, *Tamburlaine the Great*, *The Jew of Malta*. Marlowe is darker than Shakespeare and arguably better suited to AI cinema's atmospheric capabilities — *Faustus* is genuinely terrifying when properly produced.

**Ben Jonson.** *Volpone*, *The Alchemist*, *Bartholomew Fair*. Sharp satirical comedy.

**John Webster.** *The Duchess of Malfi*, *The White Devil*. Jacobean tragedy at its blackest — *Duchess of Malfi* in particular is one of the most psychologically intense plays in English. Madhouse scenes, premeditated cruelty, intricate revenge. Genuinely cinematic.

**Thomas Middleton.** *The Changeling*, *Women Beware Women*. Same Jacobean register, equally dark.

**John Ford.** *'Tis Pity She's a Whore*. Incestuous tragedy. Banned for centuries. Cinematically extraordinary if handled with care.

Pipeline fit: high. The Elizabethan/Jacobean visual era requires its own rulebook accumulation but it's a single coherent period that pays back across dozens of plays. Once you've banked rules for Tudor/early-Stuart clothing, candlelight, panelled halls, you can produce a dozen works inside that one moat.

### Restoration and 18th-century English

**William Congreve.** *The Way of the World*, *Love for Love*. Restoration comedy at its most brilliant.

**William Wycherley.** *The Country Wife*. Sexually frank, sharp, formally inventive.

**Aphra Behn** (the first English woman to make a living as a writer). *The Rover*. Genuinely interesting feminist subject when handled well.

**Oliver Goldsmith.** *She Stoops to Conquer*. Warm comedy, pipeline-perfect for the lighter end of the channel's range.

**Richard Brinsley Sheridan.** *The School for Scandal*, *The Rivals*. Some of the most quotable dialogue in English drama.

Pipeline fit: high. Drawing-room interiors, period costume, contained casts.

### The 19th-century continental revolution

The real goldmine — the era when modern drama was invented. Almost everything here is public domain in 2026.

**Henrik Ibsen** (Norwegian, 1828-1906). The single most influential dramatist of the modern era. *A Doll's House*, *Ghosts*, *Hedda Gabler*, *The Master Builder*, *Rosmersholm*, *The Wild Duck*, *An Enemy of the People*, *Peer Gynt*, *When We Dead Awaken*. Twelve major plays, each one a candidate for adaptation. *Hedda Gabler* and *A Doll's House* are the obvious starting points — single-location, dialogue-driven, psychologically intense. *Ghosts* is genuinely shocking even today.

**August Strindberg** (Swedish, 1849-1912). *Miss Julie*, *The Father*, *A Dream Play*, *Dance of Death*, *Creditors*. *Miss Julie* is the perfect first dramatic adaptation: one night, one kitchen, three characters, devastating ending. Ninety minutes of dramatic compression. The cook, the master's daughter, the valet. Class, gender, desire, ruin. *A Dream Play* is more experimental, possibly suited to a later, more ambitious phase.

**Anton Chekhov** (Russian, 1860-1904). *The Seagull*, *Uncle Vanya*, *Three Sisters*, *The Cherry Orchard*. Plus dozens of one-acts (*The Bear*, *The Proposal*, *On the Harmfulness of Tobacco*) that are 15-25 minute pieces — almost custom-built for the channel's format. The Constance Garnett translations are public domain.

**Georg Büchner** (German, 1813-1837). Wrote three plays in his early twenties before dying of typhus. *Woyzeck*, *Danton's Death*, *Leonce and Lena*. *Woyzeck* in particular is so structurally radical that it reads like a 1970s art film a hundred and forty years early. Fragmentary, hallucinatory, devastating.

**Frank Wedekind** (German, 1864-1918). *Spring Awakening*, *Lulu* plays. Subject matter that was censored for decades. *Spring Awakening* dramatises adolescent sexuality with a frankness that still feels modern.

**Maxim Gorky** (Russian, 1868-1936). *The Lower Depths*. Set in a flophouse. Multiple characters, ensemble structure.

**Gerhart Hauptmann** (German, 1862-1946). *The Weavers*. Working-class uprising as drama.

**Arthur Schnitzler** (Austrian, 1862-1931). *La Ronde*, *Anatol*. *La Ronde* is structurally extraordinary — ten interlocking scenes of sexual encounters, each one a transfer between characters. Cinematic in its essence.

**Heinrich von Kleist** (German, 1777-1811). *Penthesilea*, *The Marquise of O*, *Prince Friedrich of Homburg*. Psychologically and politically intense.

Pipeline fit: extremely high. Edwardian or near-Edwardian period costume which Final Hours has already pressure-tested. Drawing rooms, parlours, kitchens — interior cinema. Small casts. Single-location plays. The schema fits these as if it was designed for them.

### English drawing-room comedy and drama (late 19th, early 20th)

**Oscar Wilde** (Irish, 1854-1900). *The Importance of Being Earnest*, *An Ideal Husband*, *Lady Windermere's Fan*, *A Woman of No Importance*, *Salomé*. Universal public domain. *Earnest* is one of the funniest plays in English; *Salomé* is decadent and visually extraordinary. *Ideal Husband* is structurally tight political-personal drama.

**George Bernard Shaw** (Irish, 1856-1950). Copyright complex — life-plus-70 reached in 2020 in EU/UK, so all Shaw is now PD in those jurisdictions. US: pre-1930 works PD; later works (*Saint Joan* 1923 is PD in US; *Heartbreak House* 1919 PD; *Pygmalion* 1913 PD; *Major Barbara* 1905 PD). Shaw wrote prose-heavy plays with brilliant ideas-talk. *Pygmalion* in particular is a natural choice.

**J.M. Synge** (Irish, 1871-1909). *Riders to the Sea*, *The Playboy of the Western World*, *The Shadow of the Glen*, *Deirdre of the Sorrows*. *Riders to the Sea* is a 30-minute one-act about an Aran Islands family losing their last son to the sea. Devastating. Almost unbearable. Festival material.

**W.B. Yeats** (Irish, 1865-1939). Early plays (pre-1930) PD in US. *Cathleen ni Houlihan*, *The Land of Heart's Desire*. Symbolic, poetic, ritualistic.

**Lady Augusta Gregory** (Irish, 1852-1932). *The Rising of the Moon*, *Spreading the News*, dozens of short plays. Part of the Abbey Theatre canon. Mostly short pieces, perfect for episodic series.

**Sean O'Casey** (Irish, 1880-1964). Early Dublin trilogy: *The Shadow of a Gunman* (1923), *Juno and the Paycock* (1924), *The Plough and the Stars* (1926). PD in US; copyright in EU until 2034.

### American early modernism

**Eugene O'Neill** (American, 1888-1953). Early works PD in US: *Beyond the Horizon* (1918), *The Emperor Jones* (1920), *Anna Christie* (1921), *The Hairy Ape* (1922), *Desire Under the Elms* (1924), *Strange Interlude* (1928). Later masterpieces (*Long Day's Journey Into Night*, *The Iceman Cometh*) still copyright. The early work is enough for a substantial body of adaptation.

**Susan Glaspell** (American, 1876-1948). *Trifles* (1916), *The Verge* (1921), *Inheritors* (1921), *Alison's House* (1930). *Trifles* is a 30-minute murder mystery told entirely through what women notice and men miss in a farmhouse kitchen. The first significant feminist play in American drama. Five characters, one location, devastating. Festival gold.

**Sophie Treadwell** (American, 1885-1970). *Machinal* (1928, PD in US). Expressionist drama based on a real murder case. Visually extraordinary.

### Continental modernism

**Federico García Lorca** (Spanish, 1898-1936, murdered by Franco's forces). Rural trilogy: *Blood Wedding* (1933), *Yerma* (1934), *The House of Bernarda Alba* (1936). Spanish copyright complex but most works PD in many jurisdictions including EU. *Bernarda Alba* is one of the great twentieth-century plays — five sisters trapped in mourning after their father's death, sexual repression, eventual suicide. Visually rich, dialogue-driven, devastating.

**Karel Čapek** (Czech, 1890-1938). *R.U.R.* (1920) — the play that coined the word "robot." Genuinely the first major science-fiction drama. *The Insect Play* (1921), *The Makropulos Affair* (1922). PD in US (pre-1929) and EU. *R.U.R.* in particular is now hauntingly current — a hundred-year-old play about humans being replaced by their own creations.

**Luigi Pirandello** (Italian, 1867-1936). *Six Characters in Search of an Author* (1921, PD in US), *Henry IV* (1922). Pirandello broke the fourth wall before Brecht. Metatheatrical, formally radical.

**Bertolt Brecht** (German, 1898-1956). EU copyright until 2027 (life+70). Early works PD in US: *Baal* (1923), *Drums in the Night* (1922), *In the Jungle of Cities* (1923). The famous later works (*Mother Courage*, *Galileo*, *Caucasian Chalk Circle*) still copyright until 2027 in EU.

### Russian dramatic literature

**Nikolai Gogol** (1809-1852). *The Government Inspector*, *Marriage*. Comedy with bite.

**Alexander Ostrovsky** (1823-1886). Forty-seven plays. *The Storm*, *The Forest*, *Easy Money*. Russian merchant-class drama.

**Alexander Pushkin** (1799-1837). *Boris Godunov*, the "little tragedies" (*Mozart and Salieri*, *The Stone Guest*, *The Miserly Knight*).

**Leo Tolstoy** (1828-1910). *The Power of Darkness*, *The Living Corpse*, *The Fruits of Enlightenment*. Tolstoy's plays are underread and surprisingly cinematic.

### Honourable mentions

**Maurice Maeterlinck** (Belgian, 1862-1949). *Pelléas et Mélisande*, *The Blue Bird*, *The Intruder*. Symbolist drama, dreamlike, ambient.

**August Wilson** (American, 1945-2005). Still under copyright. Mentioned because *eventually* his Pittsburgh cycle becomes PD — but not for another forty years. Worth noting that the corpus continues to expand annually.

**Beaumont and Fletcher**, **Thomas Dekker**, **Thomas Heywood** — secondary Elizabethan/Jacobean.

**Carlo Goldoni** (Italian, 1707-1793). *The Servant of Two Masters*. Commedia dell'arte structure formalised.

**Pedro Calderón de la Barca** (Spanish, 1600-1681). *Life is a Dream*. Spanish Golden Age.

## The pipeline-fit matrix

Not all of these adapt equally cleanly to the current pipeline state. Honest classification:

**Tier 1 — ready for narrated adaptation now (Phase 2 architecture).** Works where the narrative voice can carry the action and dialogue can be selectively used as voiceover. Single location, small cast, controlled visual register, period largely in the Final Hours moat.

Saki short stories, M.R. James short stories, Chekhov's one-acts and short stories, Glaspell's *Trifles* (narrator-light but renderable), Synge's *Riders to the Sea* (very short, atmospheric), Wilde's *Salomé* (one location, voice-of-narrator-friendly), early Yeats. Lorca's *Bernarda Alba* with selective narration. Tolstoy's shorter plays.

**Tier 2 — wait for Phase 3-5 audio architecture.** Works where ambience and music cues per scene genuinely improve the experience even with narrator-led delivery.

Ibsen's *Ghosts* and *A Doll's House*, Strindberg's *Miss Julie* (the kitchen has its own sonic identity), Chekhov's full-length plays (*The Cherry Orchard* with the orchard's sounds), O'Neill's *Anna Christie* (the harbour).

**Tier 3 — wait for Phase 5-7 dialogue support and stabilised lip-sync.** Works where dialogue is the entire experience and narration is impossible.

Wilde's *Earnest* and *Ideal Husband*, all of Shaw, all of Restoration comedy, most Shakespeare, Webster's *Duchess of Malfi*, Pirandello's metatheatrical works, Beckett's early work (still copyright but illustrative). When the architecture supports real character dialogue with reliable lip-sync, this is where the pipeline goes.

**Tier 4 — possibly never via current pipeline.** Works whose specific demands exceed what AI cinema reasonably handles in the next decade: very large casts, complex physical staging, dance-driven sequences, deeply experimental form. Brecht's epic theatre, Aristophanes' choral comedies, Greek choral tragedies in their full ceremonial form. Might be approachable via different pipeline configurations later but not the primary target.

The honest truth: Tier 1 alone is probably ten years of channel material. The pipeline doesn't need Tiers 2-4 to be working productively — they just expand what becomes possible over time.

## The translation copyright trap, navigated

For non-English works the translation matters legally and artistically. The operational solutions:

**Use pre-1929 public domain translations.** Most great 19th-century continental drama has at least one PD translation already.

For Ibsen: William Archer's translations (1880s-1910s) — beautiful and faithful, PD.

For Chekhov: Constance Garnett's translations (early 1900s) — slightly Victorian-flavoured but PD; Marian Fell's (1916) for the major plays.

For Strindberg: Edwin Björkman's translations (1910s) — PD. Edith and Warner Oland too.

For Lorca: Several PD translations of the rural trilogy from the 1940s-50s exist; verify each specifically.

For Pirandello: Edward Storer's translations (1922) of *Six Characters* — PD.

For Maeterlinck: Several PD translations from the 1890s-1910s.

**Commission a new translation.** £1,000-3,000 per play from a literary translator gets you a fully-owned translation. For festival-prestige work this is worth it.

**Generate a translation with AI.** Claude or GPT translating from a public-domain original-language text into modern English. Legally clean (the original is PD; the AI-generated text has no human copyright); artistically variable. Probably the operational default for first-pass scripts, with human editorial polish before production. Quality on classical drama can be surprisingly good; the AI has read every translation that exists.

**Important nuance:** for festival submissions, name the translation source explicitly in the credits. "Adapted from the original Norwegian by [name] / Translation by William Archer (1908, public domain)" is professional practice and prevents any IP question from interfering with selection.

## The lost manuscript angle — genuinely practical

The romantic image: a forgotten typewritten masterpiece sitting in a dusty drawer. The practical reality is richer, less romantic, and more accessible.

**Library of Congress, Copyright Office Deposit Collection.** Since 1870, anything registered for US copyright was required to deposit copies with the Library of Congress. The collection holds millions of works including unpublished plays, scenarios, screenplays, and theatrical pieces. Many are now PD by age (pre-1930) or by failed renewal. Discoverable through the LoC catalogue and the Performing Arts Reading Room. The work being held in the LoC is not classified or hidden; it's catalogued and readable on request.

**University special collections.** Multiple repositories worth knowing:

NYU's Fales Library and Special Collections (Downtown Collection — significant unproduced theatrical work from the 20th century).

University of Wisconsin-Madison's Wisconsin Center for Film and Theater Research (the Warner Bros archive, multiple theatrical collections).

USC Cinematic Arts Library (Hollywood-adjacent unproduced material).

UCLA Performing Arts Special Collections.

Harvard Theatre Collection at the Houghton Library.

The Folger Shakespeare Library (early modern English drama and ancillary work).

The British Library's Manuscript Collections (UK theatrical archives).

**Project Gutenberg's drama section.** Free, digital, immediately accessible. Hundreds of plays already digitised and machine-readable. Search by author, era, language. This is the operational starting point for any adaptation — most major PD works are here.

**HathiTrust Digital Library.** Multi-institution digitisation. Holds books and plays university libraries have digitised but not made publicly visible. Some PD material findable here that isn't on Gutenberg.

**Internet Archive theatrical collections.** Scanned playbills, performance records, early theatrical journals. Genuine archaeological work possible here.

**Variety and other trade press archives** (pre-1929 issues are PD). Reviews of plays that opened, ran briefly, then disappeared. The "what happened to that play" trail.

**The discovery practice as artistic identity.** "The AI filmmaker who unearthed three forgotten plays from the 1920s and brought them to first production" — this is a real and rare identity. The work is genuinely valuable: not just adapting known masterpieces but rescuing work that history forgot. Festival juries and critics love this story. It frames you as cultural restorer, not just AI operator.

A practical version: spend one full day every month in archival research mode. Read the catalogue of LoC's theatrical holdings for a specific decade. Identify three works that look interesting. Read them. If one is genuinely worth producing, it becomes the next year's prestige project. Over five years, this builds a portfolio that no traditional production company can match because they're not looking in these archives.

## The first-mover thesis — the eighteen-month window

The strategically decisive observation. Lip-sync technology will stabilise in the next 12-18 months. The transition will not be gradual — it will be punctuated. Today's outputs are clearly artificial; tomorrow's will be unreliable; the day after, suddenly serviceable; six months later, indistinguishable from low-budget live-action character work.

When that transition happens, the AI filmmakers who launch dialogue-driven dramatic adaptations *at the same time as the technology stabilises* will be six to twelve months behind those who already had the architecture ready, the cast (character voices) selected, the rulebook accumulated, the festival presence established from narrated work, and a pipeline of pre-adapted scripts queued for production.

The narrated phase is not preparation for the dialogue phase. It *is* the strategic positioning for the dialogue phase. Every Saki adaptation builds rulebook entries that survive into the dialogue era. Every Chekhov short story adaptation establishes the channel's voice register and audience identity. Every festival submission accumulates credentials that justify being taken seriously when the dialogue work begins.

The full strategic shape:

**Year one (now-2027).** Narrated short fiction. Saki, James, Chekhov stories, Glaspell's *Trifles*, Synge's *Riders to the Sea*. Festival submissions begin. Audience builds. Rulebook matures across multiple period registers. Channel 3 has 8-12 published videos and is recognised as serious adaptation work.

**Year two (2027-2028).** Lip-sync technology matures. Channel architecture absorbs multi-voice dialogue (Phase 5). First dialogue-driven adaptations: Wilde's *Salomé* as ambitious launch piece; Glaspell's *Trifles* in full-dialogue form; Lorca's *Bernarda Alba*; possibly Strindberg's *Miss Julie*. Festival circuit takes notice — the work has visibly stepped up while still being made by the same operator. The transition becomes the story.

**Year three (2028-2029).** Full dialogue-driven mid-length adaptations: Ibsen, Chekhov full-length plays, O'Neill, Shakespeare's shorter works. Channel 3 produces possibly 4-8 longer-form videos per year alongside continued shorter work. Festival presence well-established. Distribution conversations begin.

**Year four (2029-2030).** Feature-length capability emerges. First 75-90 minute AI-produced dramatic adaptations from the pipeline. The "amateur movie production" frontier from the production-system-as-moat document becomes operational. Cannes-equivalent prestige AI festival likely exists by this point.

The architectural decisions made in 2026 — the hierarchical schema, the encapsulated tool interfaces, the per-channel configuration pattern, the discipline of banking tool-agnostic principles — are what allow this trajectory to be a one-person operation in 2030. Traditional production cannot match the cost. New AI operators starting from scratch in 2028 cannot match the rulebook and credential accumulation. The window is real and it's defensible.

Worth sitting with the names against the broader trajectory before committing.

## Festival positioning specifically for dramatic adaptations

The festival circuit treats literary adaptation differently from documentary. Three observations.

**Source-material prestige works in your favour.** Festival juries recognise canonical works. Submitting "AI cinematic adaptation of Strindberg's *Miss Julie*" carries cultural weight that "AI short film about a fictional Edwardian couple" does not. The source signals seriousness; the AI execution becomes the interesting variable, not the only thing being judged.

**Comparative criticism becomes a category.** When the source exists in multiple prior film/stage versions, your AI adaptation enters a critical conversation rather than appearing from nowhere. Critics know what *Miss Julie* should feel like; they can review yours against Mike Figgis's 1999 film, Liv Ullmann's 2014 version, multiple stage productions. The work has a critical context.

**Festival politics — the prestige-AI hybrid niche.** Some festival programmers want to be seen taking AI seriously but worry about associating with "tech bro demo reels." Adapted classical drama solves this — they can programme your work as legitimate cultural production that happens to use AI tools. You become the showcase example of "this is what AI cinema can be when it engages with cultural heritage." Genuine programmer demand exists for this profile.

**Specific festival fits.**

Reply AIFF (Venice premiere, June deadline): take the most prestigious source you've adapted — *Miss Julie*, *Bernarda Alba*, *Hedda Gabler* — and submit annually as your strongest work.

Runway AIF: more experimental tolerance. Adapt the formally radical source material — *Six Characters in Search of an Author*, *A Dream Play*, Büchner's *Woyzeck*. Festival is more receptive to genuine experimentation.

LifeArt: the IMDb-qualifying festival. Submit your most cinematically conventional dramatic adaptation. Goal is the IMDb credit; choose the work most likely to be appreciated by a broad festival audience.

Venice Biennale itself (not just Reply's premiere within it): genuinely worth aiming for after several years of accumulated work. The first AI filmmaker to be selected officially by Venice for an adapted-classic feature work would be a historic positioning.

Cannes when its AI program emerges (predicted 2027-2028): the prestige peak. Position to be in early conversations when their AI track launches.

## Honest risks and constraints

**Translation copyright remains the persistent friction.** Per-work verification needed. Cheap insurance but unavoidable. Build a personal database of verified PD translations per author.

**Quality bar is higher than YouTube tolerance.** Festival submissions and prestige adaptation cannot tolerate the "honest first-version imperfection" that Final Hours accepts. Channel 3's review and reshoot tolerance must be substantially tighter than the other channels. This is a discipline cost that scales with ambition.

**Source-material prestige creates source-material expectations.** When you adapt *Hedda Gabler*, your audience and critics know what *Hedda Gabler* is supposed to feel like. Mediocre adaptation of canonical work is worse than original mediocre work — you carry the source's weight without honouring it. Each project's preparation needs to be thorough: reading the play multiple times, watching prior adaptations, understanding what the work demands, then making real artistic decisions about how to render it. This is creative-craft work, not just pipeline operation.

**The "AI Wikipedia of plays" trap.** Mechanically adapting famous plays without genuine artistic vision produces work that's competent but forgettable. Festival juries see through this immediately. Channel 3's adaptations must be *interpretations*, not just productions — the framing of *Bernarda Alba* as a meditation on grief versus a feminist parable versus a class study is a real artistic decision. Defaulting to "render the play straight" is a path to invisibility. The pipeline produces; the operator interprets.

**Long horizon discipline against short-term temptation.** The narrated phase is artistically less prestigious than the eventual dialogue phase. Resisting the temptation to attempt dialogue-driven work before the technology supports it requires discipline. Premature attempts will be obviously broken and will damage the channel's credibility specifically because the source-material expectations are so high. Wait for the technology.

**Discovery work is its own time commitment.** Reading dramatic literature, evaluating works, verifying copyright, finding PD translations — this is real research time. Probably 6-10 hours per genuine new adaptation candidate. Worth budgeting honestly. The romance of "discover the forgotten masterpiece" is real but it's not free.

## What to bank now, operationally

Six concrete commitments for the next four to six months while Final Hours and Success Coach stabilise:

**One — read three plays per month during the build-up period.** Start with *Trifles*, *Riders to the Sea*, *Miss Julie*. Then *A Doll's House*, *Hedda Gabler*, *Bernarda Alba*. Then *Cherry Orchard*, *The Seagull*, *Three Sisters*. By Channel 3 launch you have nine plays personally read and the strongest two or three ready to script.

**Two — build a copyright-status spreadsheet.** Per work: author death date, first publication date, US PD status, EU PD status, recommended PD translation source, alternative translation options. Reference document that compounds in value as it grows.

**Three — bank dramatic-writing-specific principles to the universal rulebook as they emerge.** Reading prior film adaptations of these plays will reveal recurring failure modes that any future production needs to avoid. Heavy-handed symbolism, music drowning dialogue, costumes that feel like museum pieces rather than lived-in clothing. These become rules that apply across Channel 3 and beyond.

**Four — develop a "canon template" library.** Edwardian gentleman canon, Edwardian lady canon, Norwegian fjord landscape canon, Russian country estate canon, Spanish white-walled rural canon, Greek amphitheatre canon. As Final Hours and early Channel 3 work accumulates, these canon templates become reusable assets. Adapting a third Ibsen play uses the same Norwegian gentleman canon developed for the first.

**Five — make a "festival strategy calendar".** Submission deadlines for Reply AIFF, Runway AIF, LifeArt, AI ZONE, MetaMorph, others as they emerge. Which submission slot each year goes to which intended adaptation. Strategic three festivals per year rather than scattershot submissions.

**Six — write down the artistic vision.** Not "I adapt plays with AI" but specifically: *What is the relationship between this filmmaker and the dramatic canon? What do my adaptations argue? What's the directorial signature that makes a viewer recognise it as mine after one minute? What's my position on faithfulness versus interpretation?* A page or two of honest reflection. Comes back to it before each adaptation. The answers shape every production decision.

That artistic vision document is genuinely the highest-leverage thing to write in the next year. The pipeline produces; the system scales; the architecture compounds. What makes the work *yours* is the answer to those questions. Festival juries will see it before they see anything technical. Reviewers will write about it before they write about the production methodology. Audience will recognise it before they recognise the pipeline. It's the only thing that doesn't come from architecture or discipline — it comes from you.
