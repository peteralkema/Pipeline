# THE FORGOTTEN SEA — Series Bible

*Working series title: **The Forgotten Sea** (alt: *Deadlier Than the Titanic*, *The Sea Doesn't Read Uniforms*)*

This is the document the four scripts are written against. Lock it before episode one. Every script decision — POV, hook, look, packaging — answers to this page. Update it only if a published episode's data tells you to.

## 1. The premise, in one paragraph

In the final winter of the Second World War, the deadliest sinkings in maritime history happened in the same cold sea, within weeks of each other, and almost no one remembers them. Each one drowned more people than the Titanic. This series follows one human being onto each doomed ship — a refugee, a wounded soldier, a nurse, a prisoner — and stays with them to the waterline. The catastrophe is the setting; the person is the subject. The question underneath all four: **whose dead does a culture choose to remember, and whose does it let the water keep?**

## 2. The thesis (the spine that makes this a series, not four sinkings)

Not "all sides suffered." That flattening is the failure mode (see §7). The thesis is sharper and it is a *moral* one: **the sea does not read uniforms.** Three episodes recover the humanity of the enemy's drowned — German civilians, refugees, children, the wounded — people a victors'-history reflex files under "them." The fourth, Cap Arcona, breaks the pattern: the same machine of war incinerated its own victims, concentration-camp prisoners, killed by the "right" side. The series does not equate them. It complicates the reflex. By episode four the viewer is holding a question they did not have at episode one — *which drowned strangers earned my grief, and who decided that?* That is the moralised closer, built into the architecture rather than tacked onto each ending.

## 3. The binding hook (carries the whole series, repeats across packaging)

> **Each one was deadlier than the Titanic. You have heard of none of them.**

Every title, thumbnail, and cold open lives in the gravitational field of that line. It is the curiosity gap the NexLev data proved wins (the forgotten-tragedy frame took a 2,000-sub channel to 106K at a 39× outlier on Gustloff). It also positions the series in the Titanic algorithmic neighbourhood — the highest-traffic water on the platform — without being *about* the Titanic. We orbit it; we never make it.

## 4. Register (non-negotiable, every episode)

Dread-and-dignity. Present tense, third-person omniscient witness. Mournful, slow, considered. Never thriller, never mystery-bait, never "what they found will SHOCK you." The recreation is acknowledged once, early, woven in (recreated from survivor accounts, naval records, the inquiry) — not as a disclaimer. The human is named in the first fifteen seconds and never abandoned for a history lecture. (The competitor failure mode we are beating — see the Cap Arcona reference video — was exactly that abandonment: it opened on the ship and drifted into a general WWII narrative. We do not.)

## 5. The four episodes

Working numbers below are the planning figures. **Fact-lock each one against primary sources during scripting** (death tolls vary by source; this is the human-phase fact-lock, per the Constitution — lock before the machine runs).

| # | Ship | Date | Toll (working) | Sunk by | Human POV (do not repeat across episodes) | Water |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wilhelm Gustloff** | 30 Jan 1945 | ~9,400 | Soviet sub S-13 (Marinesko) | a young mother, children in the crowd, in the ballroom | proven demand (anchor) |
| 2 | **General von Steuben** | 9–10 Feb 1945 | ~4,500 | **S-13 again — same boat, same captain, 11 days later** | a wounded soldier on a stretcher who cannot reach a boat | blue water (2 videos total) |
| 3 | **MV Goya** | 16 Apr 1945 | ~6,700 | Soviet sub L-3 | a refugee who **survived Gustloff in January** and boards Goya in April | blue water (≈0 videos) |
| 4 | **Cap Arcona** | 3 May 1945 | ~4,500+ | RAF Typhoons (friendly fire) | a concentration-camp prisoner, freed-then-doomed | blue water, proven ceiling (113K) |

**Per-episode distinct opening** — so four drownings don't blur into one. None of these may open on "the ship leaves port":
- **Gustloff** — opens on the gangway, the boarding crush, the clock already running (the proven hook shape).
- **Steuben** — opens *underwater*, in S-13, on the hunter. We meet the submarine that already killed once. Dramatic irony the viewer holds and the wounded men aboard do not.
- **Goya** — opens on a face we may half-recognise: someone who already cheated this exact sea in January. The cruelty of a second chance withdrawn.
- **Cap Arcona** — opens on liberation that isn't. Prisoners told the war is over, marched onto a ship, and the planes overhead are friendly.

## 6. The cross-episode architecture (what one-at-a-time authoring must protect)

- **The S-13 bridge (ep 1 → ep 2).** Gustloff ends with the submarine slipping away into the dark. Steuben opens with the same submarine, the same captain (Alexander Marinesko), surfacing eleven days later. This is the single strongest retention device in the set — it turns two standalone sinkings into a two-part hunt. Plant it at the close of ep 1; harvest it at the open of ep 2.
- **The survivor thread (ep 1 → ep 3).** Goya's POV is someone who lived through Gustloff. The viewer who watched ep 1 gets a gut-punch of recognition; the viewer who didn't gets a reason to go back and watch it.
- **The tonal pivot (ep 4).** Cap Arcona must *feel* different — fire and aircraft instead of torpedoes and ice, the "right" side as the agent of death. It is the episode that reframes the first three. It is last for that reason.
- **The shared closer motif.** Every episode ends on the same structural move and a recurring image — the cold water closing over, and a single line naming who the water kept. Across four episodes this becomes the series' signature. (Decide the exact recurring line during ep 1 scripting; reuse its cadence, not its words.)

## 7. The moral-framing guardrail (the one real landmine — read before writing a word)

Three episodes ask the viewer to grieve Germans; the fourth grieves people the Germans murdered. Handled carelessly this reads as "sympathy for Nazis" or "everyone suffered equally." Defense, held consciously in every script:

- The subjects are **always** children, refugees, the wounded, the imprisoned — **never the regime.** No uniform is the protagonist. No episode invites sympathy for the cause; every episode invites grief for a specific drowned human.
- Cap Arcona is not a counterweight that balances the ledger; it is the episode that **exposes the reflex** the first three relied on. The point is not symmetry of suffering — it is the arbitrariness of remembrance.
- Never invent a sympathetic line for a perpetrator. Stay on the drowned.
- If a beat starts to feel like it's litigating the war's morality, cut it. The register is grief, not argument.

Get this right and it's the most powerful thing the channel has made. Get it wrong and the comment section sinks the series in 48 hours.

## 8. Craft spine — every episode must do all of this (from ante-machinam IV / V.1)

First 60 seconds (the stress-test gate, non-negotiable):
- **Named human in the first 15 seconds.** Not the ship. A person with an age and a detail.
- **Date anchor in the first 10 seconds**, exact.
- **A concrete number in the first 30 seconds**, with the Titanic comparator nearby.
- **Dramatic-irony foreshadow before 0:60** — the "she did not know that in seven hours…" hammer. This is the proven move from the 39× Gustloff script; use its *shape*, never its words.
- **Cut on a cliffhanger at ~0:60**, mid-thought.

Through the body:
- **Stay on the one human.** No general WWII history lecture. (This is where the 113K Cap Arcona video failed; at 10–12 minutes you cannot afford it.)
- **Sensation, not description** — the cold, the list of the deck underfoot, the sound — the senses the image can't supply.
- **Clock-anchor the dread**, tightening intervals as the water rises.
- **Narrator-to-viewer irony** once per act break.
- **Plant early, harvest late** — one specific detail seeded in the calm, returned to at the end with weight.

The close:
- **End on the image, then the moralised reflection** turned at the present-day viewer. Per §6, the recurring closer motif.

## 9. Packaging system (where our human angle actually wins)

The NexLev finding: **not one** competitor put a face or a name in the title or thumbnail. They led with tonnage, mystery, and numbers. That is the open ground, and it's the Mary Celeste lesson — *the boy who waved goodbye and they never came back.* Title carries the **why-click** (the human); thumbnail carries the **what** (the doomed ship/person); they complement, never echo.

**Title grammar:**
> `[Human stake] — [Ship], [Year] | The Disaster Deadlier Than the Titanic`

Examples (draft):
- *She Was 24 and Had Seven Hours Left — The Wilhelm Gustloff, 1945*
- *The Submarine Came Back — General von Steuben, 1945*
- *He Survived It Once. The Sea Wasn't Finished — MV Goya, 1945*
- *Freed, Then Bombed by His Own Side — Cap Arcona, 1945*

(Metadata uses numerals — "1945", "9,000" — per the Constitution; spoken narration spells them out.)

**Thumbnail family (consistent across all four):**
- A single small human figure against an overwhelming dark sea or hull — the person dwarfed by the ship's fate.
- Cold blue-black palette; one warm element (a coat, a lantern, a child's face) as the eye-magnet.
- A short human-stake overlay, not a number-dump.
- Same composition logic and type treatment across four, so the suggested feed reads them as a set and chains the viewer ep→ep.
- Recruits both audiences (recognition + discovery): the discovery viewer can't be disappointed because the era is foreign to them.

## 10. Production spec

- **Channel:** `final_hours` → `final-hours/`. Mode A only. Victor (Inworld).
- **Runtime / length:** 10–12 min each → ~1,950–2,340 spoken words at Victor's measured ~195 wpm → **target ~2,200 words**. ~90–120 beats at the 15–35-word grain (hard ceiling ~55; split longer or the clip stretches to dead video).
- **Look (shared):** one cold-Baltic-night look across episodes 1–3 — black water, ice, military-grey hull, ember only where fire appears. **Cap Arcona gets an ember/fire variant** of the same look. Pin via a shared `look.json` / `style_suffix` copied across all four project folders so the series is visually one set **without** batching them into one job.
- **Jobs:** **four separate, staggered jobs — not one batch.** Shared look, separate renders. Publish ep 1, watch the 48-hour signal, write ep 2 informed by it, render, publish ~weekly so each gets a clean cold-start window and the algorithm chains viewers across the cluster. Reserve true batching for eps 3–4 only if the formula is proven and there's nothing left to learn.
- **VISUAL lines:** faceless by default (frame from behind, in profile, silhouetted, soft-focus) — both the dignity register and Flux's cleanest-first-pass path. Resolve a face only where the audience must bond with one documented person. Object-substitution for crowds (Flux fails on 3+ figures): a packed deck becomes one abandoned suitcase, a single child's shoe, a coat on the rail. Empty rooms carry meaning. Fire/water as environment, never close-up on a person dying. Period-accurate; no legible on-screen text (image models can't render it).
