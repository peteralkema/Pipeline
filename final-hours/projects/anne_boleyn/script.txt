She has been awake since two in the morning. The candle is nearly gone. It is the nineteenth of May, fifteen thirty-six. The Tower is quiet. Three years ago she was crowned queen, in a gown of white and gold. Today she will die by the sword. She asked for the sword herself. The king wanted a son. She gave him a daughter, then nothing at all. The daughter's name was Elizabeth. No one yet knew what she would become. By spring the king had found someone new. Anne had become a problem. The charges came fast. Treason. Adultery. Plots almost no one believed. Five men were accused with her. All five were already dead. They died two days ago. She watched from her window. She knew. Now she waits for her own turn. The waiting is the worst of it. She was told it would happen at nine. She prepared herself for nine. Nine o'clock came. Then it passed. No one came for her. The execution was delayed. No reason given. The waiting began again. She had said her confession. She had already made her peace. Now she had to hold that peace, hour after hour, and wait. They had brought a swordsman from France, famous for his skill. A sword, not an axe. It was, in its way, a mercy. The axe was clumsy. It often took more than one blow. The sword was clean. She had asked for it. The king allowed it. A small kindness, at the end. The swordsman waited too. Told to wait, like everyone else. Outside, the scaffold was being built. She could hear the hammering. A low platform, a few feet high. Spread with straw for the blood. It was no public spectacle. Only a few hundred would watch. A private death, for the most talked-about woman in England. Noon approached. Still she waited. The second delay stretched on. Those who saw her said she was calm. Calmer than anyone expected. She even joked. The executioner was good, she said, and her neck was small. She laughed as she said it. No one knew how to answer. Her ladies wept. She comforted them. The queen comforting her servants. She asked them to pray for her. And to be kind, when she was gone. The afternoon faded. The day given to her to die was nearly over. Then word came at last. It would be the next morning, at nine. She had survived the longest day of her life. One night remained. She barely slept. She prayed in the dark with her almoner. By dawn she was dressed. A grey gown trimmed with fur. Crimson beneath. She dressed with care. She would be looked at, one last time. A white cap, to hold her hair from her neck. Chosen with purpose. She would die looking like a queen, because she had been one. At nine they came. This time, no one turned them away. She walked out into the morning, surrounded by those who would witness it. The green was bright. The grass wet with dew. The crowd silent. She walked toward the scaffold slowly, her ladies close behind. She climbed the few steps. The straw was pale beneath her feet. She was allowed to speak. The condemned could address the crowd. She did not protest her innocence. She did not accuse the king. She spoke gently of him. She called him a good and gentle prince. No one believed it. She knew it too. She said it anyway. Why? Perhaps for the daughter she left behind. Words could still wound a king. She asked the crowd to pray for her. Then her words were done. Her ladies came to help her. Their hands shook. Hers did not. They took her necklace and headdress, so nothing would be in the way. She tucked her hair beneath a plain cap. Calm, practical, to the last. She gave her ladies a last look. She told them not to grieve. Then she knelt upright in the straw. There was no block for the sword. A blindfold was tied gently over her eyes. The world went dark. She prayed aloud. God, have pity on my soul. Again and again. The swordsman did not want her to flinch. So he did something quiet. He called out softly, as if to a helper. Bring me the sword. She turned her head toward the sound, away from the blade. The sword was already in his hands. She never knew the moment came. It was over in a single stroke. Clean, as she had hoped. The crowd made no sound. Many knelt. Some were weeping. No coffin was ready for her. No one had thought to prepare one. Her ladies wrapped her themselves and carried her away. They found an old arrow chest. It was the only box that fit. The most famous woman in England, buried in a box made for arrows. She was laid beneath the stones of the chapel, inside the Tower walls. No monument. No name. For centuries, almost nothing marked her. The king was dressed for hunting. He married eleven days later. Her name was struck from the records. Her badges torn from the walls. As if England had been ordered to forget she ever lived. But she had left something behind. A red-haired daughter, not yet three. That daughter was declared illegitimate. Pushed aside. Almost forgotten too. And then, against everything, that forgotten girl became a queen. Elizabeth the First. One of the greatest rulers England has known. She ruled forty-four years. She never married. She answered to no man. She rarely spoke of her mother. But she wore a ring all her life. Inside the ring was a hidden portrait. A woman with dark eyes. It was Anne. The daughter had carried her, hidden, all along. The king tried to erase her. His own daughter made him fail. Anne Boleyn waited through the longest hours a person can wait. And she met her end the way she lived. On her own terms. They gave her one day to die. She made it last five hundred years.
