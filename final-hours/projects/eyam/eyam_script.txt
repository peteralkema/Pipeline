She is on the hillside above Riley Farm, alone, with a spade in her hands and the ground hard under the August sun. It is the third of August, sixteen sixty-six. Her name is Elizabeth Hancock. Below her, the village of Eyam is dying, house by house, and over the next eight days she will dig seven graves on this slope with her own hands. Her husband. Then her children, one after another. No one will come up the hill to help her. They will watch from the ridge, and they will not come down.

Everything you are about to see has been drawn from the Eyam parish registers, the letters the rector wrote that survive, and the seven graves on the Riley hillside that still stand today, ringed by a low stone wall.

To understand her eight days, you have to go back almost a year, to the autumn before.

It began with cloth. At the end of the summer of sixteen sixty-five, a bundle of cloth came up from London to the house of the village tailor. London that year was deep in plague, though Eyam, sixty miles north in the Derbyshire hills, thought itself far away from it. The cloth was damp from the journey. A man named George Viccars spread it out before the fire to dry. He did not know that the damp folds carried fleas, and that the fleas carried the thing that was emptying London street by street.

Within days Viccars was dead, raging with fever. By the end of September, five more in the houses nearby. By the end of October, twenty-three. And then the cold came, and the dying slowed, and through the winter the village let itself believe the worst had passed. In the whole month of May, only two died. The hills were greening. People walked out again. It felt, to the people of Eyam, like deliverance.

It was not deliverance. It was the pause before the second wave.

In June of sixteen sixty-six, the plague came back, and this time it did not slow. Twenty-one died that month. The new rector, William Mompesson, watched the burials multiply and understood what was coming. He went to the people with a plan, and it was a terrible one.

The villages all around them — Stoney Middleton, Grindleford, the farms toward Sheffield — had so far been spared. If the people of Eyam scattered, ran for those villages the way the frightened always run, they would carry the plague north into a country that had so far escaped it. Mompesson asked them not to run. He asked them to draw a line around their own village and stay inside it. To seal themselves in with the thing that was killing them, so that it would die when they died, and go no further.

They agreed. We do not entirely know why. Some out of faith, some out of fear, some perhaps because there was nowhere left to run to. A ring of boundary stones was set around the parish, and the village closed itself like a fist. Food and supplies were left at the stones by people from the outside who never came closer. Coins were left in payment in holes filled with vinegar, or dropped into the running water of a well on the hill, because they believed the water and the vinegar would clean the sickness from the metal. No one crossed the line outward. That was the agreement. That was the sentence they passed on themselves.

Elizabeth Hancock lived with her family at a farm called Riley, on the edge of the parish, a little apart from the village itself. A farmer's wife. Seven children. For a while, the distance from the village seemed to protect them. The dying was down in the houses by the church, not up here on the hill.

Then it came up the hill.

On the third of August, her husband John died. The ground at Riley is thin over rock, and there was no one to dig but her, so she dug. She wrapped him, and she dragged him up the slope, and she buried him, and she went back down to the house where her children were.

The next day, a child. Then the next, another. Across eight days in that hot August, Elizabeth Hancock buried her husband and six of her seven children on the Riley hillside, one grave at a time, alone.

There is a detail that has come down through three centuries, and it is almost unbearable. From the ridge above, people of the village watched her. They saw a woman dragging the bodies of her own family up a hill in the heat, digging, kneeling, covering them over, and going back down for the next one. And they did not come to help her. They dared not. To come close to the Hancock farm was to invite the same death into their own houses, and they had already buried so many. So they stood on the ridge, and they watched, and they let her do it alone. That was what the plague made of them. Not cruel. Only afraid past the point where help was possible.

She did not stop to ring a bell for them. No one in Eyam had time to toll bells anymore. She dug, and she carried, and she covered, and the August sun did not let up, and by the time it was over there were seven fresh graves on the slope above her house and she was the only one of her family left standing on the earth.

She lived.

That is the thing about Eyam that the story almost forgets to tell you. The woman who buried everyone she loved did not die. Elizabeth Hancock survived the plague that took her husband and six children in eight days, and when it was finally over she left the hillside and the graves and went to Sheffield with her one remaining son, and lived.

She was not the only one to lose everything. That same August, down in the village, the rector's wife was dying too. Catherine Mompesson had sent her children away to safety months before, but she had refused to leave her husband, and through all the months of dying she had walked among the sick, nursing them. One evening, walking home with her husband, she said she could smell something sweet in the air. A sweet smell, with nothing there to cause it, was one of the first signs. Within days she was dead, on the twenty-fifth of August, and her grave is the only plague grave inside the churchyard, because her husband was given leave to bury her there.

By the autumn the dying began at last to slow, the way a fire slows when it has run out of things to burn. The last to go was a man named Abraham Morten, at the beginning of November sixteen sixty-six. Fourteen months after the cloth came up from London, it was over. Of the roughly three hundred and fifty people who had stayed inside the line, somewhere close to two hundred and sixty were dead.

And the villages to the north were untouched. The plague had not crossed the line.

It is tempting to end there, with the clean shape of it — they sacrificed themselves and they saved the others, and the arithmetic balances. But that is the comfort we give ourselves, looking back. They did not have it. Standing inside that ring of stones in the summer of sixteen sixty-six, no one in Eyam knew whether their staying would save anyone at all. There were learned men, even then and afterward, who doubted it had been wise, who thought sealing the healthy in with the sick had only killed more of Eyam without certainty of sparing anyone else. The people who stayed did not get to know how it ended. They could not see the untouched villages to the north. They only had the line, and their word, and the next grave to dig.

That is the part that is theirs and no one else's. Not the outcome. The choice, made blind. Elizabeth Hancock did not bury her family on that hillside because she had been promised it would mean something. She buried them because they were hers and there was no one else to do it, inside a village that had agreed to stay and face the thing rather than carry it to strangers.

The graves are still there, on the slope above Riley, seven of them inside a low stone wall, alone in a field with the Derbyshire hills going on for miles in every direction. No village around them. Just the wall, and the long grass, and the wind coming over the moor.
