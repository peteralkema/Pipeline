He is thirty-three years old. He plays the violin for a living. His name is Wallace Hartley. Tonight he is on the Titanic. It is the night of April fourteenth, nineteen twelve. He leads the ship's band. Eight men in matching uniforms. Earlier, they played at dinner. Now they play the after-supper concert. First-class passengers sit in leather chairs, drinks in hand. A young couple dances slowly at the edge of the room. The sea is glass-flat. The sky is full of stars. It is the most ordinary night of the most extraordinary voyage. At eleven forty, there is a soft bump beneath their feet. Not loud. Not violent. Like a tray dropped in a far-off kitchen. The dancers stop. The conversation pauses. Then continues. Someone laughs. Probably nothing. They've all heard worse at sea. Hartley does not stop playing. The band does not stop playing. Outside, the ship is dying. Inside, the music plays on. Below decks, the cold sea is already pouring in. Stewards begin knocking on doors. Wake up. Put on your lifebelt. It's only a precaution, they say. It's nothing serious. Up in the lounge, the bandmaster has been told. He nods. He does not change his expression. He raises his bow. He chooses something cheerful. A waltz. Something bright. If the passengers see them play, they will not panic. That is the job. That is the only job left. Some passengers move toward the lifeboats. Some refuse to go. There are not enough boats. Not nearly enough boats. The musicians have been told to keep playing. They keep playing. After a time, the lounge begins to empty around them. They move out into the foyer, then onto the boat deck itself. Out in the cold air, under the stars, they begin to play again. The deck is tilting now. They brace their feet and play. Passengers run past them. The musicians do not look up. A woman pauses. She looks at them. Then she runs on. An officer asks Hartley if he wants to try for a boat. Hartley shakes his head. He raises the bow again. He is engaged to be married. Her name is Maria. They were going to be married in the summer. He had bought a ring. She is waiting for him in Lancashire. She does not know yet. On the deck, the angle of the ship gets sharper. Water reaches the lower decks. The lights begin to flicker. The band plays on. The instruments somehow stay in tune. Survivors will argue, for the rest of their lives, what they played at the end. Some will say it was a hymn. Nearer My God to Thee. Some will say it was a different tune entirely. Songe d'Automne. What everyone agreed on was that the band never stopped. By two in the morning, the deck is too steep to stand on. The musicians lay down their instruments. There is nothing more. Hartley straps his violin case to his body. He does not want to be parted from it. There is one more thing he says. We have done our duty. Then the great ship breaks. Then the great ship is gone. The freezing water takes most of them within an hour. Distant lifeboats hear the singing first. Then the silence. When the rescue ship arrives at dawn, the sea is empty. Of the eight musicians who played, none survived. Hartley's body was recovered ten days later. He was still wearing his uniform. His violin case was still strapped to him. He was thirty-three. He had been engaged for less than a year. His body was brought home to Lancashire. To the town where he grew up. Thirty thousand people came to his funeral. The streets were too narrow to hold them. They stood on rooftops. Seven bands played the music he had played at sea. Maria came. She wore mourning. She stood at the front. She would never marry. She lived another seventy years alone. His violin was returned to her, in its battered case. She kept it for the rest of her life. Then it disappeared. For almost a hundred years, no one knew where it was. Then, in two thousand and six, a man cleaning a loft found it. Inside was a violin, a silver plaque, and a name engraved on it. Wallace Hartley. A gift from Maria. The violin she gave him on their engagement. Experts authenticated it. The salt of the Atlantic was still in the wood. The violin that played as the ship went under. The actual one. It sold at auction for nine hundred thousand pounds. It is on display today. People travel from around the world to see it. They stand quietly. They take photographs. Some of them weep. A wooden object that should not exist. That refused to stay at the bottom. Wallace Hartley had two hours to live. He chose to play. Nothing forced him. No one would have blamed him for trying for a boat. He played because he chose to. Because the music was his answer. The ship sank. The musicians drowned. The cold ocean took them all. But the music did not. The music kept going. The music is still here. Wallace Hartley had one chance to be remembered, and he gave it away. He played for people he did not know. He played to keep them calm. He chose dignity in a place where almost no one would have judged him for less. His name became famous because of how he ended. Not how he lived. Maria never married. The violin survived. The music outlived the ship. He had one night to choose what kind of man he was. He chose the music.
