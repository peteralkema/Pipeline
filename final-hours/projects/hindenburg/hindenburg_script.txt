On the evening of May 6th, 1937, a forty-one-year-old woman named Matilde Doehner was sitting in the dining room of the airship Hindenburg with her three children. In twelve minutes, the air outside the windows would catch fire. In thirty-four seconds after that, she would have to choose which of her children she could save.

What you are about to see has been recreated from period photographs, the Doehner family's own surviving accounts, and the official Lakehurst investigation records.

Three days earlier, the family had boarded in Frankfurt, Germany. They were going home. Home was Mexico City. Hermann Doehner, Matilde's husband, was forty-nine. He had moved to Mexico from Germany when he was twenty-one and never left. He ran the country's largest German pharmaceutical company. The family had spent three months in Germany on a long holiday. Now they were returning by airship, then by train south to Mexico City. The tickets cost seven hundred US dollars per person. About fifteen thousand dollars today, in 2026 money. Five tickets. Irene was fourteen. Walter was ten. Werner was eight.

For three days, the Hindenburg moved across the Atlantic at one hundred and twenty kilometres an hour. Slow, by modern standards. The whole crossing was a single long meal. The dining room smelled of polished wood and faintly of engine oil. The carpet underfoot was so deep you could not hear your own footsteps. Through the observation windows, you could see whitecaps on the ocean four thousand feet below, but the airship moved so smoothly that the whitecaps looked like still photographs. Werner spent the mornings at the windows. Walter played cards in the lounge. Irene read. Hermann had brought a movie camera. He filmed the family at meals. He filmed the children at the observation windows. He filmed the Atlantic from above. Every evening he carried the camera back to his cabin and stored it carefully. He was filming the trip for his family in Mexico to see when they got home.

The morning of May 6th, the Hindenburg passed over Boston, hours behind schedule. By early afternoon it was over New Jersey. But the weather at Lakehurst had turned. Thunderstorms had grounded the airfield. Captain Max Pruss circled the New Jersey coast for hours, waiting for the storms to pass. The family ate lunch. Then they ate an early dinner. Then they waited. By half past six, the storms had cleared. The ship turned inland toward the mooring mast at Lakehurst. A small crowd of relatives and journalists had been waiting on the ground since morning. Hermann took out his movie camera. He started filming.

At seven twenty-one PM, the Hindenburg was two hundred feet above the field. The mooring lines were dropped to the ground crew. The descent toward the mast began. Hermann was still at the window. He had been filming the descent for perhaps three minutes when he lowered the camera and turned to walk back toward his cabin to put it away. At seven twenty-five PM, something began to flicker at the top of the tail. From the ground it looked at first like a mushroom of light. Within four seconds, it had spread the length of the airship. Inside the dining room, the windows turned orange. The light came from everywhere at once. The Hindenburg burned for thirty-four seconds.

The airship's hull was on the ground within seconds. But the dining room, on the portside mid-ship, was still fifteen to twenty feet up, tilted, with the observation windows facing the open field. Matilde had three children with her in the dining room. Hermann was somewhere on the other side of the burning airship. She took Walter first. He was ten. She lifted him to the window and threw him out. He bounced off the window frame on the way down. She had to throw him out a second time. Then Werner. He was eight. She turned back for Irene. Irene was fourteen. She was too heavy. Matilde had to make a choice she had no time to make. She climbed onto the window frame herself, alone. She jumped. She broke her pelvis on landing.

Ground crew pulled Irene from the wreckage. She was alive. She was severely burned. They drove her to Point Pleasant Hospital that night. A nurse fainted at the sight of her injuries when she was brought in. Irene died early the next morning. The next day's newspapers listed Hermann Doehner as missing. His body was found later that day in the wreckage of the dining room area. He had not made it back to his cabin. He was identified by his wedding ring. Matilde, Walter, and Werner survived. The two boys had burns on their hands, faces, and legs. Werner would be in the hospital for three months. The Hindenburg disaster killed thirteen of the thirty-six passengers and twenty-two of the sixty-one crew. The Doehner family lost two of five.

Matilde lived until 1972. She was buried in Mexico City beside her husband and her daughter. Walter became a chemist. He died in the 1950s. Werner became an electrical engineer. He lived in Mexico, then Colorado, then New Hampshire. He almost never spoke about what happened. When he died in 2019, at age 90, he was the last living person who had been on the Hindenburg. The film in Hermann's camera was never recovered.
