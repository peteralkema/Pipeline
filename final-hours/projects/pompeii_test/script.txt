They thought they had time.

It is the morning of the 24th of August. The city of Pompeii is awake, and ordinary. Bread is baking. The markets are opening. A dog sleeps in a doorway in the early heat.

Above the town, the mountain they call Vesuvius sits quiet, the way it has sat quiet for as long as anyone alive can remember. No one in the city below knows that it is a volcano. There is no word, yet, for what is about to happen to them.

A little after noon, the mountain opens.

A column of ash and rock climbs into the sky — higher than the eye can follow, twelve miles, then more — and at the top it spreads, and turns the afternoon to night.

And here is the strange part. The part that is easy to forget, two thousand years later, when we look at the bodies frozen in the ash.

They were warned.

They had hours. A whole day, for some of them. Time to gather what mattered, to reach the gate, to walk down to the sea and away.

Some did. The ones who ran early — they lived.

But many stayed. They closed their shutters against the falling ash and they waited for it to pass, the way bad weather passes. They did not know that this was not weather. They did not know that the air itself would come for them.

This is the story of the people who waited.

And of one family, down at the water's edge, who believed — right until the end — that the sea would carry them home.
