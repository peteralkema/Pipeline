It is just before midnight on Saturday the first of September, 1666. In a narrow timber-framed house on Pudding Lane, a young woman in her late teens or early twenties is finishing her work in a bakery kitchen. We do not know her name. No record of it has ever been found. In two hours she will become the first person to die in the Great Fire of London. She will die because she will not jump from the window. Everything you are about to see has been recreated from the household accounts, the British Library letter of October 1666, and the parliamentary investigation that followed. The bakery belongs to a man named Thomas Farriner. He bakes ship's biscuit for the Royal Navy. His shop sits on the eastern side of Pudding Lane — a narrow medieval street that runs from Eastcheap down toward the river, named for the carts of offal that pass through it on their way to the Thames. The kitchen at midnight smells of cooling oak ash, of yeast left rising for tomorrow's loaves, and faintly of the river — three streets south, low and slow in the dry summer. The flagstones are warm from a day of baking. The brick of the oven is still hot to the touch. A copper pot ticks softly as it cools on the trestle table. She would have heard the watchmen calling midnight — past twelve, and all is well — somewhere up Eastcheap. She would have heard the timber of the house above her settling into the night. She would have heard the rats in the walls. She would have heard the oven, fifteen feet across the kitchen from where she stood, still breathing its heat into the room. London in 1666 is a city of timber. Most of its houses were built in the time of Elizabeth, almost a century before — oak beams, lath and plaster walls, tarred wood eaves that overhang the lanes until the upper storeys nearly touch the houses opposite. Three storeys, sometimes four. A street like Pudding Lane is ten feet wide at the cobbles and three feet wide at the eaves. The summer of 1666 has been the driest in living memory. The Thames is low. The wells are thin. The oak of the city is dry to the heart. For four days an east wind has been blowing across the rooftops. At ten o'clock that Saturday night, Thomas Farriner went down into the bakehouse to rake the coals in the oven for the night. He raked them carefully. He swept the embers toward the centre of the firebox, away from the walls. He closed the iron door. He believed the fire was out. He went up the narrow wooden stairs to bed. Somewhere in the bakehouse — perhaps lodged in the soot of the chimney, perhaps fallen into the kindling stacked beside the wall, perhaps caught in the seam of an oak floorboard — a single ember was still alive. For an hour, nothing happens. For another hour, still nothing. The house above is asleep — Farriner in his bedchamber, Hanna in her room across the landing, Dagger in a smaller room toward the back, the maid in a room beneath the eaves on the upper floor. The ember is still alive. At about a quarter past midnight, the kindling stacked beside the oven begins to smoke. By half past midnight, the kindling is burning. By one o'clock, the fire has climbed into the wooden wall behind the oven. The oak beams are dry to the heart. They catch quickly. Smoke begins to rise through the floorboards of the rooms above. In a small room near the back of the house, Thomas Dagger — the journeyman who works alongside Farriner — wakes choking on the smoke. He shouts for the others. Farriner wakes. His daughter Hanna wakes. The maid wakes. The bakehouse below them is fully engulfed. The wooden stairs are burning. The fire has been moving while they slept. There is one way out — the window of an upstairs bedroom that looks across a narrow gap of perhaps four feet to the gutter of the next house. It is a drop of ten feet to the cobbles of Pudding Lane below, or a climb across the gap to the neighbour's roof. Thomas Farriner goes out first. He swings his legs through the casement, finds the gutter with his foot, and steps across. Hanna follows. She is in her shift. She finds her father's hand and crosses. Dagger climbs out after them, lowers himself onto the gutter, and follows them down through the neighbour's house and into the lane. The maid stays at the window. We do not know what she said. We do not know what they said to her. The letter that records the night, written that October to a Member of Parliament, says only this: his mayd was burnt in ye hous, not adventuring to escape as he, his daughter, and his man did, out of ye windowe and gutter. She did not adventure to escape. She was afraid of the jump. The roof of the bakery collapses within minutes. The fire spreads to the Star Inn next door, then to the houses across Fish Street Hill, then to the church of St Magnus the Martyr by the river. By dawn on Sunday, three hundred houses are gone. By Sunday evening, the fire has crossed Cannon Street. By Wednesday morning, four days later, St Paul's Cathedral has collapsed in on itself. The molten lead of its roof has run in rivers down Ludgate Hill into the Thames. Four-fifths of the City of London is gone. When the fire finally dies, thirteen thousand two hundred houses have been destroyed. Eighty-seven parish churches. The Royal Exchange. Old St Paul's. The Guildhall. About a hundred thousand Londoners are homeless. The official death toll is six. The real number was almost certainly much higher. Many of the urban poor — the elderly, the sick, the very young, the servants in upstairs rooms — were never accounted for. Their bodies, where they were found at all, had been cremated in the heat. The maid in the bakery is counted as one of the six. Her name was never recorded. Thomas Farriner survived. He rebuilt his bakery on the same spot on Pudding Lane within two years. He never publicly accepted that the fire had started in his oven. He died in December 1670, four years and three months after the night she did not jump, still maintaining his innocence. Two hundred and two feet from where the bakery stood, the city of London built a column to the fire. It is two hundred and two feet tall. It still stands. The column has a long Latin inscription at its base. It names the kings who reigned during the fire and during the column's construction. It names the architects, Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke. It names the Lord Mayor and the city officials who commissioned it. It does not name the woman who died first. Hers was never written down.
