## PART ONE - The Village and the Pit

[A] To understand the loss, you have to understand the village. Senghenydd was a mining community in the fullest sense - a place that existed to work the coal, its rows of terraced houses built for the miners, its whole life organised around the shifts of the pit. The men went down; the coal came up; the village lived on it. Almost every family had men and boys underground.

[A] The work was hard and dangerous, as coal mining always was. The men knew the risks - the roof falls, the gas, the ever-present threat of fire in the deep tunnels. But it was their living, and their fathers' living, and they went down each morning and came up each evening, and the danger was simply part of the life.

## PART TWO - The Explosion

[A] On that October morning, with more than nine hundred men and boys underground, the disaster came. Deep in the workings, gas ignited - and in a coal mine, an explosion of gas can throw up a cloud of fine coal dust, which itself ignites, turning a single blast into a chain of fire that races through the tunnels. That is what happened at Senghenydd.

[A] The explosion tore through a large part of the mine, killing men outright, collapsing tunnels, and starting fires deep underground. And then came the quieter, deadlier killer - the poisoned, oxygen-starved air that fills a mine after such a blast, which suffocates those the explosion itself did not reach.

## PART THREE - The Rescue

[A] The rescue was heroic and desperate. Rescue teams went down into the shattered, burning, gas-filled mine, at terrible risk to themselves, to search for survivors and bring out the living and the dead. They fought fires, cleared collapsed tunnels, and pushed into air that could kill them, working for days in the depths.

## CLOSE

[A] Senghenydd is a lesson about warnings unheeded. The pit had exploded before. The dangers were known. The men who died in nineteen thirteen died, in part, because the lessons of the earlier explosion had been allowed to fade.
