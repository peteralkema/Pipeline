On the night of the twenty-eighth of December, 1879, the longest bridge in the world stood across the Firth of Tay, in the east of Scotland. Nearly two miles of iron and lattice, carrying a single railway line seventy feet above the water. That evening a storm came down the firth from the west — a gale the Dundee office would log at force ten, perhaps eleven. And at a quarter past seven, in the dark and the rain, a train began to cross.

By the time it reached the middle, the bridge, the train, and everyone aboard would be gone.

What follows has been reconstructed from the Board of Trade inquiry, the testimony of the men in the signal box, and the accounts of those who stood on the shore that night.

The train was the evening service from Burntisland, bound for Dundee. It had crossed the country in the worsening weather, and stopped at the small station of St Fort to give up its tickets. Seventy-five were collected there. The exact number of souls who went on past that point was never agreed — some travelled without tickets, some held returns. The river would keep its own count.

In the signal box at the south end stood Thomas Barclay and his friend John Watt, sheltering from the gale. They watched the train's three lights move out onto the bridge, into the high girders — the great iron cage at the centre, where the line ran highest above the water.

The wind was striking the bridge broadside now, harder than anything its designer had reckoned for. Inside the carriages, lamplight. Passengers wrapped against the cold, the windows running with rain, the river invisible below. None of them could have known that the structure carrying them had been built without margin for a night like this.

At about seven sixteen, John Watt saw a sudden rush of sparks burst from the high girders — bright, and then gone. The three lights went with them.

He thought, at first, that the train had only passed out of view. The two men waited for it to appear on the Dundee side. It never did. When they tried to walk out onto the bridge to look, they could not stand against the wind.

Down at the water's edge, others gathered, staring into the dark where the centre of the bridge should have been. The high girders were gone. The train was in the river, seventy feet below, with everyone it carried.

In the days that followed, divers went down into the cold Tay. They found the locomotive almost whole on the riverbed. It was raised, repaired, and put back into service, where it ran on the railways for another forty years. The men who drove it called it The Diver.

Forty-six bodies were recovered. The rest the firth kept. The official count settled near sixty, but no one was ever certain — some who boarded that night were never named, and are missing from every record that survives.

We cross such spans every day, and trust them to hold. On the night of the twenty-eighth of December, 1879, the longest bridge in the world did not.
