It is five-oh-six in the afternoon, the twenty-seventh of March, 1977. Los Rodeos airport, on the island of Tenerife in the Canary Islands. Captain Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten is sitting at the controls of a Boeing 747. He is fifty years old. He is KLM's chief of flight training. His photograph is printed on every safety poster in every KLM cockpit in the world. In one hundred and twenty-seven seconds, he will kill five hundred and eighty-three people. What you are about to see has been recreated from the cockpit voice recorder transcripts, the Spanish and Dutch accident investigation records, and survivor testimonies given in the days that followed. The dialogue is verbatim from the cockpit tapes. The runway ahead of him is barely visible. Atlantic fog has rolled in from the west. The runway edge lights are faint smears of amber that vanish after fifty meters. The temperature is twelve degrees Celsius. The cockpit smells of jet fuel and warm electronics. The four engines are idling, a constant low vibration through the floor. Van Zanten does not know that another Boeing 747 is sitting on the same runway, somewhere ahead of him in the fog. Behind him, two hundred and forty-eight people. Most are Dutch holidaymakers. They had been promised the Canary Islands. They had been diverted here when a bomb went off in the terminal at Las Palmas, the next island over. They have been sitting on the tarmac for four hours. Van Zanten wants to go home. His crew is approaching the limit of their legal duty hours. If he does not take off in the next thirty minutes, the flight will be cancelled and the passengers will be stranded on Tenerife. Three days earlier, Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten had been the most respected pilot in KLM. The airline used his portrait in their corporate advertising. His title was Chief of Flight Training, the man who taught younger pilots how to never make decisions like the one he is about to make. In the right-hand seat, his first officer Klaas Meurs is watching him. Meurs is thirty-two. He has read Van Zanten's books on aviation safety. He has watched Van Zanten's training films. He has flown with Van Zanten before. Van Zanten releases the brakes. Meurs says: We don't have clearance. Van Zanten says: We are now at takeoff. It is an ambiguous phrase. In Dutch English it could mean we are starting our takeoff. In aviation English it sounds like we are at the takeoff position, ready and waiting. Half a mile down the runway, in the fog, the second Boeing 747 is moving slowly along the centerline. Pan Am flight 1736. Captain Victor Grubbs, first officer Robert Bragg. They have three hundred and ninety-six people aboard. They have been told to taxi off the main runway at exit four. They cannot find exit four in the fog. They are still on the runway. Through their radio, they hear Van Zanten's voice. They look at each other. Bragg keys the microphone. He says: We are still taxiing down the runway. But two transmissions cross. Both crews hear only static. In the KLM cockpit, the engines roar. Meurs looks across at his captain. He has read this man's books. He has watched this man's training films. He has flown with this man before. Van Zanten is taking off without clearance. For thirteen seconds the KLM 747 accelerates down the runway. The fog ahead begins to thin. Then the Pan Am 747 appears in the windscreen, broadside, trying to turn off at exit four. Too late. Van Zanten pulls back on the yoke. The KLM 747's undercarriage scrapes the top of the Pan Am fuselage and shears it open. The KLM does not quite get airborne. It stalls. It crashes one hundred and fifty meters down the runway and explodes. Two hundred and forty-eight people on the KLM flight. No survivors. Three hundred and twenty-six die in the explosion and the fire that follows on the Pan Am plane. Sixty-one survive, mostly people sitting in the front upper deck, who jump from the wing and run before the second blast. Five hundred and eighty-three people are dead. The fog above the runway glows orange. The smell of burning jet fuel carries half a mile. In Amsterdam, KLM's head office receives the first reports of a major airline disaster in the Canary Islands. They do not yet know which airline is involved. They want to send their chief of safety training to investigate, because he is the most senior aviation safety expert in the company. Somebody starts to make the phone call. Then somebody else realizes who they are about to phone. The cockpit voice recorder is recovered from the wreckage three days later. It captures the last thirty seconds. Van Zanten saying: We are now at takeoff. Meurs saying: We don't have clearance. Van Zanten not answering. In the investigation that followed, fifteen factors were found to have contributed. The bomb at Las Palmas. The fog. The radio transmissions overlapping. The unclear English. But one factor was identified as the cause. A senior captain, the most senior captain, made a decision he had spent his entire career teaching others not to make. In the photograph that ran in every Dutch newspaper the day after the crash, Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten was wearing his uniform, smiling. The caption underneath called him KLM's chief of safety. He had flown in fog. He had not waited for clearance. He had killed five hundred and eighty-three people in eight seconds. His own children buried him without ever hearing the recording. The recording is in the Dutch national aviation archive. It runs for thirty seconds. It is the most listened-to recording in aviation history. Every commercial pilot trained in the last forty-nine years has heard it. The pilot is silent. Then he says: We are now at takeoff.
