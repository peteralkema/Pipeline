## PART EIGHT — The Oxygen Catastrophe

[A] Number eight. More than two billion years ago, the Earth was a world without breath. The air held almost no oxygen at all, and nothing alive needed it.
VISUAL: An early Earth shoreline of bare dark rock meeting flat grey water, a thick orange-brown sky overhead, utterly barren.

[A] Life was simple and small — microbes in the shallow seas. And then one kind of microbe learned a new trick: to catch sunlight and make food from it.
VISUAL: An underwater view of green-blue microbial mats coating shallow rocks, fine streams of tiny bubbles rising toward a bright surface.

[A] The trick worked too well. As those microbes spread across the seas, they pumped out a strange new waste gas in unimaginable quantities — oxygen.
VISUAL: Vast green-blue microbial mats stretching across a shallow seafloor, dense clouds of tiny bubbles rising from them everywhere.

[A] To the life of that age, oxygen was a poison. It rusted the iron in the oceans, then began to fill the sky, and the old microbial world began to choke on it.
VISUAL: Rust-red bands of iron settling onto a seafloor, the water clouding orange, the old grey microbial mats dimming and dying.

[A] We see it in the rock as great striped bands of rusted iron, hundreds of metres thick — the oceans themselves rusting solid as the oxygen rose.
VISUAL: A colossal cliff of red-and-black banded iron rock, the stripes razor-sharp, dwarfing the bare land around it.

[A] It may have been the first mass extinction in the history of life. A whole living world, poisoned by its own success, by the very gas we cannot live without.
VISUAL: A bleak primordial sea under a brightening sky, the water gone clear and empty, dead microbial mats bleaching pale on the rocks.

[A] And in the cruel arithmetic of deep time, that catastrophe was also a beginning. The oxygen it left behind would, billions of years later, let creatures like us exist.
VISUAL: A slow upward tilt from a barren ancient sea to a clearing blue sky, the first thin sunlight breaking through the haze.

[A] For a billion years before this, the world had been strangely stable — so quiet that geologists half-jokingly call it the boring billion. The oxygen crisis helped end that long calm.
VISUAL: A still ancient sea under a pale unchanging sky, the water flat and grey, a sense of vast unbroken time hanging over it.

[A] What had nearly killed the old world had remade it. A planet that ran on poison was now a planet that ran on breath — and the stage was set for everything that came after.
VISUAL: A clearing primordial sky over a calm sea, the first shafts of bright sunlight reaching down through thinning orange haze.

## PART SEVEN — Snowball Earth

[A] Number seven. Around seven hundred million years ago, the entire planet froze. Not an ice age at the poles. Ice everywhere, all the way down to the equator.
VISUAL: A white frozen Earth seen from space, the whole globe locked in pale ice and cloud, no blue ocean visible anywhere on it.

[A] Glaciers ground down to the warm latitudes. The oceans froze over more than a kilometre deep. The whole world became a single white ball, hanging in the dark.
VISUAL: A vast frozen sea stretching to the horizon under a cold pale sky, cracked ice sheets and frost-shattered ridges in the foreground.

[A] We know it happened because we find the scars of glaciers — ground rock, dropped stones — in places that sat at the tropics at the time.
VISUAL: A close geological view of scratched, glacier-polished rock, a large out-of-place boulder resting on it, dropped by long-gone ice.

[A] For millions of years the Earth lay frozen and nearly dead, life clinging on in thin cracks of meltwater and around the warmth of undersea volcanoes.
VISUAL: A dark crack of liquid water in a vast white ice sheet, faint warmth glowing from deep below, the surface frozen solid above.

[A] What finally saved the world was fire. Volcanoes kept erupting through the ice, slowly choking the air with gases that trapped the sun's heat.
VISUAL: A dark volcanic peak breaking up through a white ice sheet, venting smoke and ash into a frozen sky, meltwater pooling at its base.

[A] When the thaw came, it came hard — a runaway melting that drowned the white world and lit the fuse, some think, for the explosion of complex life that followed.
VISUAL: A vast ice sheet breaking apart into dark meltwater, great slabs calving and tilting, blue ocean spreading between the floes.

[A] When the white world finally thawed, the warming oceans filled with oxygen and nutrients, and life seized the moment. The variety of animals exploded as never before.
VISUAL: A bright shallow sea newly teeming with strange early creatures of many shapes, sunlight streaming down through clear blue water.

[A] So the planet's deepest freeze, which had nearly ended life, may have set the stage for its grandest flowering. In deep time, catastrophe and creation are often the same event.
VISUAL: A vibrant ancient reef alive with movement, light dancing across crowded living surfaces, a world reborn after the ice.

## PART SIX — The Late Ordovician

[A] Number six. About four hundred and forty-five million years ago, life had finally filled the seas — but the land was still bare, and the world was about to turn lethal.
VISUAL: A shallow ancient sea floor crowded with corals and shelled creatures under bright water, the distant shore a strip of bare grey rock.

[A] The trouble came as a deep, sudden cold. Great ice sheets spread across the southern lands, and the sea level dropped, draining the warm shallows where life was richest.
VISUAL: A retreating shallow sea exposing a wide reef to the air, stranded shells and corals drying on bare rock under a cold grey sky.

[A] As the shallows vanished, the creatures that lived in them vanished too. Then the ice melted, the seas surged back, and the survivors were drowned and starved of oxygen.
VISUAL: A dim, oxygen-poor seafloor, dark water heavy and still, the pale empty shells of dead sea creatures scattered across the silt.

[A] Some scientists suspect a stranger trigger as well — a burst of radiation from a dying star, stripping the sky's shield and chilling the world.
VISUAL: A distant brilliant point of light flaring in a deep space sky, faint beams reaching toward a small blue-and-white Earth far below.

[A] However it began, the toll was staggering. The Late Ordovician extinction wiped out the majority of species in the sea — one of the deadliest events life has ever faced.
VISUAL: An empty grey seabed strewn with the shells of the dead, silt drifting down through cold dim green water, nothing moving.

[A] What makes this one chilling is that it was not a single catastrophe but a drumbeat. Pulse after pulse of dying, spread across millions of years, each one thinning the seas further.
VISUAL: A murky sea darkening in stages, the water growing dimmer and emptier with each layer, a slow fade from life to death.

[A] The dead zones spread from the seafloor upward, smothering everything. Whole groups of armoured fish that had ruled the oceans simply ceased to exist.
VISUAL: A dim seafloor scattered with the heavy armoured plates of dead fish, dark oxygen-poor water pressing down from above.

[A] Strangely, the trigger may have been life itself. The first true forests were spreading across the land, and their roots and falling leaves poisoned the seas with runaway nutrients.
VISUAL: An early forest of tall primitive trees on a muddy shore, dark nutrient-rich water draining from the land into a dull green sea.

## PART FIVE — The Late Devonian

[A] Number five. Around three hundred and seventy-five million years ago came not a single blow but a long, slow strangling of the seas, drawn out over millions of years.
VISUAL: A murky green ancient sea under a dim hazy sky, the water thick and still, faint shapes of reef structures fading into the gloom.

[A] The great reefs of the age, vast living structures larger than any today, sickened and collapsed as the oceans lost their oxygen and turned to dead, stagnant water.
VISUAL: A once-bright reef now grey and crumbling underwater, dark lifeless water above it, sediment settling over the dying coral.

[A] On the young land, the very first forests may have helped tip the balance — their roots breaking up the rock, feeding the seas until they choked.
VISUAL: An early forest of strange leafless tree-like plants on a muddy shore, their roots cracking into bare rock, a dim sea beyond.

[A] Wave after wave of dying swept the oceans. By the end, the reefs were gone, and the seas would not see their like again for a very long time.
VISUAL: A barren, silted seafloor where a great reef once stood, only low grey rubble remaining, the dark water empty above it.

## PART FOUR — The Great Dying

[A] The eruptions flooded the air with poison and heat. The seas lost their oxygen. The land baked. And almost everything alive simply died.

[A] Ninety-six out of every hundred kinds of sea creature were lost. Seven in ten on the land. Life on Earth came as close to total death as it has ever come.

[A] The killing did not even need an asteroid or an ocean of lava to finish it. Once the air and seas were poisoned, the damage fed on itself for thousands of years.

[A] When it was finally over, the recovery was the slowest life has ever made. It took millions of years for the world to grow rich and varied again.

[A] In Siberia, the eruptions did not stop for an almost unimaginable span — perhaps a million years of near-continuous outpouring, layering lava miles deep across the land.

## PART THREE — The End-Triassic

[A] Number three. About two hundred and one million years ago, just as life had clawed its way back, the world cracked open again.

[A] The gases from those eruptions poisoned the air and the seas once more, and another wave of death swept the planet, on land and in the water alike.

[A] But this catastrophe cleared a stage. With so many rivals gone, one group of animals was left free to inherit the world — the dinosaurs.

[A] Out of the wreckage, one group of survivors rose to fill the empty world — small, swift, and new. For the next hundred and thirty million years, the dinosaurs would own the Earth.

## PART TWO — The Asteroid

## PART ONE — The Day We Almost Ended

[A] Number one. The most recent near-end, and in one way the most personal — because this time, the species hanging by a thread was our own.

[A] It was one of the largest eruptions in millions of years. It hurled so much ash into the sky that layers of it can still be found thousands of kilometres away.

[A] The dust dimmed the sun and the world grew colder. For our small, scattered ancestors, already few in number, it may have been very nearly the end.

[A] Of all eight catastrophes, this is the one that reaches us most directly. We are not just survivors of it in theory. We are, quite literally, the descendants of the few who lived.

[A] To many scientists, that sameness is the fingerprint of a bottleneck — a moment when our numbers crashed to almost nothing, and the whole human future passed through the eye of a needle.
