## PART ONE — The World Before the Dinosaurs

[A] We imagine that the dinosaurs always ruled the Earth. But for the first stretch of their existence, they did not. They were a minor group, living in the shadow of others.

[A] In the Triassic age, before this catastrophe, the Earth belonged not to the dinosaurs, but to their rivals — a varied and formidable cast of other reptiles, many of them larger and fiercer than the early dinosaurs.

[A] Among them were the great relatives of the crocodiles — not the river-bound crocodiles of today, but a sprawling dynasty of land-dwelling hunters and giants that ruled the continents.

[A] These were the rulers of the Triassic world. The early dinosaurs were there too — but small, modest, and outnumbered, living in the margins of a world run by others.

[A] Had nothing changed, it is entirely possible the dinosaurs would have stayed that way forever — a minor branch of life, never rising to greatness, soon forgotten.

[A] But the world the rivals ruled had a hidden flaw, written into its very geography. For in the Triassic, all the world's land was joined into a single vast supercontinent.

[A] We call it Pangaea — one great landmass, stretching from pole to pole, surrounded by a single world-ocean. And a supercontinent that large does not last. Beneath it, the heat of the Earth was building.

## PART TWO — Pangaea Tears Apart

[A] As Pangaea split, the rift became a wound that bled fire. Along the tearing seam, the ground cracked open and lava began to flood out across the land, on a colossal scale.

[A] This was another of the great flood-basalt catastrophes — the same kind of eruption that had caused the Great Dying, and would later accompany the death of the dinosaurs. We call this one the Central Atlantic eruptions.
VISUAL: Sheets of lava flooding flat across a dark landscape from a great rift, layer upon layer building across the splitting supercontinent, no cone in sight.

[A] Lava poured out along the rift for hundreds of thousands of years, building vast beds of basalt across the tearing edges of the splitting continents.

[A] So enormous was this eruption that its cooled lava is now scattered across four different continents — torn apart and carried away as the rift widened into the Atlantic, but all of it erupted in the same brief moment of geological time.

[A] And as it erupted, it did what these catastrophes always do. It poured gas into the sky — carbon dioxide to overheat the world, and sulfur and acid to poison it.

[A] The carbon dioxide drove the planet into a fierce, rapid warming. Temperatures climbed. And as before, the carbon soaked into the oceans and turned them acidic, dissolving the shells of sea life.

[A] The crocodile-relatives and many of the other dominant reptiles were devastated. The masters of the old world were cut down in their masses by the heat, the acid, and the chaos.
