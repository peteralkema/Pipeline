title: The Ordovician Extinction: The First Mass Extinction — and the Star That May Have Caused It
description: Around four hundred and forty-five million years ago, life on Earth was nearly wiped out for the first time. In an age when all complex life still lived in the sea, something killed off most of the species in the oceans — the first of the great mass extinctions, and one of the deadliest of them all. This is the story of the Ordovician extinction: the strange, alien, all-ocean world it struck; how a sudden ice age froze the planet and drained the seas; how a second blow fell when the ice melted again; and the haunting possibility that the whole catastrophe began far out in space, with the death of a distant star — a gamma-ray burst that may have stripped the Earth of its protective shield. Drawn from the fossils of the trilobites, the rocks of the ancient seabeds, and the deep logic of deep time. Could a dying star really reach across the galaxy to kill life on Earth? ## PART TWO — The World Freezes

[A] One of the great continents of the Ordovician world, an enormous landmass we call Gondwana, had been drifting, slowly, across the face of the Earth — and it was drifting toward the South Pole.

[A] When a large continent sits over a pole, ice can form on it — and grow. And as Gondwana settled over the South Pole, a vast ice sheet began to build across it.
VISUAL: A great ice sheet spreading across a polar continent, white ice advancing over bare rock, the cold gripping the southern landmass.

[A] The growing ice changed the whole planet. White ice reflects the sun's heat back into space, and so a freezing world cools itself further still, in a runaway plunge into an ice age.

## PART FOUR — The Dying Star

[A] Far out in the galaxy, massive stars end their lives in titanic explosions. And the very largest can release, in their death, a beam of radiation more violent than almost anything in the universe — a gamma-ray burst.
VISUAL: A distant massive star erupting in a colossal explosion, two brilliant beams of blue-white radiation lancing out from its poles across deep space.

[A] A gamma-ray burst is a focused jet of pure, deadly radiation, fired out from a dying star as it collapses. If such a beam were ever pointed at the Earth, even from thousands of light-years away, it could be catastrophic.
VISUAL: A narrow brilliant beam of gamma radiation firing across the galaxy from a distant exploding star, racing toward a small distant Earth.

[A] Some scientists have proposed that, around the time the Ordovician extinction began, such a beam may have struck our planet — the death-cry of a distant star, reaching across the void to touch the Earth.
VISUAL: A brilliant beam of radiation from a far dying star striking the upper atmosphere of an ancient Earth, the sky flaring under the cosmic blow.

[A] A gamma-ray burst would not have burned the surface directly. Its damage would have been subtler, and in a way more terrible. It would have struck the Earth's protective shield — the ozone layer high in the sky.
VISUAL: A beam of radiation slamming into the high atmosphere of an ancient Earth, the protective ozone layer flaring and tearing under the cosmic strike.

[A] In moments, the burst could have stripped away much of that ozone, ripping a hole in the planet's shield against the Sun's own harmful ultraviolet light.
VISUAL: The high atmosphere of an ancient Earth torn open, the protective ozone shredded, the bare sunlight of an unshielded sky pouring through.

[A] With the shield gone, the Sun's ultraviolet rays would have flooded down onto the surface — onto the shallow seas, killing the tiny drifting plankton that lay at the very base of all ocean life.

[A] It is, it must be said, only a possibility — a hypothesis, not a certainty. A gamma-ray burst leaves no crater and no ash. We cannot dig up its proof the way we can an asteroid's.

[A] But it remains one of the most haunting ideas in all of science — that the first time life on Earth was nearly destroyed, the trigger may have been pulled half a galaxy away, by the death of a star that never knew we existed.
VISUAL: A vast starfield with one distant star exploding in brilliant beams, a tiny fragile Earth far below, the cosmic indifference immense and cold.
