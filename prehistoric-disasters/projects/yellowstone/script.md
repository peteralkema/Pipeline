## PART ONE — The Engine Underneath

## PART TWO — The Three Eruptions

[A] Three times, the pressure in that chamber has built past breaking, and Yellowstone has unleashed a super-eruption. We can read all three in the rock.
VISUAL: A tall cliff face showing three distinct thick pale layers of volcanic ash separated by darker rock, each a separate eruption.

[A] The first and largest came about two point one million years ago. We call its deposit the Huckleberry Ridge ash. It was one of the biggest eruptions in the last few million years.
VISUAL: A massive ancient ash column erupting over a primeval landscape, the sky utterly black with ash, the ground glowing far below.

[A] It threw out enough material to bury a vast area metres deep, and spread a fine dusting of ash across most of what is now the United States.
VISUAL: A map-style aerial of a continent with a huge grey ash plume fanning out from a single point, covering much of the landmass.

[A] The second eruption, around one point three million years ago, was smaller, but still far beyond anything in recorded human history.
VISUAL: A large ash column rising over a wide valley at dusk, dark ash raining down across a forested landscape stretching to the horizon.

[A] The third and most recent came about six hundred and forty thousand years ago, and it carved out the caldera you can still see today.
VISUAL: An aerial of a vast caldera basin forming as the ground collapses inward, ash erupting around its rim, the floor sinking.

[A] Each of these eruptions did the same terrible thing. So much magma emptied out of the chamber that the ground above had nothing left to stand on, and it collapsed.
VISUAL: A cutaway showing a magma chamber emptying as the roof of rock above it caves down into the void, ash venting from the cracks.

[A] That collapse is what makes a caldera — not a mountain blown apart, but a great circle of land that dropped into the emptied chamber beneath it.
VISUAL: A wide aerial of a roughly circular caldera, kilometres across, its rim a ring of low cliffs around a sunken green floor.

[A] The Yellowstone caldera is so large that for years no one recognised it. You cannot see it from the ground. You have to look from the sky to see the giant's footprint.
VISUAL: A high satellite-style view revealing a vast circular basin in the landscape, its outline only clear from far above.

[A] To measure eruptions, scientists use a scale of explosive force. Most eruptions you have heard of sit low on it. A super-eruption sits at the very top.
VISUAL: A simple ascending diagram of eruption sizes, small puffs at the bottom growing to a colossal column at the top of the scale.

[A] When Mount Saint Helens erupted in nineteen-eighty, it stunned the world. Yellowstone's largest eruption threw out roughly a thousand times more material.

[A] Even Tambora, the eruption that caused a year without a summer across the world two centuries ago, was small beside a Yellowstone super-eruption.

[A] The second of Yellowstone's three eruptions, the one we call Mesa Falls, was the smallest of them — and it was still far larger than any eruption in all of recorded human history.

## PART THREE — What a Super-Eruption Does

VISUAL: A colossal eruption column boiling kilometres into the sky over a darkened landscape, dwarfing distant mountains beneath it.

[A] Higher up, the eruption column would punch into the upper atmosphere, lifting an unimaginable mass of ash to be carried away on the winds.

[A] Downwind, ash would fall like a grey, choking snow, collapsing roofs, smothering crops, and clogging the lungs of anything that breathed it.

[A] In the past eruptions, that ash reached clear across the continent. A future one would do the same, blanketing vast regions in a deep, suffocating grey.

## PART FOUR — The Trail of Dead Craters

[A] The whole region trembles constantly with small earthquakes, thousands every year, most far too faint to feel — the rock adjusting as fluids move below.

## PART SIX — Is It Overdue?

[A] And now the question everyone asks. You have surely heard it. Yellowstone erupts every six hundred thousand years or so, the story goes, and the last one was six hundred and forty thousand years ago — so it is overdue.
VISUAL: A dramatic dusk over the caldera, a faint ominous red glow on the clouds, the wide basin dark and quiet below.

[A] It is a tidy story. It is also wrong. Volcanoes do not work like clocks, and the giant has never kept an appointment in its life.
VISUAL: A close view of weathered volcanic rock layers, irregular and uneven, the gaps between eruption deposits clearly all different sizes.

[A] Look at the real intervals. Between the first eruption and the second, about eight hundred thousand years passed. Between the second and the third, about six hundred and sixty thousand.
VISUAL: A geological diagram of three ash layers with very different thicknesses of rock between them, the spacing plainly irregular.

[A] Two intervals, both different, are not a schedule. They cannot tell you when, or even if, a fourth great eruption will come. Three points do not make a clock.
VISUAL: A cliff face showing the three ash bands, the eye drawn to the unequal gaps between them, no pattern to be found.

[A] There is more. A super-eruption may not even be the most likely future for Yellowstone at all. In between the giant eruptions, it has had many smaller ones.

[A] Great slow flows of lava have oozed across the caldera since the last big eruption — enormous in their own right, but nothing like a continent-burying super-eruption.

[A] The honest scientific answer is humbling. The giant will almost certainly erupt again someday — but most likely as one of these smaller events, and most likely not for a very long time.

[A] There is even a real question among scientists about whether Yellowstone will ever produce another super-eruption at all.

## PART SEVEN — The More Likely Disaster

[A] There is a catastrophe at Yellowstone far more likely than a super-eruption — one most people have never heard of, because it does not fit the doomsday story.
VISUAL: A steaming thermal basin at dusk, pools of hot water with scorched rims, the ground around them cracked and mineral-stained.

[A] It is called a hydrothermal explosion. Not magma erupting, but superheated water, flashing suddenly to steam underground and blasting a crater open at the surface.
VISUAL: A sudden burst of steam and shattered rock erupting from flat ground, debris flying outward, a ragged crater opening in an instant.

[A] These happen far more often than great eruptions. The largest left a crater in the shore of Yellowstone Lake more than two kilometres wide, blasted out in moments.
VISUAL: An aerial of a wide shallow bay with an unnaturally round shoreline, a vast old explosion crater now filled by lake water.

[A] A big one today, in a crowded part of the park, could be deadly for anyone nearby — sudden, local, and violent, with little warning.
VISUAL: A boardwalk crossing a steaming basin near a hot spring, small figures on it, the ground around faintly cracked and discoloured.

[A] But it would be a local tragedy, not a global one. No ash winter, no buried continent. It is the realistic hazard, sitting quietly beneath the famous, unlikely one.
VISUAL: A single hot spring steaming gently at golden hour, rings of mineral colour around it, the wider landscape calm and bright.

[A] Knowing this changes how you should hear the word Yellowstone. The likely danger is small and local. The famous danger is real but rare and far off. The fear has them exactly backwards.
