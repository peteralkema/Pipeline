## PART ONE - THE GIFT THAT SEEMED GOOD

[A] To feel why heaven counted writing a crime, you first have to feel how good it seems. Of all the watchers' gifts, this is the one you would want. Weapons kill. Sorcery deceives. But writing - writing remembers.

[A] Before the mark, a thought lived only as long as the breath that spoke it. A wisdom died with the wise. A promise was only as strong as the memory of the one who heard it. Everything human was written on water, and vanished.

[A] And then came the mark, and everything changed. A thought could be set down and carried a thousand miles. A wisdom could outlive the wise by a thousand years. The dead could speak to the unborn. Writing gave mankind a kind of memory that did not die.

[A] This is Penemue's gift, and it is the foundation of everything we know. The scriptures themselves survive only because someone learned to write. ## PART TWO - THE POISON IN THE INK

[A] So why did heaven count it a sin? Because the same power runs both ways. A mark that can make a truth immortal can make a lie immortal too. And the human heart, the old text knew, writes down its darkness far more eagerly than its light.

[A] Before writing, a lie died with the liar. A curse faded when the cursing mouth was stopped. Evil was loud but it was mortal - it could not reach past the life of the one who spoke it. Writing tore down that last mercy.

[A] Now the lie could be written, and copied, and carried, and taught to those not yet born. A single dark thought could poison a thousand minds across a thousand years. The mark gave sin what it had never had before - a way to outlive the sinner.

[A] And that, the old text says, is Penemue's true crime. Not that he taught a skill, but that he handed a fallen race the power to make its falls permanent. Through this knowledge, the text says, many have gone astray - because now their going-astray could be written down and passed on.

## PART THREE - THE BITTER AND THE SWEET

[A] The text pairs writing with a second, stranger phrase. Penemue taught mankind, it says, the bitter and the sweet. And the old readers understood this as more than taste. It was the knowledge of how to divide the world into what pleases and what does not.

[A] Set beside writing, the pairing makes a terrible sense. Penemue taught mankind both to record its desires and to refine them - to write down what it wanted, and to make what it wanted ever more enticing. The gift of memory, joined to the gift of temptation.
