## PART ONE — The Garden and the One Command

[A] The man had been alone, and God had said that was not good. So while he slept, God took part of his own side and made the woman — bone of his bone, the text says. Two halves of one life. That union is the very thing the serpent would set out to divide.

## PART TWO — The Serpent

[A] It did not begin with a command, or a threat, or a promise. The oldest temptation in the world began with a question. Did God really say, the serpent asked, that you must not eat from any tree in the garden?

[A] Listen to what it does. It twists the command before it even attacks it. God did not forbid every tree. He forbade one. But the serpent reframes the single limit as if the whole garden were a cage. It makes generosity sound like cruelty.

[A] And the woman answers honestly. We may eat from the trees, she says. Only not from the one in the middle. God said we must not even touch it, or we will die. And the serpent had what it wanted — a conversation. A door left open just a crack.

[A] Then it strikes. Not with a knife. With four words that have echoed ever since. You will not surely die.

[A] It is the exact opposite of what God had said — and it is the first lie ever spoken. God said death. The serpent says no death. And between those two voices, the woman now has to choose which one she will believe.

[A] And then comes the cleverest poison of all. God knows, the serpent says, that on the day you eat it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God yourself — knowing good and evil. It tells her that God is holding her back. That the limit is not protection. It is jealousy.

[A] This is the lie underneath every other lie. That God cannot be trusted. That his rules are chains. That to become more, you step over the one line he drew. The serpent did not force her hand. It only changed how she saw the fruit — then went quiet, and let her look.

## PART THREE — The Fruit

[A] So the woman looked at the tree. And the old text tells us exactly what she saw, in three steps, each one deeper than the last. She saw that the tree was good for food.

[A] Then she saw that it was a delight to the eyes. Beautiful. The most desirable thing in a garden full of beautiful things. And then the deepest hook of all — that the tree was desirable to make one wise. To make her more than she was.

[A] Good for the body. Beautiful to the eye. And a promise of becoming something greater. Notice that the lie is woven through with things that are almost true. The fruit was beautiful. ## PART FOUR — The Voice in the Garden

[A] They heard the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden, in the cool of the day. The same presence they had once walked beside without a trace of fear. And now, for the first time, they were afraid of it. So they hid.
VISUAL: A broad shaft of warm gold light moving slowly through the distant trees as if approaching, and two small faceless silhouettes pressed back into deep shadow behind a trunk, hiding from it; warm light advancing, the figures dark and small in the cold.

[A] And notice who moves first. The two who sinned do not come looking for God. It is God who comes walking into the garden, looking for them. The very first thing he does after the Fall is come searching for the ones who ran from him.
VISUAL: A broad warm shaft of light moving slowly and deliberately between the trees of the dim garden, searching, patient, parting the shadow as it comes; warm gold light advancing gently through deep blue dark.

[A] And a voice called out through the garden. Where are you? It is the first question God asks a fallen human being. Not a question he needed answered — he knew exactly where they were. It was a question meant to make them step out of the dark and face what they had done.
VISUAL: A vast shaft of warm light filling a clearing in the garden, and at its edge two faceless silhouettes half-emerging from shadow, hesitant, drawn toward the light but flinching from it; warm gold radiance, deep blue shadow, the figures caught at the border.

[A] And the man answered. I heard you, he said, and I was afraid, because I was naked, so I hid. And God asked him the question that exposes everything. Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree I commanded you not to touch?
VISUAL: A single faceless silhouette standing at the edge of the great light with head bowed and shoulders low, half-turned away, the warm radiance falling full upon his dark bent form; gold light, long shadow stretching back into the dark behind him.

[A] And here the world breaks a second time. Because the man does not say yes. He does not say sorry. He says — the woman. The woman you put here with me, she gave me the fruit. In one sentence he blames his wife, and behind her, he blames God himself for making her.
VISUAL: Two faceless silhouettes in the light, one turned slightly away from the other with a hand half-lifted toward her as if pointing, the second figure lower and turned aside; cold gold light, the space between them suddenly wide and divided.

[A] And the woman, asked in turn, points past herself to the serpent. The serpent deceived me, she says, and I ate. The blame travels down the line like something falling. No one says the two words that might have begun to heal it. I did.

[A] The shame was bad enough. But this is the deeper wound. In a single afternoon, the first family has learned to hide from God, to fear him, and to turn on one another. Paradise was not only a place. It was a way of being together. And that, too, was already gone.

## PART FIVE — The Curse and the Promise

[A] And so God speaks the words that would shape the world we still live in. He turns first to the serpent. Cursed are you above all creatures, he says. On your belly you will crawl, and dust you will eat, all the days of your life.

[A] But in the middle of the curse on the serpent, God says something Christians have read ever since as the first promise of rescue. I will put enmity, he says, between your offspring and the woman's. He will crush your head — and you will strike his heel.

[A] Hidden inside the judgment is a prophecy. One day, a child of the woman will come. The serpent will wound him — strike his heel. But that child will crush the serpent's head. From the first hour of the Fall, a rescuer is already promised. The story is not over.

[A] It is the smallest seed of hope, planted in the hardest ground. The first humans are standing in the wreckage of everything they had — and God's first move is not only to judge them, but to promise them. Before they have even left the garden, the rescue is already set in motion.

[A] And yet even her pain carries the promise. For it is through a woman bringing a child into the world — the very thing now marked by suffering — that the rescuer himself would one day come. The curse and the cure would run through the same door.

[A] And then he turns to the man. Because you listened, God says, the ground itself is cursed for your sake. You will eat from it only through hard labour, all your days. It will give you thorns and thistles. By the sweat of your face you will eat your bread.

## CLOSE — The Flaming Sword

[A] And then God does something that is easy to rush past, but it may be the most moving thing in the whole story. Before he sends them out, he makes them clothes. Their fig leaves were not enough. So God himself makes garments of skin, and covers them.

[A] And to make a covering of skin, something had to die. Just out of the text's sight, an animal was killed — the first death in the world — so the two who had earned death could be covered. The first blood was not spilled in punishment. It was spilled to clothe them.

[A] And then they are sent out — not in rage, but in sorrow, and in mercy. For now that the man knows good and evil, he must not also reach out and take from the Tree of Life, and live forever in this broken state. Their exile is also a protection.
