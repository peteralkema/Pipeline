## PART TWO — The Challenge

[A] When Ahab saw Elijah, he spat the words at him. Is that you, you troubler of Israel? As if the drought were Elijah's fault. But Elijah threw it back. I have not troubled Israel, he said. You have — you and your father's house, by abandoning God and chasing after Baal.

## PART FOUR — The Fire Falls

[A] And as evening came, Elijah said to the people: come here to me. And they gathered close. And the first thing he did was rebuild the altar of the Lord, which had been torn down — taking twelve stones, one for each tribe of Israel, and setting them in place.

[A] Those twelve stones were a quiet sermon in themselves. One for each of the twelve tribes — for a nation that had fractured and forgotten itself. By building the altar that way, Elijah was reminding them of who they were. One people. One covenant. One God they had all, together, walked away from.

[A] Then he laid the wood, and the offering upon it. And then he did something that made no sense — unless you wanted to remove every possible doubt. He had them dig a trench around the altar, and said: fill four great jars with water, and pour it over the offering and the wood.

[A] Do it again, he said. And they did. Do it a third time. And they did. Until the offering was drenched, the wood soaked, and the water ran down and filled the trench. Elijah had made it impossible. No hidden spark could light a thing that wet. Whatever happened next would be undeniable.

[A] Stop and feel the risk of it. If the fire did not fall, he had not only failed — he had made his failure spectacular, soaking the altar so nothing could ever burn. He had cut off his own escape. He had bet his life, and his God’s name, on fire falling on wet stone.

## PART FIVE — The Rain Returns

## CLOSE — The God Who Answers

[A] So in a single day, everything had turned. The prophets of Baal, silenced. The nation, on its face, crying out to the true God. The drought, broken in a flood of rain. One man had walked out of hiding to face a kingdom — and his God had answered, in fire and in water both.

[A] And the heart of it is that one piercing question Elijah asked at the start. How long will you limp between two opinions? It was never about Baal. It was about a people who wanted one foot in heaven and one in the world. And the fire on Carmel forced them, that day, to choose.

[A] There is something bracing about Elijah. He did not hedge. He did not water down the test to make it safer. He drenched the altar, to make the miracle harder, because he was certain of his God. And his certainty was answered. The boldest faith leaves God no small way out.

[A] And notice how it ended — not in fire, but in rain. The fire proved who God was. The rain showed what he is like. A God who answers the wandering with mercy, who breaks the long drought, who pours life back onto a land that had forgotten him. Judgment, and then grace.

[A] And maybe that is the question the fire on Carmel still puts to anyone who hears it. Not whether you believe God exists. But whether you will stop limping between two opinions — stop trying to keep one foot in heaven and one in the world — and actually, finally, choose.
