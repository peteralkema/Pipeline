Twelve gates. No more, no less. The oldest book is precise about it. He counted twelve gates cut into the face of the moon, and measured each. Six on the east, he says, where the light of the sun goes in. Six on the west, facing them, where that same light comes back out. He gave the exact width of every gate, like a man reading a dial. And he named the beings who stand at each one and work it. Then he wrote how much of the moon is lit, night by counted night — one measured part on the first night, he says, and more on the second, and the third, and the seventh, and the eleventh, each written as a fraction — and the fourteenth, when the whole face is full. Nobody does that to a vision. Nobody measures a dream in parts. You do that to a machine. You do it when the thing can be read. Three thousand years ago, a man walked out of the ordinary world, and the book says he was taken up and up, past the weather, past the stars, past anything anybody on the ground had ever been shown — up to the place where the machinery of the sky is kept. And what he found there was not heaven at all. No throne. No choir. It was machinery. Stone and light, running on rules, exactly as he describes it. You almost certainly know the famous part of this book, even unopened — two hundred of them came down out of the sky onto a mountain top, swore an oath together, took human wives, and broke the world wide open — and that whole story, the one everybody knows, is only the first third. Past it, at chapter seventy-two, the voice quietly changes. The story stops. The story stops. The drama ends, and something much stranger begins. And the text starts counting. Numbers, measures, fractions, page after ruled page. Chapters seventy-two to eighty-two, eleven of them, are the astronomical book — and almost nobody who owns a copy of Enoch has ever read these chapters, because they are not a story at all, and were never meant to be one. They are a specification. A technical manual for the sky, written before Christ. So go outside tonight, anywhere on earth, look up, and find the moon. That is the same moon. The very object he is describing, over your head — the same face turned toward you, the same grey marks all over it, the same slow turning it has kept, without a missed beat, since the start — the same one the Roman sentries watched from the tops of their walls, the same one already ancient, unimaginably old, on the first night the first city anywhere on earth had walls and a name and a king. And somebody, three thousand years ago, sat down with a reed and wrote down how the whole enormous thing works, gate by gate. So there are only two possibilities, and the book leaves you between them. They were either wrong about all of it — or somebody actually told them. ------ Nobody knows who actually wrote this book. Not a name, not a city, nothing. But we know almost exactly when it was written, and that is the strange part. Because the dating does not come from guesswork. It comes from the ground. In nineteen fifty-two, in a cliff absolutely riddled with caves above the Dead Sea, in the dry open desert at a place called Qumran, they found the scrolls — and among them, eleven separate Aramaic manuscripts of the Book of Enoch. Four of those eleven were this book. This one. The astronomical book. The moon. The oldest of the four was copied out by hand, the scholar Milik dated it, to around two hundred and twenty-five years before the birth of Christ. Which means the thing it was copied from is older still. Older than we can see. Older than the Book of Daniel, which sits in every Bible on earth. This is, quite literally, the oldest surviving piece of science that anybody has. And here is the first genuinely strange thing about it, straight from the dig. At Qumran, this part of Enoch did not arrive bound in with the rest. It travelled entirely alone. Its own scroll, copied by its own separate scribe — kept apart from the story of the fallen angels, apart from the great flood, by somebody who plainly believed it was a completely different kind of text. Not scripture. Not a story. Something closer to a set of working tables. And here is the second strange thing, and it points a thousand miles east. The astronomy inside this book is not Jewish in origin at all. It is Babylonian. The twelve gates, the sevenths, the ratios, the rising and setting tables — all of it echoes the cuneiform star-lists, the ones pressed into wet clay tablets, the compendium the Babylonians called MUL.APIN, centuries older than Enoch himself. Somebody carried a Mesopotamian science a thousand miles west across the desert, translated it out of wedge-marks and into Aramaic, and set it down again — and then put the whole of it into the mouth of a Hebrew patriarch. And here is the third strange thing, and it is, honestly, the worst of them. The Aramaic version we dug up is longer than the version everybody reads. The one in every copy of Enoch is the Ethiopic, the Ge'ez translation — the single complete text of this book that survives anywhere in the world. And set beside the older Aramaic, that complete Ethiopic text is an abridgement. James VanderKam, who wrote the standard modern commentary on the whole book, concludes that something drastic happened in the gap between the two versions. Somebody, at some point, cut it. Shortened the sky. Trimmed the working tables down. And nobody now living knows exactly what it was that they cut out, or why. What survives, in the end, is these eleven chapters. Seventy-two through eighty-two. Eleven chapters of pure, patient, ruled arithmetic about the machinery of the sky. It is a fragment of something that was plainly once larger. But it is enough. It is enough to see the shape of the thing. Enough to see the ambition. Because in these eleven chapters, somebody counts the entire sky. ------ Six gates in the east. That is where he starts. Six gates in the west, directly facing them, across the width of the sky. Twelve in all, set in a ring around the rim of the world, and he measured every single one of them by hand, and wrote the widths down. Six on the eastern side, where the light of the sun goes into the machine, six on the far western side, where that same light comes back out again. That is the whole engine. Twelve openings, and a light that moves. And it is not decoration. It is a working description. Because the light never once uses the same gate two mornings running. It rises through one gate, and the next morning it has moved one gate along — and one along, and one along, tracking steadily down the eastern rank, exactly the way real sunrise creeps along your horizon through the year. It runs until it reaches the sixth and final gate, the far end of the row. Then it turns, and it walks all the way back, gate by counted gate, and he wrote every step of that journey, out and back, across the year. Now go and stand outside, on any clear morning, and face the east yourself. Mark the exact spot where the sun's edge first breaks the ground. Mark it with a rock, or a post, or a notch — anything at all that holds still. Come back in a week. The sunrise will have moved along the ridge. Come back in a month, and it will have moved further, in the very same direction. It has moved. Not the sun being restless — the year turning under your feet. Come back a season later and it is standing at the far end of the ridge entirely, and then, over months, it walks the whole way back to where it began. The sun walks that same line every year. It is the oldest calendar there is. But the sun takes a whole year to make that journey down the horizon and back. The moon runs the very same track, gate by gate — but does it in a month. So he gave each gate a width, a number, a share of the days — thirty days apportioned to this gate, thirty-one to the next one along, the lengths of days and nights rising and falling in ruled steps. He wrote the precise fraction by which the daylight had grown, night after night. He says the longest day of the year is exactly twice the length of the shortest, and he sets that ratio down, flatly, in parts, like an engineer. Nobody measures a miracle. You do not put a wonder into fractions and tables. And beside every great gate, he counts smaller windows — hundreds upon hundreds, too many to number, he admits, sunk in ranks across the rock — and out of those small windows, he says, at night, the stars themselves come. The whole sky, in his telling, is one wall with openings cut in it. Twelve great gates for the two large lights. Countless small ones for the stars. And he has not yet said one word about what the light actually is, or who it is, exactly, that stands up there and pours it through the gates. ------ The light is not the moon's own, he says. It is borrowed. He repeats this, over and over, as if he needs you not to miss it. It is given to her in exact measure, he says — never in a flood. Poured in, never poured out, and always, always by sevenths. That number again. In through the six gates on the eastern side the light arrives, portioned out, and that measured pouring, he says, is the entire reason the gates are there. And when the light is not inside her, when no seventh remains, when the last measured part has drained back out through the western gates — he says the whole moon stands completely dark. A shell. Empty, without light, he writes, except for one single seventh part of it — and there is that number yet again. Seven. The sevenths build the whole face. And when she is full, when the entire disc is burning edge to edge, her light, he says, is exactly one seventh of the light of the sun. He does not say she is dimmer, or fainter, as a poet would. He gives you the exact fraction. One seventh. A measured, checkable quantity. And then he says the single strangest thing in the entire astronomical book. The sun and the moon, he states plainly, are exactly the same size in the sky. Up there, side by side, he says, the two great lights are the very same width. One burns seven full times brighter than the other, by his count — and yet the two are, to the eye, exactly the same size. That is not a thing anybody just guesses at, or stumbles into by accident. And every so often, on an ordinary afternoon, with no warning at all, one of the two slides silently across the bright face of the other — and covers it over exactly, edge for edge, nothing left over. It is the plainest proof he could have named that the two discs match. So: the light comes into the moon, through the gates, from the eastern side. But never, he insists, all of it at once. Never the whole in a single night. It arrives in sevenths, one measured part at a time, added night by night — half a seventh laid on the first night, another half on the second night, and on, for fourteen straight nights, the lit part widening by rule, until at last the whole enormous face of the moon is burning at once. Then the light leaves again the same way, in reverse, back out through the west. And he gives the exact fraction gained or lost for every night. It is not a poem about the moon. It is a table. It is a set of tables. Somebody is measuring the sky out by hand, night after night, and writing every measured portion of it down, in ruled and ordered columns. And something, somewhere, has to carry all that light across the whole sky — because none of it, he is certain, ever moves one inch by itself. There is a mechanism up here, and there is a driver standing behind it, and he has not named either one. Not the machine, not the hand. ------ So he tells you what carries the light across the sky. He is specific. And this is the point where the whole thing gets genuinely difficult to read. He says the moon rides in a chariot. That is his exact word. He uses that same word twice in the book — once for the moon, once for the sun. And a chariot is not mysterious. It is a thing built to be driven. So then he tells you, quite directly, exactly what it is that drives it. Not a hand reaching down. Not an arm out of the clouds. Nothing like that. The wind. He says the wind drives the whole sky. The plain, ordinary wind. The same word you would use for the wind on a ridge tonight. And where does that wind come from? He has an answer, and it is chapter seventy-six. At the ends of the earth, he says, are twelve more great gates — three in the east, three in the west, three to the north, three to the south, and out of them the winds of the world come pouring across the land. Twelve gates for the light. Twelve gates for the wind that moves the light. Three thousand years ago, somebody wrote that the sky runs on weather. That the great lights are carried, like everything else, on nothing but moving air. And he says the machine never once stops, never rests, never pauses in its work. It goes down in the west every single evening, without fail, through the gates — and he tells you exactly where it goes after that, in the dark, out of sight. All the way round, he says. Behind the far north of the world. Behind everything, where no one on the ground has ever seen. And he says it is running out there right now, tonight, on the far side of things, on the far side of everything you can look at, coming round to rise. And then he stops describing the machine, and he starts naming the ones who run it. Because a machine this large, in his telling, is not left to run itself. Four great leaders stand at the very top of the whole arrangement. Four names. One for each quarter of the turning year. He lists them in order. Melkejal. Helemmelek. Meleyal. Narel. He sets each name down, precisely, in its place. Then twelve more leaders underneath those four — one for every month of the year. And beneath those twelve, more still — heads set over thousands. One for every single day, he says, of the whole three-hundred-and-sixty-day year. He gives you the entire roster of them, rank under rank, in careful order. Three hundred and sixty days, each with a name assigned, each with an overseer. And then four more days on top of that, he insists, that nobody ever counts. Every rank has a name and a number. Nothing is left unassigned. It is the most relentlessly organised thing that anybody has ever imagined. A sky run like an institution, with a chain of command to the day. Nothing drifts. Nothing is free. Every light has a keeper, and every keeper a name. And every last part of the machine has somebody in charge of it. So who, exactly, is it that is standing next to him, telling him all of this? ------ Somebody is standing there telling him all of this. That stays with me. That is the part of the book I keep coming back to. The presence. Because nobody works out the width of a gate by squinting at the sky. Nobody derives a table of fractions by standing on a hill and hoping. Somebody has to stand there and say the numbers, in order, out loud. And the book tells you, without any drama at all, exactly who that somebody is. His name is Uriel. He is named on the very first line of the whole section. Before the gates, before the light, before a single measurement — Uriel first. The book says the Lord of glory set him over every light in the sky. Not a messenger. Not a passing visitor. The keeper. The one who runs the machine. And here is the genuinely strange part, given the book he appears in. He never once tells you what Uriel looks like. Not a wing. Not a face. Nothing. In a book where two hundred came down onto a mountain in plain sight, where the fallen ones are described in vivid, physical, unmistakable detail — this one, the keeper of the entire sky, is given no body at all. No shape. What he gives you instead is something much smaller, and honestly much stranger. He says simply: he was with me. He showed me. Over and over. He showed me. As if the two of them walked the machine together, side by side. And what Uriel showed him, in chapter seventy-seven, was not only the moon. He was shown seven mountains, higher than any mountain on earth, with a throne of stone standing, empty, on the summit of the very tallest one. He was shown seven rivers, each wider than any river anybody knew, running down across a plain to a bright sea, with seven great islands in it. The keeper showed him the architecture of the world, not only the sky — the mountains, the rivers, the ends of the earth, the chambers of the winds. I wrote it down, Enoch says, exactly as he showed it to me. Exactly as shown. Somebody stood next to him and pointed, patiently, at the whole of creation, and named its parts, slowly enough that an old man could write it down. And this same keeper, the book says, the one who counts out the light each night, is also given charge of the pit at the very bottom of the whole world — the pit where the two hundred fallen ones are chained, waiting. The one who tends the moon also holds the door on the things that ended the world. The same hands. The same keeper. The gentle arithmetic and the locked abyss. And when he has shown Enoch every last part of it, top to bottom, the keeper turns, at the very end, and says one more quiet thing to him. One line, almost thrown away, right at the close of the whole astronomical book. After the gates and the sevenths and the winds and the roster of the days — after the mountains and the rivers and the pit at the world's bottom — he turns to the old man with the reed and the page, and he simply says: Behold, Enoch. I have shown you everything. And that is where it ends. ------ Three hundred and sixty-four. That is his number for the days of the year. That is his number, and he loves it. He returns to it again and again. He states it four separate times, just to be certain of it. Three hundred and sixty counted days, and four more that nobody adds in — and he is genuinely, openly angry at the people who leave those four days out. The year is exact, he insists. Three hundred and sixty-four. He is certain. It divides perfectly. Fifty-two weeks. Four seasons. A clean machine. And he is wrong. Quietly, fatally, provably wrong, and we can measure exactly how. Because the real year is not three hundred and sixty-four days long at all. It is a little over three hundred and sixty-five. A day and a quarter longer. Which does not sound like anything. A day and a quarter. A rounding error. But it never comes back. It is lost every year, and it accumulates, silently. Four years on, and his calendar is five days out of step with the sky. Twenty-five years on, and it is a whole month adrift. The seasons have slipped. Your spring feast now lands, absurdly, in the middle of the winter rain. And then, right here, at chapter eighty, the book does something very odd indeed. Uriel, somehow, is suddenly talking again — after the book already ended his speech. He has only just finished saying he showed Enoch everything there was to see, and now, out of nowhere, he says the years of the world will be cut short. He says the seed will be late going into the ground, and slow to come up. He says the moon herself will alter her fixed order and not appear on time. He says the stars will change their courses, and drift from their appointed paths, and that men will look up, mistake the wandering stars for gods, and worship. Eleven whole chapters of calm, careful, confident arithmetic about a perfect sky — and then, suddenly, one single strange page insisting the whole thing will fail. So try this. Take chapter eighty out of the book entirely. Just lift it out. Lift out that one odd page about the years shortening and the sky going wrong, and the sentence before it and the sentence after join up perfectly — join straight together as if nothing had ever been sitting there between them. Both of them, before and after, begin with the very same run of words. It is the classic signature of an insertion. A join. A seam in the older text. The scholars who painstakingly reassembled the old Aramaic came to a conclusion. Chapter eighty, they argue, was written later and slid into the book — some time after the beautiful, confident numbers had quietly started to slip. After somebody had gone outside, year upon year, and marked the drifting sunrise, and watched the perfect machine of the calendar drift out of true. So somebody wrote a warning, and put it into the mouth of the keeper himself — and made the angel who runs the sky the one to announce that the sky will fail. The keeper of the perfect machine, confessing, in a later hand, that it breaks. It is the most human thing in the book: the correction, hidden as prophecy. ------ Now for the honest part. The part where I have to hedge everything. The gates are not physically on the moon. He never says they are. He says they are down on the horizon — six in the east, six in the west. The wall of cut stone, the ranked openings, the machinery — that is my picture. It is a picture I built, to help you see it. Mine, not his. Everything you have watched for twenty minutes is my reconstruction. And the four angels' names? Those are, honestly, partly a spelling choice. The Ethiopic and the older Aramaic do not agree on how to render them. Four different scholars will give you four different spellings of the same name. Nobody now alive actually knows how those names were meant to be pronounced. So strip all of that away. Take my pictures away. Take the reconstructions away. Take away the cut stone and the shafts of light and the angel with no face. And what is left, underneath all of it, is still a table. Still hard arithmetic. Six gates in the east and six in the west, indexed carefully against the months. The moon's light poured in by exact sevenths, one measured part at a time. One seventh of the sun's light, and the same apparent size in the sky. The whole sky driven on nothing but wind, from the gates at the world's ends. Three hundred and sixty days, each named, and four more that nobody counts. All of it written down, in Aramaic, before the birth of Christ. All real. Nobody needed my pictures to make any of that genuinely, permanently strange. And all of that is only eleven chapters of it. Only the astronomical book. The whole Book of Enoch runs to a hundred and eight chapters in all. And there is one more place in it, near the end, I have to show you. Another mountain entirely. This one out in the west, at the edge of things. Four hollow places, he says, cut deep into the hard grey rock of it. Deep, and wide, and very smooth. That is his phrase for them. Smooth places. Four again. There is that number one more time, at the bottom of the world. And those four hollow places, he says, are where the dead are kept, and wait. The souls of everyone who has ever died, held in four chambers in the rock. And one of those four chambers, he says, is not quiet. One of them has a sound. Abel is in it, the first murdered man — and he is still shouting. Still crying out against his brother, from a hollow place at the end of the world. But that is the very next film, and another night, and a different kind of dark. Tonight, just go outside. Anywhere on the whole earth at all. And look up. He was not shown a wonder, up on that escarpment. Not a vision. He was shown a machine — and, standing beside it, the ones who run the machine. And he wrote every measured part of it down, night by counted night, so that three thousand years later, on an ordinary night, in your own garden, you could stand there, and look up at the moon, and do the one thing he did. You could check his arithmetic for yourself.
