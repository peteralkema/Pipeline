## PART ONE — The Outcry and the Bargain

[A] It begins not with fire, but with a visit. Three travellers came to the tent of an old man named Abraham — and one of them, the text quietly reveals, was the Lord himself. Abraham welcomed them, fed them, and they brought him news that he and his wife would finally have a son.

## PART FOUR — The Rescue

[A] So Lot ran through the night to the young men engaged to his daughters, and begged them to flee. Get up, he pleaded. Get out. The Lord is about to destroy this place. But to them, the old text says, he seemed like a man making a joke. And they would not come.

[A] Two of his daughters would walk out alive. Two more, and the men they were to marry, would not — because they could not believe a warning that sounded too large to be true. The line between the saved and the lost ran straight through one family, drawn by who believed.

[A] It is one of the saddest lines in the story. The warning was real. The door was still open. And the people who could have walked through it simply laughed, and rolled over, and went back to sleep — because the danger was too large, and the night too ordinary, to believe.

## CLOSE — Remember Lot's Wife

[A] Centuries later, the old text tells us, a teacher would point back to this one moment with just three words. Remember Lot's wife. Not the fire. Not the cities. Not the mob. Of all the images in this terrible story, the one we are told to carry is the woman who looked back.

[A] He said it while warning people about the end of the age — a day when judgment would come suddenly on a world going about its business, eating and drinking and building, exactly as Sodom had. And in the middle of that warning, he reached back across the centuries for one image. Remember Lot’s wife.

[A] Why did she look back? The old readers always said the same thing. She looked back because her heart was still there. Her feet were fleeing the city, but everything she loved was still inside it. And you cannot be pulled to safety while you are still turning toward the thing being destroyed.

[A] That is the warning that has lasted three thousand years. Not simply — do not disobey. But something deeper. Do not love the burning world so much that you cannot leave it. Do not let your heart stay behind in the very thing you are being saved from.
