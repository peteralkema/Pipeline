## ACT 1 — THE FOOL AND THE WISE WOMAN

[A] At this point in his life, David was not yet king. He had been anointed in secret, but the throne still belonged to Saul — and Saul, mad with jealousy, was hunting David to kill him. [A] In that region lived a very rich man named Nabal. He owned three thousand sheep and a thousand goats, and his wealth was enormous. But the Book introduces him with a brutal pair of descriptions. Nabal was surly and mean in his dealings. And his name — Nabal — actually means fool. He was a wealthy fool, harsh and badtempered and proud.

## ACT 2 — THE OATH OF VENGEANCE

[A] The ten men returned to David and reported every word. And David's reaction was instant, and terrifying. Put on your swords, he ordered. And his men strapped on their swords, and David strapped on his. About four hundred men set out with him — leaving two hundred to guard the supplies — riding to wipe Nabal and his entire household off the earth.

[A] And as he rode, David swore a brutal oath. May God deal with me, ever so severely, he vowed, if by morning I leave alive even one male of all who belong to Nabal. He was not riding out to teach Nabal a lesson. He was riding out to commit a massacre — to kill every man and boy in the household, over an insult and a refused meal.

[A] And here we have to be honest about David. This is the same David who would become Israel's greatest king, the man after God's own heart. And in this moment, he is wrong. David needed to be stopped. And no man in his army was going to stop him.

## ACT 3 — THE WOMAN WHO RODE OUT TO MEET AN ARMY

[A] And Abigail wasted not one moment. She moved with breathtaking speed and decisiveness. She gathered a massive gift — two hundred loaves of bread, two skins of wine, five dressed sheep, roasted grain, a hundred cakes of raisins, two hundred cakes of pressed figs — and loaded it all onto donkeys.
VISUAL: Servants swiftly loading donkeys with bread, wine, dressed sheep, and baskets of figs and raisins. Faceless, the urgent preparation.

[A] She sent her servants on ahead with the gift, and told them — I will come behind you. But she did not tell her husband Nabal a single word of it. There was no time, and no point. She simply acted, on her own authority and her own wisdom, to undo the catastrophe her husband had set in motion. Then she mounted her own donkey and rode out toward the oncoming army.

[A] The moment Abigail saw David, she got down off her donkey, and bowed down before him with her face to the ground. And there, in the dust, in the path of an army, she began to speak. And what she said is one of the great speeches in all of scripture — a masterpiece of courage, humility, and wisdom delivered under the shadow of the sword.

[A] Let the blame fall on me alone, my lord, she said. Please pay no attention to that wicked man Nabal, she went on. He is just like his name — his name means fool, and folly goes with him. I, your servant, did not even see the men you sent.

[A] And then Abigail did something brilliant. She did not just beg for mercy. She appealed to David's own future — to the king he was going to be, and the conscience he would have to carry into that future. The Lord, she said, will certainly make a lasting dynasty for my lord, because you fight the Lord's battles. You are destined for the throne. So do not do this thing.

[A] Do not let this be a staggering burden on your conscience, she pleaded — the burden of needless bloodshed, of having avenged yourself with your own hands. When the Lord has done for you every good thing he has promised, and has made you ruler over Israel — you will not want this massacre on your soul. Do not let a fool like Nabal make you a murderer.

[A] Leave the matter to God, was her message. Let the Lord handle Nabal. Do not stain the hands that are meant to hold a kingdom. It was wise, it was bold, and it was spoken to an armed and furious man who could have killed her where she knelt. And it landed.

## CLOSE — THE HAND THAT WAS STAYED

[A] David looked down at this woman who had ridden out alone to stop him, and something in him broke open. The rage drained away. And he spoke words of profound gratitude — to her, and to the God he suddenly realised had sent her.

[A] Praise be to the Lord, the God of Israel, David said, who has sent you this day to meet me. And blessed are you for your good judgment, and for keeping me from bloodshed this day, and from avenging myself with my own hands. He understood, all at once, what he had nearly done — and that this woman had saved him from it.

[A] If you had not come quickly to meet me, David admitted, not one male belonging to Nabal would have been left alive by morning. He accepted her gift, and said: go home in peace. I have heard your words, and I grant your request. The army turned around. The massacre was called off. Four hundred swords went back into their sheaths because one woman had the courage and the wisdom to ride out and speak.

[A] Think about what Abigail actually accomplished in those few minutes. She saved her entire household from slaughter. But she also saved David — the future king — from a crime that would have haunted his reign and stained his conscience forever. She protected the innocent and she protected the guilty man who was about to become a murderer. Her wisdom reached in both directions at once.

[A] Abigail rode home. And there she found her husband Nabal holding a banquet like a king, feasting and very, very drunk — completely unaware that his wife had just saved his life and the lives of every man in his house. So she said nothing until morning. And in the morning, when he was sober, she told him everything.

[A] And when Abigail told Nabal how close he had come to death — how his own folly had nearly destroyed them all — the Book says his heart failed him, and he became like a stone. The shock of it struck him down. And about ten days later, the Lord struck Nabal, and he died. The fool's own folly, in the end, was his undoing — and God dealt with him, exactly as Abigail had said to leave him to God.

[A] When David heard that Nabal was dead, he praised God again — this time for upholding his cause against Nabal, and for keeping him, David, from doing wrong with his own hands. God had handled it. [A] And Abigail — the wise woman who had ridden out alone to stop an army — became the wife of the man who would be king. [A] Abigail's story is short, tucked into the middle of David's long saga. But it holds one of the most important lessons in all of scripture — about the power of the right words, spoken with courage, at the right moment, to turn a heart away from disaster.

[A] And it holds a lesson about vengeance. David, the great hero, was about to ruin himself by taking justice into his own hands over a wounded pride. And Abigail's wisdom was simply this: leave it to God. Do not stain your own hands. Do not let a fool turn you into something you will regret.
