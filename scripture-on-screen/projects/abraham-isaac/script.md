## ACT 2 — THE COMMAND

[A] Isaac grew into a boy. And then, one day, God came to Abraham again. And the Book introduces what follows with three quiet, devastating words: God tested Abraham.

[A] God called his name. Abraham. And Abraham answered, as he always did: here I am. And then God spoke the most terrible sentence a parent has ever heard.

[A] Take your son, God said. Your only son, Isaac, whom you love. Go to the region of Moriah, and sacrifice him there as a burnt offering on a mountain I will show you.

[A] Listen to how the command is worded, because every word turns the knife. Your son. Your only son. Isaac. The one whom you love. God names, one by one, everything that makes the request unbearable — as if to say, I know exactly what I am asking. I know exactly what he is to you.

[A] Everything in this command cuts against everything Abraham knew. It cut against his love for his son — the deepest love of his life. It cut against the promise itself, because the whole future ran through Isaac; to lose Isaac was to lose everything God had sworn. [A] By every measure, the command made no sense. It contradicted God's own promise. It demanded the one thing Abraham could not give. A lesser faith would have argued, or bargained, or simply refused. What Abraham did instead is the heart of the whole story.

[A] He obeyed. The Book records it with a stillness that is almost unbearable. Early the next morning, Abraham got up and saddled his donkey. He cut the wood for the burnt offering. He took two of his servants and his son Isaac. And he set out for the place God had told him about.

## ACT 3 — THE THREE-DAY JOURNEY

[A] The mountain was three days away. Three days. Sit with that. This was not a moment of madness, a single rash act in the heat of feeling. It was three days of walking, three days of nights, three days in which Abraham looked at his living son across the campfire, knowing what he believed he had to do.
VISUAL: The small party walking a long road through wilderness hills, the boy beside the old man. Faceless, the long road.

[A] Every step was a fresh choice to keep obeying. Every meal shared, every night Isaac slept safe and trusting nearby, every conversation on the road — all of it under the shadow of the mountain ahead. Three days is not a test of impulse. It is a test of resolve, sustained past the point where any feeling could carry it. Only faith could.
VISUAL: A campfire at night, the old father watching his sleeping son across the flames. Faceless, the vigil.

[A] Think of what those nights must have held. A father lying awake, listening to his son breathe in the dark, turning the command over and over, searching for another meaning and finding none. The chance to turn back was there every single hour. The donkey could have been steered home. No one would have known. And every hour, Abraham chose the mountain again.
VISUAL: The old man lying awake by the dying fire, his sleeping son nearby, the dark wilderness around them. Faceless, the sleepless choice.

[A] We are so used to this story that we can forget how monstrous the command sounds, and how easily a lesser man could have explained it away. Surely God did not really mean it. Surely a loving God could not want this. Abraham had every reason available to talk himself out of obeying. He used none of them. He simply kept walking.

[A] On the third day, Abraham looked up and saw the place in the distance — the mountain God had named. And he turned to his two servants and said something extraordinary. Stay here with the donkey, he told them. The boy and I will go over there. We will worship — and then we will come back to you.

[A] We will come back. Both of us. Whatever Abraham believed was about to happen on that mountain, he also believed — somehow — that he and Isaac would walk back down it together. ## CLOSE — THE LORD WILL PROVIDE

[A] Abraham! Abraham! The voice of the angel of the Lord called out from heaven, urgent, twice. And Abraham answered, as he always had: here I am.
VISUAL: The old man's head turning toward the breaking light, the knife arrested in the air. Faceless, the turn.

[A] Do not lay a hand on the boy, the voice said. Do not do anything to him. Now I know that you fear God, because you have not withheld from me your son, your only son.
VISUAL: A shaft of gold light breaking fully across the mountaintop onto the altar and the two figures. Symbolic, the rescue.

[A] The test was complete. It had never been about Isaac's death — God had no intention of taking the boy. It was about Abraham's heart. Would he hold back even this, his dearest treasure, from the God he trusted? And the answer, proven through three days and a raised knife, was no. He would withhold nothing.

[A] And then Abraham looked up — and there, caught by its horns in a thicket, was a ram. A male sheep, provided at the exact moment of need, in the exact place of need. Abraham went over, took the ram, and offered it as a burnt offering instead of his son.

[A] Where is the lamb? Isaac had asked on the way up. God himself will provide, Abraham had answered. And now, on the mountaintop, the answer stood caught in the thicket. God had provided. The substitute had come. The son went free.

[A] So Abraham named that place The Lord Will Provide. And to this day, the Book says, it is said: on the mountain of the Lord, it will be provided. The name was a memorial — that at the very edge of the unbearable, when there was no way through, God himself provided the way.

[A] Father and son walked back down the mountain together — both of them, exactly as Abraham had told the servants they would. Isaac alive. The promise intact. The hardest test in the history of faith, passed.

[A] For thousands of years, people have wrestled with this story — and they should. It is meant to be wrestled with. It is not a comfortable tale. But at its heart it is not about a God who delights in a father's pain. It is about a God who, at the last second, stops the knife and provides the lamb himself.

[A] Abraham's faith was not that the command made sense — it did not. It was that the God who gave the command was good, and kept his promises, and could be trusted even into the dark, even up the mountain, even with the knife in his hand. He trusted that somehow, God would provide. And on the mountain of the Lord, it was provided.

[A] And there is a shadow here that the faithful have seen for centuries — a father, an only son who loved, who carried the wood for his own offering up a hill, and a substitute provided in his place. The mountain of Moriah, tradition holds, looks out toward another hill, where another only Son would one day carry his own wood up a slope, and where no voice would stop it — because that time, the Son was the lamb God provided.

[A] That is why this story has never been only about Abraham. It is about a pattern woven into the heart of the whole Bible — that at the place of sacrifice, God provides the offering himself. Isaac asked, where is the lamb? And the answer that began on that mountain echoes through the entire story that follows: God himself will provide.
