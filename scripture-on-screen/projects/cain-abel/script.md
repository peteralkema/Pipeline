## ACT 1 — TWO BROTHERS, TWO ALTARS

[A] After Adam and Eve were sent out from the garden of Eden, they began the human story the hard way. Once, they had walked with God in paradise, in a world without death or pain or toil. Then came the fall — the one forbidden tree, the choice to disobey — and everything changed.

[A] Now they lived east of Eden, by the sweat of their brow, in a world marked by struggle and thorns and the long shadow of death that their choice had let in. This is the world their children would be born into — not paradise, but a place already broken.

[A] And in time, Eve gave birth to her first child. With the help of the Lord, she said, I have brought forth a man — and you can hear the hope in it. Perhaps this was the promised one, the child who would set right what they had broken. She named him Cain.

[A] They grew, and they took up different work. Abel became a keeper of flocks — a shepherd, tending sheep across the open hills. Cain worked the ground — a farmer, coaxing crops from the stubborn earth their father had been cursed to till.

[A] And the Lord looked with favour on Abel and his offering. But on Cain and his offering, he did not look with favour. One was received. The other was not. And the Book does not spell out every reason — but it drops a clear hint in how it describes each gift.

[A] Abel brought the firstborn, the fat portions, the best. Cain brought simply some of his crops. One gave God his first and his finest. The other gave God whatever was to hand. The offerings revealed the hearts behind them. And God, who sees the heart, knew the difference.

[A] This is the first thing the story is quietly teaching. God does not weigh the size of a gift. He weighs the heart that gives it. Abel's offering said: God comes first, and gets my best. Cain's offering said: God gets what is left over, once I have taken what I want.
VISUAL: The two altars from above, one's smoke rising clean and high, the other's drifting low and grey. Symbolic.

[A] And worship, it turns out, was never really about the lamb or the grain at all. It was about what the offering revealed — whether a person gave God the throne of their heart, or only a corner of it. That is still the question every act of worship asks. Cain's grain was fine. It was Cain's heart that was the problem.

## ACT 2 — SIN CROUCHING AT THE DOOR

[A] When Cain saw that his offering was not accepted, he was furious. The Book says his face fell — his expression darkened, his whole bearing curdled with anger and resentment. And in that moment, God did something merciful. He came to Cain. He reached out to him, before anything terrible had happened.
VISUAL: A man with a darkened, downcast face standing by his cold altar, fists clenched. Faceless, the anger.

[A] Why are you angry? God asked him. Why has your face fallen? If you do what is right, will you not be accepted? The door was still open. The offering could be made again, rightly. Nothing was ruined yet. God was offering Cain a way forward.
VISUAL: A shaft of light reaching toward the angry man at his altar, an invitation in it. Symbolic.

[A] And then God gave him a warning — one of the most vivid and important sentences in all of scripture. But if you do not do what is right, God said, sin is crouching at your door. It desires to have you. But you must rule over it.
VISUAL: A dark, predatory shadow gathering low at the threshold of a doorway, crouched and waiting. Symbolic, menace.

[A] Sin is crouching at your door. Picture it — a wild animal, low to the ground, muscles coiled, waiting just outside, wanting to get in and devour you. That is what God told Cain his jealousy was. Not a small thing. A beast at the door. And then the hope in the warning: but you must rule over it. You can master this. You do not have to let it in.
VISUAL: The crouched shadow-beast at the threshold, and a hand on the door, the choice held. Symbolic, tension.

[A] This is the hinge of the whole story, and the reason it still speaks to every one of us. Cain was not doomed. He was warned. He was told exactly what was crouching in his heart, and told plainly that he could master it. The choice was entirely his. He could rule the thing at the door — or open it.
VISUAL: The man standing at the threshold, the shadow-beast crouched before him, the decision suspended. Faceless, the crossroads.

[A] And Cain made his choice. He nursed the jealousy instead of mastering it. He fed the resentment instead of ruling it. He let it grow, and harden, and take him over — until the beast at the door was no longer outside him, but inside.
VISUAL: The man turning away from the light, the shadow drawing closer around him. Symbolic, the surrender.

[A] This is how it always works. The beast at the door does not break in all at once. It is let in slowly, fed a little at a time. A resentment turned over in the mind at night. A comparison that curdles into envy. A grievance rehearsed until it feels like justice. Cain did not become a murderer in the field. He became one over days and weeks of feeding the thing at his door.
VISUAL: A shadow lengthening and thickening around a brooding figure over the passing of day into night. Symbolic, the slow corruption.

[A] By the time he spoke to his brother, the decision was already made deep inside him. The field was just where it surfaced. Murder is what jealousy becomes when it is fed instead of ruled — and Cain had been feeding his for a long time.
VISUAL: The brooding figure fully wreathed in shadow now, the warmth of the earlier light gone. Faceless, the hardened heart.

[A] Now Cain said to his brother Abel — let us go out to the field. Just a few quiet words. An invitation. But there was murder behind them now. He led his own brother away from anyone who might see, out to the empty country, with a plan already formed in his darkened heart.
VISUAL: Two figures walking out toward an empty field at dusk, one a half-step behind the other. Faceless, dread, from behind.

## ACT 3 — THE FIELD

[A] And while they were in the field, Cain attacked his brother Abel, and killed him.
VISUAL: A wide, distant view of the empty field at dusk, the act left entirely unshown, only the vast indifferent landscape. Restrained, faceless, no depiction.

[A] In a single sentence, the Book records the first murder in human history. The first death of any kind. Abel, the shepherd who gave God his best, became the first human being to die — killed by the only other person on earth who shared his mother and father.
VISUAL: A single shepherd's staff lying fallen in the empty field at dusk, its owner gone. Symbolic, restrained.

[A] Somewhere not far off, Adam and Eve were about to become the first parents to lose a child — and to lose him at the hand of their other child. Every grief the human race has ever known about death, about violence, about a family torn apart, began in that one field, on that one evening. All of it was new. All of it started here.
VISUAL: The distant simple dwelling at dusk, lamplit, unaware of what has happened in the field beyond. Faceless, dread.

[A] And here we return to where we began. Cain standing alone in the field. The ground drinking human blood for the first time. And the voice of God, breaking the terrible silence with a question.
VISUAL: The lone standing figure against the blood-red dusk, the empty field, the dread. Backlit silhouette.

[A] Where is your brother Abel? God asked. Now, God knew the answer. The question was not for information. It was an invitation — a last open door — for Cain to confess, to break, to come clean. And Cain slammed it shut.
VISUAL: The standing figure turned toward the unseen voice, jaw set, refusing. Partial, no full face.

[A] I do not know, Cain said. And then he asked the question that has echoed down every century since — cold, defiant, and damning. Am I my brother's keeper?
VISUAL: The shadowed figure spreading his hands in false ignorance, the lie plain in his posture. Faceless, the defiance.

[A] Am I my brother's keeper. It is one of the oldest questions in the human story, and Cain meant it as a way to dodge responsibility — surely I am not responsible for him. But the whole Bible answers back across the centuries: yes. Yes, you are. You always were. We are all, always, our brother's keeper. That is the entire point.
VISUAL: The defiant figure small against the vast darkening sky, the weight of the question pressing down. Reflective, scale.

[A] And God answered Cain's lie not with an argument, but with something that must have chilled his blood. What have you done? God said. Listen — your brother's blood is crying out to me from the ground.
VISUAL: The cracked red earth of the field, a sense of a voice rising up out of it toward heaven. Symbolic, the crying ground.

[A] Your brother's blood cries out from the ground. The very earth that Cain farmed, the soil he had given his life to working, had drunk his brother's blood — and now it testified against him. There is no hiding murder from God. The ground itself remembers. Creation itself cries out.
VISUAL: The wide field under the red sky, the ground itself seeming to accuse. Symbolic, reflective.

## CLOSE — THE WANDERER AND THE MARK

[A] And God pronounced the judgment. Now you are under a curse, driven from the ground that opened its mouth to receive your brother's blood. When you work the soil, it will no longer yield its crops for you. You will be a restless wanderer on the earth.
VISUAL: The cursed man bowed beneath the verdict, the field behind him barren and refusing. Faceless, the sentence.

[A] The farmer who lived by the ground would now be rejected by the ground. The man who killed to keep his place would lose his place entirely, condemned to wander the earth with no home and no rest. The punishment fit the crime with terrible precision.
VISUAL: A lone figure setting out across an endless wilderness, no destination, no home. Faceless, the exile.

[A] And Cain cried out — not in repentance, but in self-pity and fear. My punishment is more than I can bear, he said. I will be hidden from your presence, a restless wanderer, and whoever finds me will kill me. Even now, his thoughts were only for himself.
VISUAL: The man on his knees in the wilderness, hands raised, pleading for himself. Faceless, the self-pity.

[A] And here the story turns in a way no one expects. Because God, even now, showed mercy to the first murderer. Not so, the Lord said. Anyone who kills Cain will suffer vengeance seven times over. And the Lord put a mark on Cain — so that no one who found him would kill him.
VISUAL: A shaft of light placing a sign upon the bowed wanderer, a strange protection. Symbolic, mercy.

[A] The mark of Cain has been misremembered for centuries as a curse. It was the opposite. It was a sign of protection — God's mercy laid even on the man who committed the first murder, sparing his life when justice could have demanded it. Judgment and mercy, woven together, from the very first crime.
VISUAL: The marked wanderer walking on, shielded by the strange sign, into the unknown land. Faceless, protected.

[A] So Cain went out from the presence of the Lord, and lived in a land east of Eden — a land the Book names Nod, which means wandering. And there the first murderer built the first city, and the human story carried on, now marked forever by the knowledge of what one brother could do to another.
VISUAL: A lone figure cresting a ridge toward an unknown land at dusk, a faint settlement rising in the distance. Faceless, the wandering.

[A] Cain and Abel is only a handful of verses, near the very start of the Bible. But almost everything that follows is somewhere inside it. The first jealousy. The first anger nursed instead of mastered. The first violence. The first lie. And the first time a human being looked at God and said, I am not responsible for anyone but myself.
VISUAL: The two altars from the beginning, one smoking, one cold, beneath the darkening sky. Reflective, symbolic.

[A] Notice, too, what the story refuses to do. It does not make Cain a monster, born evil, nothing like us. It makes him painfully ordinary — a man who was disappointed, who compared himself to his brother, who felt overlooked, and who let that feeling run unchecked until it became the worst thing a person can do. His sin began somewhere we have all stood.
VISUAL: The lone figure at the cold altar, ordinary and human, the shadow only beginning to gather. Reflective, faceless.

[A] And notice the mercy threaded through even this — the first and darkest crime. God warned Cain before he sinned. God came to him after, with a question instead of a thunderbolt. And God marked him for protection even in judgment. From the very first murder, the God of the Bible is shown to be both perfectly just and astonishingly merciful.
VISUAL: The marked wanderer small in the wilderness under a sky that holds both shadow and a thread of light. Reflective, symbolic.

[A] But the deepest thing in the story is that warning at the door — because it was true for Cain, and it is true for every one of us. Sin is crouching at your door. The resentment you are nursing, the jealousy you are feeding, the anger you are letting harden — it is crouching right now, low to the ground, wanting in.
VISUAL: The shadow-beast crouched again at a threshold, waiting, in every life. Symbolic, reflective.

[A] God told Cain the same thing he tells us. You can rule over it. You are not doomed to open the door. The beast is real, and it wants you — but the choice of whether to let it in is still yours, right up until the moment you do.
VISUAL: A hand resting on a door, the crouched shadow outside, the choice still open. Symbolic, the crossroads.

[A] So here is the question Cain and Abel leaves with you. There is something crouching at your door right now — you know what it is. A grudge, an envy, a resentment you keep feeding instead of mastering. Will you rule over it? Or will you, like Cain, open the door and let it lead you out to the field?
