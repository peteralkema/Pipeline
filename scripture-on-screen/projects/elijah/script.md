## ACT 1 — THE DROUGHT

[A] Elijah lived in one of the darkest times in Israel's history. The nation was ruled by King Ahab — and the Book says plainly that Ahab did more evil in the eyes of the Lord than any king before him. But the deeper darkness came through his wife: Jezebel, a foreign queen who hated the God of Israel and was determined to wipe out his worship entirely.
VISUAL: A king and a proud foreign queen enthroned together in a richly decorated hall, idols around them. Faceless, the wicked court.

[A] Under Jezebel, Israel abandoned the Lord and bowed to Baal — a Canaanite god of storm, rain, and fertility, believed to bring the rains that made the crops grow. The land filled with altars to Baal, and Jezebel hunted down and killed the prophets of the true God. It seemed the worship of the Lord might be snuffed out altogether. And into that darkness, God sent Elijah.
VISUAL: Altars to a false god rising across the land, the worship of the Lord driven into hiding. Faceless, the apostasy.

[A] Elijah appeared before King Ahab with a single, devastating announcement. As surely as the Lord, the God of Israel, lives — whom I serve — there will be neither dew nor rain in the next few years, except at my word. And then he vanished. No rain. Not a drop. Until a prophet of the true God said so.
VISUAL: A rugged prophet declaring judgment before the enthroned king, then gone. Identity by costume, the pronouncement.

[A] And here is the brilliance of the judgment. Baal was the storm god — the god the people had turned to precisely because they believed he controlled the rain and the harvest. So God shut up the sky. For three and a half years, no rain fell. The storm god could not produce a single drop. The drought was not just a punishment. It was a public exposure of a god who could do nothing.

[A] Through those long years of drought, God hid and provided for Elijah in miraculous ways — fed by ravens beside a brook, then sustained in the home of a starving widow, whose tiny supply of flour and oil simply would not run out, day after day, until the rains returned. Even raised her son from death. God kept his prophet alive while the land withered, preparing him for the showdown to come.
VISUAL: A prophet in the wilderness, ravens bringing food, a widow's jar that never empties. Faceless, the provision.

[A] Finally, in the third year, God told Elijah it was time. Go, present yourself to Ahab, he said, and I will send rain on the land. And so Elijah walked back out of hiding, into the court of the king who had been hunting him for three years, to set in motion the greatest confrontation between the true God and a false one that the Bible records.

## ACT 2 — THE CHALLENGE ON MOUNT CARMEL

[A] When Ahab saw Elijah, he snarled at him. Is that you, you troubler of Israel? And Elijah threw the accusation right back. I have not made trouble for Israel, he answered. You have — you and your father's family — by abandoning the Lord's commands and following the false gods. The real troubler of Israel was not the prophet who spoke truth. It was the king who had led the nation into ruin.

[A] And then Elijah issued his challenge. Summon all Israel to meet me on Mount Carmel, he said — and bring the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, and the four hundred prophets of Asherah who eat at Jezebel's table. One prophet of the Lord, against eight hundred and fifty prophets of the false gods. In front of the whole nation. On a mountaintop. Winner takes the faith of Israel.
VISUAL: A vast crowd and hundreds of pagan prophets assembling on a great mountain, one lone figure facing them. Wide, faceless, scale.

[A] When the whole nation had gathered, Elijah stood before them and asked the question that cut to the heart of everything. How long will you waver between two opinions? he called out. If the Lord is God, follow him. But if Baal is God, follow him. Stop limping between two different gods. Choose.
VISUAL: The lone prophet addressing the immense crowd from a high place, the question ringing out. Faceless, the challenge.

[A] How long will you waver between two opinions. The people had been trying to have it both ways — keeping a little of the Lord, a little of Baal, hedging their bets, refusing to commit to either. And Elijah forced the choice into the open. And the Book records the people's response in three devastating words: but the people said nothing. They could not even answer. They were paralysed between the two.
VISUAL: The vast crowd standing silent and uneasy, unable to answer, caught between two gods. Faceless, the silence.

[A] So Elijah proposed a test that would leave no room for doubt. Get two bulls, he said. Let the prophets of Baal prepare one on their altar, and I will prepare the other on mine. But put no fire to the wood. Then you call on the name of your god, and I will call on the name of the Lord. And the god who answers by fire — he is God. And all the people agreed: what you say is good.

[A] It was the perfect test. Baal was supposedly a god of fire and storm and lightning. If he was real, sending a bolt of fire to his own altar should have been effortless. Elijah even let the prophets of Baal go first, and choose their own bull, and have all day. He gave every possible advantage to the other side. He was that certain of his God.

## ACT 3 — THE GOD WHO ANSWERS BY FIRE

[A] So the prophets of Baal began. They prepared their bull, and called on the name of Baal from morning until noon. Baal, answer us! they cried. But there was no response. No one answered. And they danced around the altar they had made, leaping and pleading, growing more frantic as the hours passed and the sky stayed empty.
VISUAL: Hundreds of pagan prophets dancing and crying out around their altar, growing desperate. Faceless, the frenzy.

[A] At noon, Elijah began to mock them — and his taunts are some of the sharpest humour in the whole Bible. Shout louder! he called. Surely he is a god! Perhaps he is deep in thought, or busy, or travelling. Maybe he is sleeping and must be awakened. Elijah was exposing the absurdity of it — a god who might be napping, or away on a journey, while his desperate followers screamed for him.
VISUAL: The lone prophet calling out his mockery, the frantic prophets redoubling their efforts. Faceless, the taunt.

[A] So they shouted louder. And they did something terrible — they slashed themselves with swords and spears, as was their custom, until their blood flowed, trying to force their god to notice them through their own pain. Midday passed, and they raved on through the afternoon. But the Book records the verdict with merciless simplicity: there was no response, no one answered, no one paid attention.
VISUAL: The pagan prophets in bloody, exhausted frenzy at their altar, the sky utterly empty above. Restrained, faceless, the futility.

[A] No one answered. Hours of screaming, dancing, bleeding, pleading — and nothing. Because there was nothing there to answer. The storm god could not produce a spark. And as the evening drew near, exhausted and defeated, the prophets of Baal fell silent. Then Elijah said to all the people: come here to me. And the crowd gathered close to watch.

[A] Elijah took twelve stones — one for each of the twelve tribes of Israel — and with them, he repaired the altar of the Lord that had been torn down. [A] And then Elijah did something that looked like madness. Fill four large jars with water, he ordered, and pour it on the offering and the wood. They did. Do it again, he said. They did it again. Do it a third time, he said. And they did. The water ran down all around the altar and even filled the trench. The wood was soaked. The sacrifice was drenched. Anything that could burn was now waterlogged.

[A] Why drench an altar you are about to ask God to set on fire? Because Elijah wanted to remove every possible doubt. No hidden ember, no trick, no natural explanation. If fire came now, on a soaked altar swimming in water, everyone would know beyond all argument that it came from God alone. He was not hedging his bet. He was staking everything on God, and making the miracle impossible to fake.

[A] Then, as the time of the evening sacrifice came, Elijah stepped forward and prayed. And notice the contrast with the screaming, bleeding prophets of Baal. No frenzy. No theatrics. Lord, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel — let it be known today that you are God in Israel, and that I am your servant, and that I have done all these things at your command.

[A] Answer me, Lord, answer me, Elijah prayed — so that these people will know that you, Lord, are God, and that you are turning their hearts back again. His prayer was not about winning. It was about the people — that they would see, and know, and come home to God. And the moment he finished speaking, heaven answered.

[A] And here we return to where we began. The fire of the Lord fell from the sky — and it did not just light the wood. [A] When all the people saw it, they fell prostrate and cried out: the Lord — he is God! The Lord — he is God! The wavering was over. The silence was broken. In the face of fire from heaven on a drenched altar, there was no more limping between two opinions. The whole nation, on its face, knew the truth at last.

## ACT 4 — THE WHISPER

[A] The false prophets were seized and the rule of Baal on that mountain was broken. And then Elijah told Ahab that the long drought was about to end. He climbed to the top of Carmel, bent down to the ground, and prayed for rain. He sent his servant to look toward the sea. Nothing, the servant reported. Go back, Elijah said. Seven times he sent him.
VISUAL: The prophet bowed in prayer on the mountaintop, his servant scanning the empty sea. Faceless, the waiting.

[A] On the seventh time, the servant said: a cloud as small as a man's hand is rising from the sea. That was all Elijah needed. A tiny cloud, a handsbreadth on the horizon. Soon the sky grew black with clouds and wind, and a heavy rain began to fall. After three and a half years, the drought broke. The God who sent the fire now sent the rain — proving that he, not Baal, had always held the storm.
VISUAL: A small cloud growing into black storm clouds, the first heavy rain falling on the parched land. Spectacle, faceless.

[A] You would think that after fire from heaven and the breaking of the drought, Elijah would be unstoppable. Instead, something strange and deeply human happened. When Queen Jezebel heard that her prophets were dead, she swore to kill Elijah by the next day. And this same prophet who had faced down eight hundred and fifty men alone — ran. Terrified, exhausted, and suddenly despairing, he fled into the wilderness.
VISUAL: The prophet fleeing alone across a darkening wilderness, fear in his flight. Faceless, the collapse.

[A] He sat down under a solitary tree and prayed that he might die. I have had enough, Lord, he said. Take my life. The man on the mountaintop was now a broken man in the desert, wanting to die. It is one of the most honest portraits of exhaustion and despair in the Bible — even the greatest prophet, after the greatest victory, crashing into the dark.

[A] And how did God treat his broken prophet? Not with a rebuke. Then Elijah travelled forty days to Mount Horeb, the mountain of God, and went into a cave. And there God asked him: what are you doing here, Elijah?

[A] Elijah poured out his despair — I am the only one left, and they are trying to kill me. And then God told him to stand on the mountain, for the Lord was about to pass by. And a great and powerful wind tore the mountains apart — but the Lord was not in the wind. Then an earthquake — but the Lord was not in the earthquake. Then a fire — but the Lord was not in the fire.

[A] And after the fire came a gentle whisper. A still, small voice. And that was where God was. The prophet who had just called down a pillar of fire was taught the deepest lesson of his life: that God is not only in the spectacular and the overwhelming. He is also — perhaps most often — in the quiet whisper, the gentle voice that meets a broken man in a cave and calls him back.

[A] In that whisper, God did not release Elijah from his calling. But he was no longer alone, and no longer the only one. God gently set him back on his feet.

## CLOSE — THE CHARIOT OF FIRE

[A] Years later, at the end of Elijah's ministry, the time came for God to take him. And the manner of his departure is one of the most extraordinary in all of scripture. Elijah and his successor Elisha were walking together, and they came to the Jordan River. Elijah took his cloak, rolled it up, and struck the water — and the river parted, and the two of them crossed over on dry ground.
VISUAL: Two figures crossing a parted river on dry ground, the waters standing back. Faceless, the crossing.

[A] On the far side, Elijah asked Elisha: what can I do for you before I am taken from you? And Elisha asked for a double portion of Elijah's spirit — to carry on the work with even greater power. You have asked a difficult thing, Elijah said. But if you see me when I am taken, it will be yours. And as they walked on together, talking, the sky itself opened.
VISUAL: The two prophets walking together, the sky above them beginning to blaze. Faceless, the moment before.

[A] Suddenly a chariot of fire and horses of fire appeared and separated the two of them. And Elijah was taken up to heaven in a whirlwind. He did not die. He was carried bodily into heaven in a storm of fire — one of only two people in the entire Bible who never tasted death.
VISUAL: A blazing chariot and horses of fire amid a whirlwind, the prophet caught up into the sky. Spectacle, faceless, the ascension.

[A] Elisha watched him go, crying out, and then he picked up the cloak that had fallen from Elijah — the mantle of the prophet — and with it, the double portion he had asked for. The fire that had marked Elijah's whole life — fire from heaven on Carmel, the fire on Horeb, the chariot of fire at the end — carried him home.
VISUAL: Elisha lifting the fallen cloak, the empty sky above where the chariot vanished. Faceless, the succession.

[A] Elijah's life was one of mountaintops and valleys — the towering triumph of Carmel, and the desert despair beneath the lone tree. And that is part of why his story has spoken to people for thousands of years. He was a man of God who did mighty things — and a man who also broke, and despaired, and wanted to die, and was gently put back together by a God who came to him in a whisper.
VISUAL: The mountain of Carmel and the desert cave together under a wide sky, triumph and despair both. Reflective.

[A] At the heart of his story stands that question on the mountain — the one he flung at a whole wavering nation. How long will you waver between two opinions? It is still the sharpest question faith can ask. Most of us do not openly reject God. We just limp between two opinions — a little faith, a little of the world, hedging our bets, never quite committing to either. Elijah forces the choice into the open. Decide.
VISUAL: The mountaintop altar, the question hanging over it, the call to choose. Symbolic, reflective.

[A] And the answer came not from argument, but from fire. When the people finally saw God act, the wavering ended in an instant — the Lord, he is God. Sometimes we are waiting for certainty before we will commit. Elijah's life suggests the order is often reversed: commit, drench the altar, stake everything — and then watch what God does.
VISUAL: The drenched altar consumed in fire, the staked-everything faith vindicated. Symbolic, reflective.

[A] So here is the question Elijah leaves with you. Where in your own life are you wavering between two opinions — trying to keep one foot with God and one foot somewhere else, refusing to fully commit either way? Elijah would ask you the same thing he asked Israel on that mountain. How long? When will you stop limping, and choose?
