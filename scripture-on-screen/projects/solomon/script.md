## ACT 1 — THE QUESTION IN THE DREAM

[A] Solomon was the son of King David — Israel's greatest king, the shepherd who killed Goliath. When David grew old and died, the throne passed to his son Solomon. And Solomon inherited a kingdom at the height of its power: united, prosperous, at peace. But he also inherited an impossible weight — to rule God's people well. And he felt the weight keenly.
VISUAL: A young king seated on his father's throne, the burden of a great kingdom upon him. Faceless, the inheritance.

[A] Early in his reign, Solomon went to the high place at Gibeon to offer sacrifices to God. And that night, the Lord appeared to him in a dream, and asked him a staggering, open-ended question. Ask for whatever you want me to give you. Anything. The God of all creation, offering a young king a blank cheque.
VISUAL: A young king asleep, a radiant presence in the dream, a question hanging in the air. Symbolic, the dream.

[A] Think of what most people would ask for. Wealth beyond counting. A long life. Power over their enemies. Fame. Solomon could have asked for any of it, and received it. And what he asked for instead is the reason his name has meant wisdom for three thousand years.
VISUAL: The dreaming king, the weight of the choice before him. Faceless, the moment.

[A] Solomon answered humbly. You showed great kindness to my father David, he said, and now you have made me king in his place. But I am only a little child. I do not know how to carry out my duties. And here I am among the people you have chosen — a people too numerous to count. Before asking for anything, he confessed his own inadequacy.

[A] So give your servant a discerning heart, Solomon asked, to govern your people and to distinguish between right and wrong. For who is able to govern this great people of yours? He did not ask for riches, or victory, or long life. He asked for a wise and understanding heart, so that he could lead God's people well. He asked for the one thing that would help others, not himself.

[A] And the Book says the Lord was pleased that Solomon had asked for this. Since you have asked for this, God said — and not for long life or wealth for yourself, nor for the death of your enemies, but for discernment in administering justice — I will do what you have asked. I will give you a wise and discerning heart, so that there will never have been anyone like you, nor will there ever be again.

[A] And then God gave him more than he asked. Moreover, God said, I will give you what you have not asked for — both wealth and honour — so that in your lifetime you will have no equal among kings. There is a deep principle buried here: Solomon sought the right thing first, and the other things were added to him. He asked for wisdom to serve, and received wisdom, riches, and honour besides.

## ACT 2 — THE SWORD AND THE TRUE MOTHER

[A] Solomon woke, and realised it had been a dream. But the gift was real — and soon it would be put to the test in the most famous courtroom scene in all of history. Two women came before the king with a dispute that no judge on earth could untangle.
VISUAL: Two women approaching the throne with a desperate dispute, the court gathered. Faceless, the case.

[A] The two women lived in the same house, and each had given birth to a baby boy just days apart. But in the night, one woman's child had died. And now both women stood before the king, each insisting that the one surviving baby was hers, and the dead child the other's.
VISUAL: The two women before the throne, a single living infant between them, each reaching for it. Faceless, the conflict.

[A] The living child is mine, said the first woman. Her child is the dead one. No, said the other — the dead one is yours, and the living one is mine. Back and forth they argued before the king. There were no witnesses. No evidence. No way to prove who was telling the truth. It was one woman's word against another's, with a child's life hanging between them.
VISUAL: The two women arguing bitterly before the silent king, the baby in the middle. Faceless, the deadlock.

[A] And then Solomon did something that must have frozen the entire court. He called for a sword. Cut the living child in two, he ordered. Give half to one, and half to the other. The wisest king in the world had apparently just commanded that an innocent baby be hacked in half and divided between the two claimants.
VISUAL: The king ordering the sword brought forward, the court recoiling in shock. Faceless, the terrible command.

[A] And here we return to where we began. The raised sword. The living child. The two women. But watch what the command revealed — because Solomon was not being cruel. He was setting a trap that only true love could spring. He knew that a real mother's heart would give itself away in an instant.
VISUAL: The soldier's sword raised over the infant, the two women's faces in the balance. Faceless, the test.

[A] The woman whose son this really was was filled with compassion for her child, and she cried out: please, my lord, give her the living baby! Do not kill him! She would rather lose her son to another woman than see him die. Her love could not bear his death, whatever it cost her.

[A] But the other woman said, coldly: neither I nor you shall have him. Cut him in two. She would rather see the child dead than let the other woman have him. Her words revealed everything. And in that instant, Solomon had his answer — not from evidence, but from the heart each woman exposed when the child's life was on the line.

[A] Give the living baby to the first woman, the king declared. Do not kill him. She is his mother. The one willing to give him up to save his life was the true mother; the one willing to see him killed was not. Solomon had found the truth that no witness could provide — by reading the human heart.

[A] When all Israel heard the verdict the king had given, they were filled with awe — because they saw that he had wisdom from God to administer justice. The story spread across the land and beyond. This was no ordinary cleverness. This was discernment that reached past the surface into the human heart. The gift in the dream had become visible to everyone.

## ACT 3 — THE GOLDEN AGE AND THE TEMPLE

[A] Solomon's wisdom became the wonder of the age. The Book says his wisdom was greater than that of all the people of the East, greater than anyone. He spoke three thousand proverbs, and his songs numbered over a thousand. He could speak of the trees and the plants, the animals and the birds and the fish. People came from all nations, sent by kings who had heard of his wisdom, just to listen to him.
VISUAL: A wise king teaching among scholars and visitors from many nations, scrolls and learning around him. Faceless, the renown.

[A] And under his wise rule, Israel entered a true golden age. The kingdom was vast and at peace on every side. Gold flowed into Jerusalem until silver was considered almost worthless. The people lived in safety, each under their own vine and fig tree. It was the most prosperous, peaceful era in the nation's history — a glimpse of what a kingdom ruled by God-given wisdom could be.

[A] The fame of Solomon spread so far that a queen travelled from the ends of the earth to see it for herself. The Queen of Sheba came from a distant land with a great caravan, having heard reports she could scarcely believe, to test Solomon with hard questions. She came determined to find the limits of his famed wisdom.
VISUAL: A magnificent royal caravan arriving in Jerusalem, a foreign queen at its head. Wide, faceless, the arrival.

[A] She asked him everything that was on her mind — the hardest questions she could devise. And Solomon answered all of them; nothing was too difficult for him to explain. When the queen saw his wisdom, his palace, the order of his court, and the glory of all he had built, the Book says there was no more breath left in her. She was overwhelmed.

[A] The report I heard in my own country was true, she told him — but I did not believe it until I came and saw with my own eyes. And indeed, not even half was told me. [A] But the crowning achievement of Solomon's reign was not his wealth or his fame. It was the temple. His father David had longed to build a permanent house for the Lord in Jerusalem, but had been told his son would build it instead. And so Solomon undertook the greatest building project in the nation's history — a temple for the God of Israel.

[A] For seven years they built it. The finest cedar was floated down from Lebanon. The stone was cut and shaped away from the site, so that no sound of hammer or chisel was heard at the temple itself as it rose. The inner sanctuary — the Most Holy Place — was overlaid entirely with pure gold. Golden cherubim spread their wings over the place where the ark of God would rest. It was breathtaking.

[A] And when it was finished, the priests brought the ark of the covenant into the Most Holy Place. And as they did, a cloud filled the temple of the Lord — the glory of God, so thick that the priests could not even stand to perform their service. The God of heaven had come to dwell in the house Solomon built. It was the high point of the entire Old Testament — God and his people, together, in a house of gold.

[A] Solomon stood before the whole assembly of Israel and prayed one of the great prayers of the Bible — dedicating the temple, asking that God would hear his people whenever they prayed toward this place, and acknowledging that the highest heavens could not contain God, much less a house made by human hands. It was the summit of his reign, and of the nation's faith. Everything was as good as it had ever been, or ever would be.

## CLOSE — THE DIVIDED HEART

[A] And here the story of the wisest man who ever lived takes its tragic turn. Because wisdom, it turns out, is not the same as faithfulness. A man can know exactly what is right — and still, slowly, over years, let his own heart be pulled away from it. And that is precisely what happened to Solomon.
VISUAL: The golden temple at dusk, a long shadow beginning to fall across the splendour. Reflective, the turn.

[A] Solomon loved many foreign women, and married hundreds of them — wives from the very nations God had warned his people not to intermarry with, because they would turn the people's hearts to other gods. And the Book records the slow, devastating result in one heartbreaking line: as Solomon grew old, his wives turned his heart after other gods.
VISUAL: The king surrounded by the influences of foreign courts and foreign gods, his focus drifting. Faceless, the drift.

[A] His heart was no longer fully devoted to the Lord his God, as the heart of his father David had been. The man who had asked God for a whole, discerning heart now had a divided one. He built high places for the foreign gods of his wives, on the very hills around the city of God. The wisest man in the world began, in his old age, to do the most foolish thing imaginable — to turn from the God who had made him wise.
VISUAL: Altars to foreign gods rising on the hills around Jerusalem, the temple's glory dimmed. Faceless, the apostasy.

[A] And there is the terrible irony at the centre of Solomon's life. He asked, as a young man, for an undivided heart — a heart that could discern, that was wholly given to the task God set him. And he ended his life with the most divided heart of all: a heart pulled a thousand ways, devoted to the Lord and to a host of false gods at once. The very thing he asked for is the thing he lost.
VISUAL: The aged king alone amid his fading splendour, his heart pulled in many directions. Faceless, the tragedy.

[A] God was angry with Solomon, and told him that because of this, the kingdom would be torn away from his family — though not in his own lifetime, and not entirely, for the sake of his father David. The golden age would not survive him. After Solomon died, the kingdom he had built split in two, and the long decline of Israel began. The wisest reign in history ended in division, because the wisest heart had divided itself.
VISUAL: The united kingdom beginning to fracture, the cracks spreading across the land. Symbolic, the breaking.

[A] So how do we hold the two halves of Solomon's life together — the young king who asked for wisdom and judged with the heart of God, and the old king who turned away? The Bible does not flinch from either. It gives us both, honestly, because both are true, and because together they teach something we desperately need to know.
VISUAL: The two images of Solomon — the wise young judge, and the divided old king — side by side. Reflective.

[A] And it is this: being wise is not the same as staying faithful. Solomon had more God-given wisdom than anyone who ever lived, and it did not, by itself, keep his heart true. Knowledge of what is right does not guarantee that we will keep doing it. The heart has to be guarded, daily, deliberately — even, especially, by the wise. Wisdom is a gift. Faithfulness is a choice, made again and again.
VISUAL: A single heart at a crossroads, the long discipline of keeping it whole. Symbolic, reflective.

[A] Solomon himself seems to have learned this, late. Tradition holds that the book of Ecclesiastes — with its weary wisdom that everything under the sun is meaningless apart from God — came from his old age, the reflection of a man who had tasted every pleasure and pursuit and found them empty. His final counsel was simple: fear God, and keep his commandments. The wisest man's last lesson was the first thing he had begun to forget.
VISUAL: An old king writing by lamplight, the hard-won wisdom of a long life on the page. Faceless, the reflection.

[A] So here is the question Solomon leaves with you. Solomon asked for a whole, undivided heart — and slowly let it divide, until the dividing cost him everything. Look honestly at your own heart. Is it whole, given fully to what you most believe in? Or is it quietly dividing — a little here, a little there — the way the wisest man who ever lived let his own heart be pulled away?
