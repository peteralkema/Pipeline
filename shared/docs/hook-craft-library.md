# Hook-Craft Library — First 60 Seconds

**Purpose:** A working reference of high-retention opening patterns from cinematic-historical YouTube channels, with a stress-test protocol every Final Hours, Synthetic Press, and Lazarus Films script must pass before lock.

**Last updated:** 2 June 2026 (v0.1 — initial pass, Final Hours-relevant scope)
**Maintenance:** Add 3-5 new hooks per month as channels grow. Review patterns quarterly.

---

## How to use this document

1. Before script lock, transcribe your own first 60 seconds verbatim.
2. Run it through the stress-test protocol in Section 4.
3. If it fails a check, revise the opening, not the video.
4. The library is a reference, not a template — copy the *moves*, never the words.

## Section 3: Pattern Catalog

Patterns observed across the corpus, ordered by frequency. Each pattern includes a definition, examples from the corpus, and notes on when to deploy.

### 3.1 — Date anchor within 10 seconds (11/11 hooks)

**Definition:** A specific date stated in the first 10 seconds, ideally the first sentence. Day, month, year preferred. Year-only acceptable for distant antiquity.

**Examples:**
- "Today, we visit London... We're going to the year 1300." (London 1300, 0-5s)
- "Persepolis, Persia, the spring equinox, 450 BC" (Iran, 0-6s)
- "On December the 6th, 1917, two ships collided in a Canadian harbor" (Halifax, 0-4s)
- "It is the night of April fourteenth, nineteen twelve" (Violin, ~7s)
- "It is the nineteenth of May, fifteen thirty-six" (Boleyn, ~5s)

**Why it works:** The date is the cognitive anchor that lets the viewer locate themselves. Without it, the brain cannot file what comes next. Even Arthur's "Today we visit..." opening that LOOKS like vague intro actually plants the year by second 8.

**When to deploy:** Always. There is no Final Hours-tier video where the date arrives later than second 12.

### 3.2 — Named protagonist within 15 seconds (8/11 hooks)

**Definition:** A specific human being introduced by name (first name, full name, or named role) in the first 15 seconds. Not "a sailor" — "Captain David Morehouse."

**Examples:**
- "His name is Wallace Hartley" (Violin, ~5s)
- "an Athenian companion named Thais" (Iran, ~45s)
- "Captain David Morehouse" (Mary Celeste, ~10s)
- "a 41-year-old woman named Matilde Doehner" (Hindenburg, ~10s)
- "Augustus stands before the Senate" (Rome, ~15s)
- "Mark Antony and Cleopatra sit on golden thrones" (Alexandria, ~5s)

**Why it works:** Names create attachment. The brain commits to following a specific human in a way it doesn't commit to following "the people of Pompeii." Final Hours' literary craft is strongest precisely because every episode locks onto a named protagonist by second 15.

**When to deploy:** Always when possible. Even when the historical event is bigger than one person, find ONE named figure to anchor the first scene through. Mary Celeste opens with Captain David Morehouse (the discoverer), not Captain Briggs (the disappeared) — protagonist-of-the-opening-scene, not protagonist-of-the-mystery.

### 3.3 — Scale claim with specific number (10/11 hooks)

**Definition:** A concrete number that conveys scope, ideally surprising. Not "a big city" — "100,000 souls packed within its walls."

**Examples:**
- "100,000 souls" / "500 ft into the sky" / "three times larger" (London)
- "23 nations ascending a grand staircase" (Iran)
- "15,000 people" / "approaching" (Pompeii Before)
- "1 million people" / "50,000 seats" (Rome)
- "600,000 people" / "50 km out to sea" (Alexandria)
- "20,000 square meters" / "over a thousand rooms" / "four levels high" (Knossos)
- "50,000 people" / "less than a kilometer wide" (Halifax)
- "171 barrels of industrial alcohol" (Mary Celeste)
- "$700 per person, about $15,000 today" (Hindenburg)

**Why it works:** Numbers convert abstract historical claims into concrete imagination. "Big" is forgettable; "500 feet tall, taller than anything Britain would build for another 400 years" is not.

**When to deploy:** At least one specific number in the first 30 seconds. Two or three preferred. Bonus points for a comparative anchor ("3x larger than", "1800 years before", "until Hiroshima").

### 3.4 — Comparative anchoring (8/11 hooks)

**Definition:** Pairing a scale claim with a comparison that makes it feel impossible or absurd. "X years before Y existed" or "Z times larger than W."

**Examples:**
- "Three times larger than any other English city" (London)
- "Taller than anything Britain would build for another 400 years" (London)
- "Steam engines 1800 years before the Industrial Revolution" (Alexandria)
- "Plumbing that won't be matched in Europe for 3,000 years" (Knossos)
- "The largest man-made explosion in human history until Hiroshima 28 years later" (Halifax)
- "200 years known as the Pax Romana" (Rome)

**Why it works:** Numbers in isolation feel like trivia. Numbers paired with comparisons feel like revelation. The comparison creates a curiosity gap — *how is that possible?* — that pulls the viewer forward.

**When to deploy:** Whenever you have a scale claim, attach a comparative anchor. Especially powerful in seconds 30-50, just before the cliffhanger setup.

### 3.5 — Catastrophe / mystery foreshadow at 40-55 seconds (10/11 hooks)

**Definition:** A pivot from setup-of-the-known-world to threat / mystery / disaster. The signal that *something is about to go wrong.* Typically lands in the third quarter of the first minute.

**Examples:**
- "But in the next 50 years, two catastrophes would bring this mighty city to its knees" (London, ~45s)
- "Then, in 330 BC, during a wild feast..." (Iran, ~40s)
- "Unaware that..." (Pompeii Before, cut at 60s)
- "But what he's really restoring is the city itself" (Rome — subtle, lower-intensity)
- "Didn't die from conquest but the..." (Alexandria, cut at ~55s)
- "Outside, the ship is dying" (Violin, ~50s)
- "They were warned. They had hours, a whole day" (Pompeii Final Hours, ~50s)
- "She has become a problem" (Boleyn, ~40s)

**Why it works:** The first 45 seconds set up the world; the foreshadow at second 45-55 promises that world is about to end. This is the structural promise that retains the viewer through the meat of the video. Without it, the opening is a tourist brochure.

**When to deploy:** Always. If your opening minute has no foreshadow, it has no promise. Land it between second 40 and 55, never later than 55.

### 3.6 — Mid-sentence cliffhanger at the 60-second mark (9/11 hooks)

**Definition:** Cut the opening mid-thought. Not a clean sentence ending. Leave the viewer in a forward lean.

**Examples:**
- "3 years of relentless rain that..." (London, 60s cut)
- "But, here's the irony. The Persians..." (Iran)
- "Unaware that..." (Pompeii Before)
- "Didn't die from conquest but the..." (Alexandria)
- "Women held power that wouldn't be seen..." (Knossos)
- "Where it should have hung..." (Mary Celeste — 15:55 video, so first 60s extends; cliffhanger lands later)
- "Irene was 14..." (Hindenburg)
- "Time to gather what mattered, to reach the gate, to walk down to the sea and away. Some did, the ones who ran..." (Pompeii Final Hours)
- "A cargo so dangerous the..." (Halifax)

**Why it works:** The brain is wired to complete unfinished patterns. A clean sentence at second 60 lets the viewer leave; an unfinished one creates active resistance to leaving.

**When to deploy:** Always for short-form (5-8 min). Long-form (15+ min) can extend the cliffhanger to second 75-90, but the principle holds.

### 3.7 — Present-tense immersion (5/11 hooks, all Tier C)

**Definition:** First-person-present narration that places the viewer IN the moment rather than ABOVE looking down. "She is awake" not "She was awake."

**Examples (all Final Hours):**
- "He is thirty-three years old. He plays the violin for a living." (Violin)
- "She has been awake since two in the morning" (Boleyn)
- "Bread is baking. The markets are opening. A dog sleeps in a doorway" (Pompeii Final Hours)
- "She is sitting in the dining room of the airship Hindenburg with her three children" (Hindenburg)

**Why it works:** Present tense collapses the historical distance. The viewer is no longer watching a documentary about people long dead; they are watching events unfold. This is Final Hours' single biggest craft advantage over Arthur, who uses tour-guide past tense ("Pompeii had become...").

**When to deploy:** Final Hours videos by default. Synthetic Press selectively (works for boardroom scenes, hospital scenes, last-minute decisions; doesn't work for tech industry context-setting). Lazarus Films almost always.

### 3.8 — Dramatic irony made explicit (4/11 hooks)

**Definition:** Tell the viewer what the historical actor doesn't know. The audience holds knowledge the character lacks.

**Examples:**
- "It is the most ordinary night of the most extraordinary voyage" (Violin)
- "No one in the city below knows that it is a volcano. There is no word yet for what is about to happen to them" (Pompeii Final Hours)
- "Unaware that..." (Pompeii Before — implicit)
- "They were warned. They had hours, a whole day" (Pompeii Final Hours)

**Why it works:** Dramatic irony is the single most powerful retention device in storytelling. The viewer commits because they know what's coming and the characters don't.

**When to deploy:** Wherever the historical knowledge gap is meaningful. The Pompeii residents thought Vesuvius was a mountain. The Titanic crew thought the bump was nothing. The Mary Celeste crew thought their ship was sailing fine. Find the gap; name it explicitly in the opening.

### 3.9 — Contrast structure (5/11 hooks)

**Definition:** Pair past-state with future-state, peak with collapse, ordinary with catastrophic. Often constructed as "Three years ago X. Today Y."

**Examples:**
- "Three years ago she was crowned queen, in a gown of white and gold. Today she will die by the sword" (Boleyn)
- "I found Rome a city of bricks... and left it a city of marble" (Rome, embedded quote)
- "Outside, the ship is dying. Inside, the music plays on" (Violin)
- "London is three times larger than any other English city... But in the next 50 years, two catastrophes would bring this mighty city to its knees" (London)

**Why it works:** Contrast creates emotional whiplash. The brain registers the gap between states more intensely than either state in isolation. This is also why Final Hours title craft works ("He Could Have Run. Instead, He Picked Up His Violin").

**When to deploy:** Whenever the historical situation has a meaningful before/after structure. Almost every Final Hours video does. Synthetic Press episodes about specific moments (boardroom vote, Anthropic founding day, the verdict) will use this heavily.

### 3.10 — Source authority statement (1/11 hooks, Tier C)

**Definition:** Brief declaration of the historical sources informing the recreation. Earns trust in seconds.

**Example:**
- "What you are about to see has been recreated from period photographs, the Doehner family's own surviving accounts, and the official Lakehurst investigation records" (Hindenburg, ~20s)

**Why it works:** Documentary tier vs. content-mill tier is signaled in 8 seconds with this move. The viewer immediately reads "this is researched" not "this is AI-slop."

**When to deploy:** Selectively. Once or twice per Final Hours episode is enough. Don't make it a tic. Strongest when the episode includes recreated dialogue or scene reconstruction that needs source backing. Probably becomes standard for Synthetic Press where the AI-industry primary sources (Hao, Cade Metz, Levy) matter to credibility.

### 3.11 — Subversion of expectations (3/11 hooks)

**Definition:** Set up what the viewer expects, then immediately negate it.

**Examples:**
- "Not warrior kings, but priestesses conducting rituals in the name of a goddess" (Knossos)
- "Didn't die from conquest but the..." (Alexandria — cliffhanger version)
- "They thought they had time" (Pompeii Final Hours — opening line)

**Why it works:** The brain has prior beliefs about historical events. Naming and overturning those beliefs makes the viewer recommit to the video as a source of new information.

**When to deploy:** Whenever the historical truth contradicts popular belief. For Synthetic Press: most AI industry moments have a popular narrative (the four-day weekend boardroom story, the OpenAI founding-as-pure-altruism story) and an actual contradicting truth. Lead with the contradiction.

## Section 4: The Stress-Test Protocol

Run every Final Hours / Synthetic Press / Lazarus Films first 60 seconds through this checklist before script lock. Pass = all 7. Fail any 2+ = revise the opening.

### Question 1 — Date anchor

Is a specific historical date stated within the first 10 seconds?
- Day-month-year preferred
- Year-only acceptable for distant antiquity
- "Recently" or "in the modern era" fails

### Question 2 — Named protagonist

Is a specific human introduced by name within the first 15 seconds?
- First name + last name preferred
- Named role acceptable ("Captain David Morehouse")
- "A young woman" or "the residents" fails

### Question 3 — Specific number

Is at least one concrete scale number stated in the first 30 seconds?
- Population, distance, dimension, money, time-interval all acceptable
- "Many" or "thousands" fails
- Bonus: comparative anchor attached ("3x larger than", "1800 years before")

### Question 4 — Foreshadow at 40-55 seconds

Is there a clear pivot from setup to threat/mystery/disaster between second 40 and 55?
- The promise that something is about to go wrong
- "But" / "Then" / "Yet" / "Until" / "What no one knew" all signal candidates
- A clean tourist-brochure paragraph at second 50 fails

### Question 5 — Cliffhanger at 60s mark

Does the 60-second mark land mid-thought, not at a clean sentence end?
- Mid-sentence cut preferred
- Unfinished revelation acceptable
- "...And that's the story of X." at 60s fails

### Question 6 — Tense check

For Final Hours and Lazarus Films: is the opening in present tense?
- "She is awake" / "Bread is baking" passes
- "She had been awake" / "Bread was baking" — flag for revision
- For Synthetic Press, past tense is acceptable for context, present tense for specific moments

### Question 7 — Dramatic irony or contrast

Does the opening contain at least ONE of:
- An explicit dramatic irony statement ("No one knew...", "Unaware that...")
- A contrast structure ("Three years ago X. Today Y.")
- A subversion of expectations ("Not what you think, but...")
- An ordinary-to-catastrophic juxtaposition

Bare exposition without any of these fails.

## Section 5: Channel-Specific Calibration Notes

### Final Hours

**Current state:** Tier C craft (the *literary* moves — present tense, named protagonist, dramatic irony) is already STRONGER than Tier A (Arthur). The performance delta is NOT in opening craft — it's in discovery layer (CTR, thumbnail, channel age, topic-keyword anchoring).

**What to keep:** Present-tense immersion, named protagonist, contrast structures, dramatic irony explicit. These are working.

**What to add:** More aggressive scale numbers in the first 30 seconds. Arthur runs 3-5 specific numbers per opening minute; Final Hours runs 1-2. Bring the count up without losing the literary register.

**What to fix:** The Mary Celeste opening (longer video, 15:55) reverted to reportage tense. Long-form Final Hours videos must hold the present-tense discipline of the short-form. Otherwise the channel splits into two voices.

### Synthetic Press

**Pre-launch — calibration before Episode 1:** Synthetic Press cannot use Final Hours' present-tense literary immersion for everything. Tech industry context-setting (board structures, valuations, equity arrangements) requires past tense. But the specific moments — boardroom vote, founding meeting, the verdict — should adopt Final Hours present-tense discipline.

**Hybrid structure suggested for AI-drama:**
- Cold open: present-tense iconic moment (60 seconds of the verdict, the boardroom, the founding meeting)
- Bridge to past tense for context
- Return to present tense for each subsequent scene

**Named protagonist discipline non-negotiable:** Every Synthetic Press episode anchors on ONE named protagonist per opening. Sam Altman OR Ilya Sutskever, not "OpenAI leadership." Mira Murati OR the board, not "the board members." This is the Final Hours discipline applied to AI.

### Lazarus Films

**Standard:** Same as Final Hours but with permission to be MORE literary, given the literary-adaptation positioning. Sentence rhythm should match the source text's register.
