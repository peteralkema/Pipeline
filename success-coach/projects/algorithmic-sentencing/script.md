## PART ONE - THE CASE FOR THE MACHINE

[A] Take the argument seriously, because it is strong. Research has found that judicial decisions vary with things that ought to be irrelevant. The hour of day. The judge's mood. Whether they have eaten.
VISUAL: A bright modern clock on a clean pale wall in daylight, ordinary and quietly consequential.

[A] Two people with near-identical cases can receive very different sentences from different judges, or the same judge on different days. That is not justice. It is weather.
VISUAL: A bright clean pair of nearly identical documents in daylight, receiving very different marks.
[CARD: the same case, different days, different sentences]

[A] A model does not vary like that. Give it the same inputs and it returns the same output, every time, for everyone. That is a real and serious form of fairness.
VISUAL: A bright clean mechanism producing identical results in daylight, steady and impartial, precise.

[A] So a person who says algorithms have no place in a courtroom must explain why they prefer the inconsistency we have. That is a genuinely difficult question.

## PART TWO - WHERE IT GOES WRONG

[A] Now the trouble. These systems learn from the past, and the past was not just. A model trained on who was arrested learns who the police arrested. Not who committed crimes.
VISUAL: A bright modern archive of clean ordered records in daylight, complete, orderly, and partial.

[A] If certain neighbourhoods were policed more heavily, the records show more crime there. The model learns that pattern faithfully, and calls it risk.
VISUAL: A bright clean map in daylight, some districts more densely marked than others, plain and skewed.
[CARD: it learns who was caught - not who was guilty]

[A] So the system does not remove the old bias. It launders it. What was a prejudice becomes a number, and a number wears the costume of objectivity.
VISUAL: A bright clean number displayed calmly on a modern screen in daylight, neutral-seeming and precise.

[A] And now it is worse than before. A biased judge can be argued with, appealed, replaced. A biased score arrives with the authority of mathematics, and nobody in the room can read it.
VISUAL: A bright clean opaque box in daylight, closed and confident, its workings unavailable, high-key.

## PART THREE - THE THING JUSTICE ACTUALLY REQUIRES

[A] But suppose we solved all that. Suppose the model were perfectly unbiased, perfectly accurate. Would it then be right to let it sentence people?
VISUAL: A bright clean flawless instrument in daylight, precise, unbiased, and entirely correct.

[A] Something still refuses. And the reason is one of the deepest ideas in the whole of law.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary person standing in a clean sunlit courtroom, present and fully human.

[A] A defendant is not owed a correct outcome. They are owed a hearing. The right to stand before another human being, to be listened to, and to receive an answer they could argue with.
VISUAL: A bright honest exchange between two people across a clean sunlit courtroom, attentive and real.
[CARD: you are owed a hearing - not merely a correct outcome]

[A] Justice is not a calculation performed upon you. It is something done with you, by people who must look at you and account for what they do.
VISUAL: A bright dignified person being genuinely heard in clear daylight, calm and respected.

[A] A perfectly accurate machine that never hears you has not delivered justice efficiently. It has delivered something else, which happens to be correct.
VISUAL: A bright clean correct answer in daylight, precise and lawful, and somehow entirely empty.

## PART FOUR - THE ANSWER NOBODY CAN GIVE

[A] There is a second thing a person is owed, and it is stranger. An explanation they can understand and contest.
VISUAL: A bright clean plain explanation written openly on a sunlit surface in daylight, legible and honest.

[A] A judge must say why. The reasons may be poor, but they exist in language, and a lawyer can attack them. That is what an appeal is.
VISUAL: A bright modern appeal court in clean daylight, orderly and open, reasons under examination.

[A] A model's reason is a weighting across hundreds of variables. It is true, and it is unusable. You cannot cross-examine a matrix.
VISUAL: A bright clean field of numbers on a modern screen in daylight, honest, complete, and unarguable.
[CARD: you cannot cross-examine a matrix]

[A] So the defendant loses not only the hearing, but the appeal. The system may be right. It simply cannot tell them why in any way they could ever contest.
VISUAL: A bright closed clean door in daylight, orderly and final, no handle upon the near side.

## PART FIVE - WHERE THE MACHINE BELONGS

[A] None of this means the tools are forbidden. It means we have been putting them in exactly the wrong place.
VISUAL: A bright modern courthouse in clean daylight, open and civic, orderly and human.

[A] A model that audits sentencing patterns and shows a judge that they are harsher after lunch is a gift to justice. It makes a human being answerable to evidence.

[CARD: point the machine at the judge - not at the defendant]

[A] A model that flags inconsistency across a whole court, or finds cases where similar people were treated differently, strengthens the human decision rather than replacing it.
VISUAL: A bright clean comparison of two similar cases in daylight, side by side, plainly examined.

[A] Point the machine at the system, and it makes justice better. Point it at the defendant, and it quietly takes the hearing away.
