## PART ONE - WHAT WAS MISSING

[A] The psychologist Carol Gilligan noticed something in how people reasoned about moral problems, and in how their answers were graded.
VISUAL: A bright modern research room in clean daylight, orderly notes on a clean pale table, precise.

[A] Some people reasoned by rules and rights, abstracting away from the particular people involved. Others reasoned about relationships, needs, and who would be hurt.
VISUAL: A bright clean pair of distinct approaches on a pale surface in daylight, both careful, both serious.

[A] The first was scored as more advanced. The second, more often given by women, was scored as less developed moral reasoning.
VISUAL: A bright clean scoring sheet in daylight, orderly and confident, and quietly assuming something.
[CARD: one way of reasoning was scored as "less developed"]

[A] Gilligan's suggestion was simple and devastating. It was not a lesser voice. It was a different one, and the scale had been built without it.
VISUAL: A bright clean measuring instrument in daylight, precise, and calibrated to the wrong thing.

## PART TWO - WHAT CARE ETHICS SAYS

[A] The claim is not that rules and rights are wrong. It is that they are the ethics of strangers, and most of your moral life is not lived among strangers.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary home in clear daylight, warm and lived in, full of particular people.

[A] Care ethics says the fundamental moral fact is not a rule. It is a relationship, in which someone real and particular needs something, and you are the one who is there.
VISUAL: A bright honest scene of one person attending to another in clean daylight, specific and present.
[CARD: the moral fact is a relationship, not a rule]

[A] It attends to particulars. Not what would any rational agent do, but what does this person, whom I know, actually need from me, now?
VISUAL: A bright close ordinary act of attention in clear daylight, specific, careful, and unhurried.

[A] And it takes seriously something the grand theories skip entirely. That care is work. It costs time, patience, and a life. Somebody is always paying.
VISUAL: A bright tired ordinary person doing steady unglamorous work in clean daylight, dignified and real.

## PART THREE - WHY THE STRANGERS' ETHICS FAILS AT HOME

[A] Test the great theories in a room where someone is ill. Consequentialism says maximise well-being. But whose? And measured how? The theory falls silent on the person in front of you.
VISUAL: A bright modern room in clean daylight, a person needing help, the abstraction unable to assist.

[A] Duty ethics says treat everyone as an end. Excellent. It still does not tell you whether to sit up all night with this particular frightened person.
VISUAL: A bright chair drawn close beside another in clear daylight, patient and warm, plainly a choice.

[A] What actually guides you is attention. Noticing. Responsiveness. Skills that look like instinct, and are in fact learned, practised, and often unnoticed.
VISUAL: A bright practised hand doing exactly the right small thing in clean daylight, skilled and calm.
[CARD: attention is a skill, not an instinct]

[A] Care ethics names those skills as moral. That is its great contribution. What was dismissed as women's work turns out to be the substance of ethics, not its background.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary act of care held up plainly in clear daylight, honoured and unsentimental.

## PART FOUR - THE HONEST OBJECTION

[A] There is a serious challenge, and it must be met. If ethics is about those near to us, does it not simply license favouring our own, and ignoring the distant?
VISUAL: A bright clean circle of close relationships in daylight, warm inside, and bounded, honest.

[A] That would be a real failing. A morality of care that ends at your doorstep is just tribalism with a kinder vocabulary.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary doorstep in clear daylight, the street beyond it full of other people, sunlit.

[A] The better answer is that care teaches you what obligation feels like, and then asks you to extend it, not to hoard it. You learn what someone needs by having once tended someone.
VISUAL: A bright widening circle of attention spreading outward in clean daylight, warm and expansive.
[CARD: care is where obligation is learned - not where it stops]

[A] So keep the rules for strangers. Keep the arithmetic for policy. And keep care for what it is. The place where you first learned that another person is real.
VISUAL: A bright balanced life in clear daylight, holding both distant duty and near attention, calm.

## PART FIVE - THE MACHINE THAT CANNOT CARE

[A] Now bring it to our century, because this is where care ethics becomes practical and rather urgent.
VISUAL: A bright modern care setting in clean daylight, capable people and calm technology together.

[A] We are automating care. Systems to monitor the elderly, to answer the lonely, to triage the sick, to check on the vulnerable at scale.
VISUAL: A bright clean modern device in daylight, quietly attentive, calm and orderly, uncluttered.

[A] And there is real good in it. But notice what a machine performs, and what it cannot do. It can execute the tasks of care. It cannot be present to anyone.
VISUAL: A bright clean screen in daylight performing the actions of attention, precisely and hollowly.
[CARD: the tasks of care can be automated. the presence cannot.]

[A] Care is not a service delivered. It is one particular person being responsive to another. Take out the responsiveness and you have a well-run process, not care.
VISUAL: A bright human hand and a bright device side by side in clean daylight, both capable, differently.

[A] Which gives us an honest test. Automate the labour of care, gladly, so that human beings have more time to be present. Automate the presence itself, and you have taken away the thing.
VISUAL: A bright person freed by good tools to sit and attend to another in clear daylight, warm and real.
