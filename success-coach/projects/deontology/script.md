## PART ONE - THE MAN AND THE IDEA

[A] The great architect of this view was Immanuel Kant, a philosopher of such fixed routine that neighbours set their clocks by his afternoon walk.
VISUAL: A bright modern street in clean daylight, a single figure walking with steady purpose, orderly and calm.

[A] Kant wanted a morality that did not depend on consequences, which we can never fully predict, nor on feelings, which shift like weather.
VISUAL: A bright clear sky with fast-moving clouds above a steady sunlit landscape, motion above stillness.

[A] He wanted a foundation as solid as logic itself. And he found it in a single test he called the categorical imperative.
VISUAL: A bright clean geometric form on a pale surface, precise and balanced, modern and exact.

[A] The test is this. Act only in a way that you could will everyone to act, always. If the rule breaks when everyone follows it, it was never moral.
VISUAL: A clean bright card holding one elegant line of text, sunlit and editorial.
[CARD: could you will everyone to act this way, always?]

[A] Take lying. If everyone lied whenever it suited them, words would mean nothing, and lying itself would become impossible. The rule destroys itself. So it fails.
VISUAL: A bright modern scene of people in clear conversation, open and trusting, sunlit and connected.

## PART TWO - THE HEART OF IT

[A] But underneath the logic is something warmer, and it is the real gift of this tradition. Kant's second great formulation.
VISUAL: A single ordinary person framed with real dignity in bright even light, whole and present.

[A] Treat every human being always as an end in themselves. Never merely as a means to your ends.
VISUAL: A bright clean composition of a person looking directly out at the viewer, calm and unmistakably human.
[CARD: treat people as ends, never merely as means]

[A] It means a person is not a tool. Not a resource. Not a number in someone else's calculation. Each one is a world, owed respect for their own sake.
VISUAL: A bright wide row of diverse people in clear daylight, each one evenly and warmly lit, each distinct.

[A] This is where deontology plants its flag. There are things you may not do to a person, even to help others. Their dignity is not yours to trade.
VISUAL: A single figure standing firm and protected in bright open light, dignified and secure.

[A] The pure results view feels the pull of five over one. The deontologist does not even do the maths. You may not carve up a person. Full stop.

[CARD: the line holds regardless of the total]

[A] This is the tradition that says torture stays wrong even when it might work. That the innocent may not be punished even to calm a mob.
VISUAL: A bright clean balance held perfectly level and still, immovable, sunlit against a pale field.

[A] In a world forever tempted to sacrifice the few for the many, deontology is the voice that says, no. Not this one. This one is not for sale.

## PART FOUR - WHERE IT STRAINS

[A] But the strength becomes a weakness at the edges. Because deontology does not bend, it can break in ways that trouble us.

[A] The classic test. A murderer comes to your door asking where your friend is hiding. Kant's strict rule says you still may not lie. Even now.

[CARD: must the rule hold even here?]

[A] Most of us refuse that. Surely the duty not to lie yields to the duty to protect an innocent life. And so the rigid system starts to flex.
VISUAL: A bright figure standing thoughtfully at the threshold, weighing two duties, clear daylight.

[A] Later thinkers softened it, ranking duties, allowing exceptions. But every softening admits the same thing. Pure rules, alone, are not quite enough either.
