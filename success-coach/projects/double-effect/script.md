## PART ONE - THE DISTINCTION

[A] The idea goes back to Thomas Aquinas. An act can have two effects. One you aim at. One you foresee but do not aim at.
VISUAL: A bright clean single action producing two distinct outcomes in daylight, precise and diagrammatic.

[A] And it says these are morally different. Intending a harm makes it part of your plan. Foreseeing a harm means it happens because of you, but it is not what you are trying to do.
VISUAL: A bright clean arrow aimed at one target, glancing past another, in clear daylight, high-key.
[CARD: intended = part of your plan. foreseen = a cost you accept.]

[A] Test it against your own instincts. Return to the trolley problem, and to the footbridge. The arithmetic there was identical, but the feeling was not.
VISUAL: A bright modern pedestrian bridge over a sunlit rail line in daylight, clean and ordinary.

[A] Pull the lever, and the man's death is a side effect. Push him off the bridge, and his death is your instrument. You need him to die. Without it, your plan fails.
VISUAL: A bright clean pair of diagrams in daylight, one man beside the plan, one man inside it, sunlit.

[A] That is why the two cases feel so different, though the numbers agree. The doctrine of double effect names precisely what your instinct already knew.

## PART TWO - THE CONDITIONS

[A] But the doctrine is not a licence. It has strict conditions, and they are usually forgotten by whoever is quoting it.
VISUAL: A bright clean set of four simple conditions on a pale surface in daylight, ordered and precise.

[A] The act itself must not be wrong. You may not do an evil thing and excuse it by its good effects.
VISUAL: A bright clean firm boundary in daylight, plain, unarguable, and drawn before any calculation.

[A] The harm must not be the means to the good. If your good outcome requires the harm, you have intended it, whatever you tell yourself.
VISUAL: A bright clean mechanism in daylight where one part cannot be removed without the whole failing.
[CARD: if the plan needs the harm, you intended the harm]

[A] The good effect must be what you aim at. Not a convenient story told afterwards about what you were really trying to do.
VISUAL: A bright honest figure aiming plainly and openly at something good in clear daylight, unhidden.

[A] And the good must be proportionate to the harm. A small good does not license a great evil merely because you did not aim at it.

## PART THREE - WHY IT IS TRUE

[A] Some philosophers reject the whole distinction. If you knew, they say, you chose. Foreseeing is intending, dressed for court.

[A] But consider. A surgeon knows that operating causes pain and risks death. A parent knows that letting a child cycle to school risks a crash.

[A] If foresight were intention, then every surgeon intends to hurt, and every parent intends to endanger. That is not moral seriousness. It is a confusion.

[CARD: if foresight were intention, every surgeon would be an assailant]

[A] The distinction is real because plans are real. What you are trying to do shapes what you will do next, what you will accept, and what you would abandon.

[A] Watch for the test. If the harm did not occur, would you be relieved, or would your plan have failed? That single question exposes what you truly intended.

## PART FOUR - HOW IT BECOMES AN EXCUSE

[A] And now the abuse, because this doctrine is a favourite of people doing terrible things.

[A] Nations bomb, and call the dead a side effect. Companies pollute, and call the sick a side effect. Everyone insists they intended only the good.

[A] But the conditions were never met. The harm was often disproportionate. Often it was the means. And often the good aimed at was not the good stated.

[A] The doctrine of double effect is not a way to permit harms. It is a way to explain why some unavoidable harms are not murders. Confuse the two, and it becomes a laundering machine.
