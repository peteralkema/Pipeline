## PART TWO - THE TRICK IN THE ARGUMENT

[A] Watch the sleight of hand. It slides between two very different claims, and hopes you will not notice.
VISUAL: A bright clean pair of nearly identical shapes on a pale surface in daylight, subtly distinct.

[A] The first claim. Whenever you act, you are acting on your own desire. That is trivially true, and utterly harmless. Of course it was your desire. Whose else would it be?
VISUAL: A bright ordinary person acting on their own wish in clear daylight, plainly and unremarkably.

[A] The second claim. The object of your desire is always your own benefit. That is a strong, interesting claim. And it does not follow from the first at all.
VISUAL: A bright clean arrow in daylight pointing outward at another person, not back at its source.
[CARD: your desire = a desire for yourself]

[A] Because desires have objects. You can desire your own pleasure. You can also desire that your child be safe. Both are your desires. Only one is about you.
VISUAL: A bright honest parent watching a child play safely in clean daylight, attentive and outward-facing.

[A] The egoist mistakes the fact that it is your desire for the claim that it is a desire for you. Once you see it, the theory deflates.

## PART THREE - THE FEELING GOOD OBJECTION

[A] But the strongest version remains. You do feel good when you help. Surely that pleasure is the real motive.

[A] Turn it around. Why does helping feel good? It feels good because you actually wanted the other person to be well, and now they are.

[A] The pleasure is the result of caring, not the reason for it. If you did not already want the stranger to be safe, their safety would give you no pleasure at all.

[A] Which the theory gets exactly backwards. It sees the warm feeling arriving and declares it the driver, when it was always the passenger.

[A] Now here is why any of this matters practically, and it is not about human character at all.
