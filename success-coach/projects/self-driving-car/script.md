## PART ONE - WHY IT FEELS DIFFERENT

[A] When a human driver faces this, we do not really judge them. There was no time to think. Whatever they did was reflex, and we call it a tragedy, not a choice.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary street in clean daylight, an unavoidable instant, no time for anyone to reason.

[A] But a machine has no reflexes. It has policies. Written down. Debated. Approved. Every crash it has is, in a sense, premeditated.
VISUAL: A bright clean document of plain rules on a pale desk in daylight, deliberate and orderly.
[CARD: a machine has no reflex - only a policy someone approved]

[A] That is what disturbs us. Not that a machine decides. That someone decided, in advance, in comfort, with a coffee, months before anybody was anywhere near that street.
VISUAL: A bright calm meeting room in clean daylight, a decision being made unhurried and abstractly.

[A] And a decision made calmly in advance is exactly the kind we hold people fully responsible for.
VISUAL: A bright clean signature line on a plain document in daylight, waiting, sunlit and consequential.

## PART TWO - WHAT ENGINEERS ACTUALLY DO

[A] So how do the people building these cars answer the dilemma? Mostly, they refuse it. And their reasons are better than they first appear.
VISUAL: A bright modern engineering workspace in clean daylight, capable people at clean workstations.

[A] They point out that the car almost never knows what the philosophers assume it knows. It does not reliably see a child, and two adults, and calculate their fates.
VISUAL: A bright clean sensor view in daylight showing uncertain shapes, honest about what it cannot know.

[A] It sees uncertain shapes with probabilities attached. The clean dilemma requires a certainty that no real system possesses in the half second available.
VISUAL: A bright clean street rendered as probabilities in daylight, blurred at the edges, honest and modern.
[CARD: the car does not see a child - it sees a probability]

[A] So instead of choosing whom to kill, engineers build systems that brake as hard as possible, stay in their lane, and above all, avoid ever arriving at that street at that speed.
VISUAL: A bright clean car braking decisively and predictably in daylight, sober and unglamorous.

[A] It is an unromantic answer. It is also, quite possibly, the ethical one. The best response to an impossible choice is to make it far rarer.
VISUAL: A bright safe modern street in clear daylight, ordinary traffic flowing calmly, uneventful and good.

## PART THREE - BUT SOMEONE STILL CHOSE

[A] And yet. Deciding not to decide is still a decision, and it has victims. A car that always stays in its lane has chosen the pedestrian on the pavement over nobody at all.
VISUAL: A bright clean lane marked firmly on a sunlit road in daylight, simple and consequential.

[A] A car programmed to protect its passenger above all others has made a very definite moral claim about whose life is worth more. It just never says so out loud.
VISUAL: A bright clean car in daylight, its protected interior warm, the street beyond it merely outside.
[CARD: "protect the passenger" is a moral claim, quietly made]

[A] Researchers asked the public a revealing question. Should cars sacrifice their passenger to save several pedestrians? Most people said yes, that is clearly right.
VISUAL: A bright modern crowd in clear daylight, most of them nodding, reasonable and agreed.

[A] Then they were asked whether they would buy such a car. And most said no, absolutely not. They would buy the one that protects them.
VISUAL: A bright modern showroom in clean daylight, the same people choosing very differently, calm.

[A] We want everyone else's car to be moral. We want our own car to be loyal. That contradiction is the actual problem, and no engineer can solve it for us.
VISUAL: A bright clean balance in daylight, principle on one pan and self-interest on the other, honest.

## PART FOUR - THE HARDER QUESTION

[A] So here is what the trolley framing hides. The real ethical decisions in autonomous driving are not the dramatic ones. They are the boring, enormous ones.
VISUAL: A bright plain modern engineering document in clean daylight, dull, detailed, and important.

[A] How much testing is enough before deployment? How safe must these cars be to be allowed on the road? Merely better than the average human, or far better?
VISUAL: A bright clean threshold line on a pale surface in daylight, plain, precise, and deeply consequential.
[CARD: how safe is safe enough to deploy? that is the real dilemma.]

[A] Because that question kills or saves people in the thousands, quietly, over years. The child in the street is one life. The deployment threshold is tens of thousands.
VISUAL: A bright wide modern city of ordinary drivers and pedestrians in clear daylight, alive and numerous.

[A] And here the arithmetic cuts strangely. Waiting for a perfect car means years of human drivers, who are far more dangerous. Delay has a body count too.
VISUAL: A bright ordinary street of human-driven traffic in daylight, familiar, and quietly hazardous.

[A] Every month of caution saves the people a flawed system would have hurt, and costs the people a good system would have saved. Nobody escapes the ledger.
VISUAL: A bright clean balance in daylight, both pans holding real lives, neither side empty, sobering.

## PART FIVE - WHO GETS TO DECIDE

[A] Which brings us to the question underneath all of it. Not what the rule should be. Who has the standing to write it.
VISUAL: A bright meeting room in clean daylight, a diverse team around a clean table, deliberating.

[A] Right now, these rules are largely written inside companies, by engineers and lawyers, answerable to shareholders, in documents most of us will never read.
VISUAL: A bright modern corporate office in clean daylight, capable, private, and quietly powerful.

[A] But a rule that decides who lives in an unavoidable crash is not a product feature. It is a law, applied by a machine, to people who never voted for it.
VISUAL: A bright modern civic building in clean daylight, public and open, where such rules ought to be argued.
[CARD: a crash policy is a law - written by nobody we elected]

[A] So the deepest question is democratic, not technical. Not what would you programme. But who should be in the room, and what may they be allowed to decide alone.
VISUAL: A bright open public forum in clear daylight, ordinary citizens present and engaged, warm.

## PART SIX - THE WEIGHT YOU CARRY OUT

[A] Should a self-driving car decide who dies? It already does, every time it holds its lane. The choice was simply made earlier, and quietly.
VISUAL: The balance-scale mark, clean and level, bright petrol and amber, steady and serious.

[A] So do not ask whether machines should make moral choices. They cannot avoid it. Ask instead whether the choices are visible, argued, and owned by someone we can name.

[A] And notice your own contradiction. You want the moral car for everyone, and the loyal car for yourself. Until we resolve that honestly, no engineer can build what we say we want.
