## COLD OPEN

## PART ONE - WHY IT IS HARD

[A] The trolley problem was made famous by the philosopher Philippa Foot, and later sharpened by Judith Jarvis Thomson. It has haunted the field ever since.
VISUAL: A bright modern lecture hall, clean daylight through tall windows, a simple track diagram on a large screen.

[A] It looks like a simple puzzle. It is not. It is a trap, carefully built to catch two things you believe at the very same time.
VISUAL: Two clean geometric shapes on a bright surface, interlocking in a way that also seems to pull apart.

[A] The first belief is this. Saving five lives is better than saving one. More people alive is better than fewer. That feels obvious.
VISUAL: Five bright figures on one pan of a wide clean balance, a single figure on the other, the five side lower.
[CARD: belief one - more lives saved is better]

[A] The second belief is this. It is wrong to kill an innocent person. Not unfortunate. Wrong. That feels obvious too.
VISUAL: A single person standing alone in a bright open space, calm and dignified, clear even light around them.
[CARD: belief two - killing an innocent is wrong]

[A] The trolley problem forces those two obvious beliefs to collide. Pull the lever, and you honour the first while breaking the second.
VISUAL: The two clean shapes from before now grinding hard against each other, bright and tense on a pale field.

[A] There is no answer that keeps both. Whatever you choose, you have to give one of them up. That is the trap.
VISUAL: The balance-scale mark, one pan now empty, bright and stark against clean petrol.

## PART TWO - THE FIRST ANSWER, PULL THE LEVER

[A] Let us take the first path seriously. The case for pulling the lever. It has a name. It is called consequentialism.
VISUAL: A single bright clean road running forward through open sunlit country, clear and inviting.

[A] The idea is simple. The rightness of an action is measured by its results. Look honestly at the outcomes, and choose the better one.
VISUAL: Two bright roads side by side, one ending at five figures, one ending at a single figure, clear and open.
[CARD: consequentialism - judge the action by its outcome]

[A] Five living people is a better outcome than one. So you pull the lever. This is not cold arithmetic. It is a kind of mercy, at scale.
VISUAL: Five people walking away safely in bright sunlight, alive and ordinary, going about their day.

[A] Most people, asked quickly, agree. Around nine in every ten say they would pull it. The numbers feel like they settle the matter.
VISUAL: A bright modern crowd, most of them stepping toward one side, a clean visual majority in full daylight.

[A] But hold on. If results are all that matter, the trolley problem is about to ask you a much harder question. And your answer will change.
VISUAL: The bright road ahead narrowing slightly, a faint hint of doubt, but still in clear open light.

## PART THREE - THE SECOND ANSWER, DO NOTHING

[A] Now the other path. The case for doing nothing. This one has a name too. It is called deontology.
VISUAL: A bright figure standing firmly still, hands at their sides, choosing not to move, clear daylight.

[A] It says some actions are wrong in themselves, whatever the results. And using a person as a tool, to be sacrificed, is one of them.
VISUAL: A single person framed with real dignity in bright clean light, unmistakably a human being, not an object.
[CARD: deontology - some acts are wrong regardless of outcome]

[A] When you pull the lever, you do not merely let one person die. You choose their death. You make them the instrument that saves the others.
VISUAL: The switch lever again, a hand upon it, the deliberate weight of the decision, bright and sharp.

[A] The philosopher Immanuel Kant put it plainly. A human being must always be treated as an end in themselves. Never merely as a means.
VISUAL: A bright, clear composition of an ordinary modern person looking directly out at the viewer, calm and whole.

[A] So the deontologist steps back. Five deaths would be a tragedy. But it would not be a murder. And they will not commit a murder to prevent a tragedy.
VISUAL: A person deliberately turning their hand away from the lever, resolute, in bright open daylight.

## PART FOUR - THE VARIATION THAT CHANGES YOU

[A] Here is where it becomes personal. A new version, from Judith Jarvis Thomson. Forget the lever. You are standing on a footbridge above the track.
VISUAL: A bright modern pedestrian bridge over a rail line, sunlit and clean, a person standing at the railing.

[A] The same trolley races toward the same five people. Beside you on the bridge stands a very large stranger. His weight alone could stop it.
VISUAL: Two figures on the sunlit bridge, one large, the track and five workers visible below in bright light.

[A] The arithmetic is identical. Push the one man off the bridge, and five people live. One death, five saved. Exactly the trade you made before.
VISUAL: A clean bright diagram, one figure, five figures, the same balance as earlier, entirely unchanged.
[CARD: same maths - 1 dies, 5 live - identical to the lever]

[A] But now almost nobody will do it. The nine in ten who happily pulled the lever will not push the man. Something in us simply refuses.
VISUAL: A bright crowd, this time most of them stepping back, hands raised, a clear reversal in daylight.

[A] And that is the genius of the problem. The outcome is the same. The arithmetic is the same. But pushing a man with your own hands feels like murder. And perhaps it is.
VISUAL: A hand hovering near a shoulder, then withdrawing, in bright clean light, the refusal made visible.

[A] Which means the thing guiding you was never really the arithmetic. It was something deeper. Something about what we are permitted to do to one another.
VISUAL: The balance-scale mark, bright, the amber dot glowing steady at its centre.

## PART FIVE - THE MACHINE AT THE LEVER

[A] For half a century, this was a thought experiment. A puzzle for philosophers and quiet afternoons. It is not a thought experiment any more.
VISUAL: A bright modern city street, contemporary and alive, clean daylight, ordinary traffic flowing smoothly.

[A] Right now, self-driving cars are on real roads. And engineers must decide, in advance, what the car does when a crash becomes unavoidable.
VISUAL: A sleek modern autonomous car on a sunlit road, sensor array visible, bright and clean.

[A] Swerve to miss the child who ran into the road, and you strike the two people on the pavement. Stay straight, and the child is gone.
VISUAL: A bright, clear street scene, a child at one edge, two pedestrians at the other, the car between them.
[CARD: the car's dilemma - swerve and hit two, or stay and hit one]

[A] That is the trolley problem. Except now no one stands at the lever in the moment. The choice was made months earlier, in an office, written into code.
VISUAL: A bright modern office, a developer at a clean desk, sunlight on the walls, calm lines of code on the screen.

[A] And it is not only cars. The same choice is quietly moving into hospitals, where artificial intelligence now helps decide who is treated first.
VISUAL: A bright modern hospital corridor, clean daylight through wide windows, calm and orderly, staff moving with purpose.

[A] Someone has to decide the rule the machine will follow. The many, or the one. And they decide it once, in advance, for everyone.
VISUAL: A bright meeting room, a diverse team around a clean table, the dilemma sketched on a glass wall, full daylight.

[A] So the trolley problem stopped being about you and a lever. It became about who writes the rules, what values we hide inside our machines, and whether we even agree.
VISUAL: The autonomous car again, bright, moving calmly through a sunlit city full of ordinary people.

## PART SIX - THE WEIGHT YOU CARRY OUT

[A] So what is the answer. After all of this, here is the honest truth. There is no clean one. And that is precisely the point.

[A] Maybe you are the person who saves the five. Maybe you are the person who will not push the one. Both of you are trying to be good.
