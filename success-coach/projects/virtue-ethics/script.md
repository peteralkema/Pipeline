## PART ONE - ARISTOTLE'S ANSWER

[A] The great teacher here is Aristotle, writing more than two thousand years ago, and still startlingly practical today.
VISUAL: A bright sunlit colonnade, clean and modern in feel, open and full of light, calm and spacious.

[A] He asked what everything is ultimately for. And he concluded that everything we chase, money, success, comfort, we chase for the sake of one final thing. A flourishing life.
VISUAL: A bright clean series of stepping forms leading toward a single warm point of light, sunlit and ordered.

[A] He called it eudaimonia. Not fleeting pleasure, but a life lived well and fully. The deep, durable well-being of a human being working as they should.
VISUAL: A bright figure standing fulfilled and grounded in clear open daylight, calm and whole.
[CARD: eudaimonia - a life lived well and fully]

[A] And how do you build such a life? Through virtue. Through excellences of character. Courage. Honesty. Generosity. Justice. Practised until they are simply who you are.
VISUAL: A bright clean set of interwoven strong threads on a pale surface, forming one solid whole, sunlit.

## PART TWO - CHARACTER IS A HABIT

[A] Here is the insight that makes this tradition so practical. Virtue is not something you are born with. It is something you build. By doing.
VISUAL: A bright modern hand practising a craft in clean daylight, repeating a motion, skilled and steady.

[A] You do not become brave by reading about courage. You become brave by doing brave things, again and again, until courage becomes your habit, your reflex, your nature.

[CARD: you become what you repeatedly do]

[A] This is why Aristotle said we are what we repeatedly do. Excellence is not an act. It is a habit. Character is the sum of a thousand small choices.
VISUAL: A bright clean path made of many small even stones stretching forward under daylight, each one placed.

[A] And it is strangely hopeful. It means you are not fixed. Whatever you are today, you can become, by practice, something better tomorrow.

## PART THREE - THE GOLDEN MEAN

[A] But which habits? Aristotle gave a beautifully practical guide. He called it the golden mean. Every virtue sits between two vices.
VISUAL: A bright clean balance held perfectly level in daylight, poised between two extremes, sunlit.

[A] Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness. Too little nerve, and you flee. Too much, and you throw your life away. Courage is the steady centre.
VISUAL: A bright three-part diagram on a pale surface, a calm centre flanked by two extremes, modern and clear.
[CARD: courage = the mean between cowardice and recklessness]

[A] Generosity sits between stinginess and waste. Honesty between cruelty and flattery. The good life is a life of well-judged balance, not blind extremes.
VISUAL: A bright series of small balanced scales in a clean row under daylight, each poised and level.

[A] And finding that centre takes the master virtue. Practical wisdom. The learned skill of knowing the right amount, at the right time, for the right reason.
VISUAL: A bright thoughtful figure judging a fine balance with care in clear daylight, skilled and calm.

[A] Virtue ethics fills that gap. It cares about the whole arc of a life. About mercy, and honesty, and courage as lived textures, not just correct answers.
