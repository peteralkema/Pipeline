I want to tell you about six minutes. The most important six minutes in Sarah's career. She walked into a meeting room earning fifty-eight thousand. She walked out earning a hundred and twenty. It was not luck. It was not a clever trick taught in a book. It was three small choices she made in those six minutes. Most professionals get this moment wrong. Here is exactly what she did, and why it worked. She has been at her company for three years and eight months. She knows exactly what she is worth. She has done the research. Two competitor offers sit in her drawer. She has not used them. Today is her annual review. The conversation she has avoided for a year. Her boss is a kind man. That is part of the problem. Kind bosses give kind raises. Three percent. Maybe four. She has watched colleagues leave for forty-thousand-pound jumps. She has stayed because she likes the team. The work. The familiar walk to the kitchen. But she has been paying for that loyalty every single month. Today she is going to ask for what she is worth. She wakes at six. She prepares like she is interviewing for the job she already has. She makes a list. Three columns. Achievements. Comparables. Counter-arguments. She practises one sentence in the mirror until it sounds natural. The sentence is: I need a hundred and twenty for me to stay. She does not soften it. She does not pad it. She does not apologise for it. She does not dread this conversation. She is going to lead it. At nine she walks into the office. Coffee in hand. Completely normal. At ten the calendar reminder fires. Review with Mark, eleven thirty. At eleven she stops working. She rereads her list. She closes her notebook. At eleven twenty-eight she walks across the office to the glass meeting room. Mark is already there. Laptop open. Friendly smile. He likes her. He starts the way kind bosses start. He asks how her year has been. She tells him. Briefly. She does not list. She tells him she had a good year. He nods. He brings up her file on his screen. He says the team values her. He says she has grown significantly. He pauses. Like he is about to give her a gift. He says they would like to offer her a four percent raise. Four percent on fifty-eight is two thousand three hundred and twenty pounds a year. After tax, fewer than fifteen hundred. About a hundred and twenty pounds a month. She does not react. She does not nod. She does not thank him. She lets the number sit between them. Five seconds. Then ten. Mark fills the silence. He says he knows it is not a lot. He says budgets are tight this year. He says he hopes she understands. Now is the moment she has practised. She does not rush it. She says, Mark, I appreciate the offer. I want to be honest with you. She says, I have done some research. And I have had two conversations. She does not name the companies. She does not show the offers. She says, The market for someone with my work is between one hundred and ten and one hundred and thirty. She says, I love the team. I would prefer to stay. She says, I need a hundred and twenty for me to stay. Then she stops. She does not explain. She does not justify. She does not add anything. Mark looks at her. He looks at his laptop. He looks at her again. He says he was not expecting this. She does not fill the silence. He says he will need to talk to his director. She nods, once. She says, I understand. Take whatever time you need. She says, I would like the offer in writing before I respond either way. She stands up. She thanks him for his time. She walks out. Six minutes have passed. The meeting ended seventeen minutes early. Mark sits alone for two of those minutes before he calls his director. By the end of the day she has an email. The number is one hundred and fifteen. She does not reply that evening. She does not reply the next morning. She replies at three the next afternoon. One paragraph. Calm. She thanks him for moving so quickly. She says one hundred and fifteen is close. She says she would accept one hundred and twenty plus an additional week of leave. She signs off. Closes her laptop. Does not refresh her inbox. The reply comes back at five. One hundred and twenty. The extra leave approved. She accepts in writing. One short professional sentence. Thank you. I look forward to continuing. She closes the laptop. She does not tell anyone for two days. Her base salary has more than doubled in nine minutes of actual conversation. After tax that is approximately three thousand four hundred pounds a month more. Forty thousand more a year. For the rest of her career. Compounded. She did not threaten. She did not show her competing offers. She did not list her achievements. She did not ask for permission. She named her number. She held the silence. She left. That is the whole technique. Three things. Six minutes. Most professionals do not ask. They wait to be given. They confuse loyalty with patience. They confuse politeness with weakness. They believe a good boss will eventually notice their worth. Good bosses notice. They still pay you the budget number. Sarah's boss did not become unkind when she asked for more. He paid her more because she asked for more. He still liked her. The conversation was not a fight. It was a clarification. She told him what she was worth. He paid it. Three things she did. She named her number, specifically. She held the silence. She did not justify or explain. She did not respond immediately. She gave herself time. That is six minutes. That is forty thousand pounds a year. Compounded across a career, that single conversation is worth more than a house. Have the conversation. Most of you have earned it already.
