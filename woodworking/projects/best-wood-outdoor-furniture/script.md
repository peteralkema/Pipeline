## PART ONE - WHAT MAKES WOOD SURVIVE OUTSIDE

[A] The secret to a weatherproof wood is not hardness. It is what the living tree left behind inside its own grain. As a tree ages, its inner wood - the heartwood - fills with natural oils, resins, and compounds the tree made to defend itself against rot and insects.
VISUAL: A cross-section of a log showing the darker heartwood at the centre ringed by paler outer wood, clean and well lit; warm natural tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Those built-in compounds are a natural preservative. They make the heartwood distasteful to fungi and slow to absorb water. That is why a naturally durable wood can sit out in the rain for years while a plain one rots - the durable one is quietly poisoning the very fungi trying to eat it.
VISUAL: A close study of rich, oily heartwood grain, dense and deep in colour, almost glowing under soft light; clean neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] But here is the catch that ruins many outdoor builds. Only the heartwood carries that protection. The pale outer ring, the sapwood, has almost none of it - even on a durable species. Build with the sapwood and you lose the very thing you chose that wood for.
VISUAL: A board showing the clear line between dark protected heartwood and pale vulnerable sapwood running along its length; clean, evenly lit, warm tones, no people.

[A] So durability outdoors comes down to two questions. Does this species make protective heartwood at all? And am I actually using the heartwood, not the pale sapwood beside it? Get both right and the weather barely touches your work.
VISUAL: A single rich heartwood board resting alone on a clean surface, sound and deep-toned, soft even light across the grain; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART TWO - THE WOODS TO REACH FOR

[A] Start with the gold standard. Teak is the wood that decks ocean-going boats, and for good reason - it is so loaded with natural oil that it sheds water, resists rot, and weathers to a silver grey without ever weakening. It is expensive, but it lasts a lifetime outdoors with almost no care.
VISUAL: A rich golden-brown teak surface in soft daylight, the grain straight and oily-looking, water beading on top; clean neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] If teak is out of reach, western red cedar is the everyday champion. It is light, easy to work, and full of natural compounds that fight decay and insects. It will not take a beating like a hardwood, but for garden furniture left in the weather, it endures beautifully.
VISUAL: A warm reddish-brown cedar board with soft straight grain, resting on a clean surface in daylight; calm, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] White oak is the quiet workhorse. Unlike its cousin red oak, white oak's pores are naturally plugged with a tissue that blocks water from wicking through it - the same trait that made it the wood of choice for wine barrels and wooden ships. Strong, dense, and genuinely weatherproof.
VISUAL: A pale golden-brown white oak board with tight straight grain and subtle ray flecks, evenly lit on a clean surface; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And do not overlook cypress, redwood, and the dense tropical hardwoods. Each carries its own built-in defence against rot. The thread running through all of them is the same - a wood that makes its own preservative will always outlast one that does not, no matter what you brush on top.
VISUAL: A small group of different durable wood samples arranged on a clean surface, warm browns and reds together, soft even light; neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - THE WOODS TO AVOID

[A] Now the ones to walk past. Plain pine is the great outdoor mistake. It is cheap, soft, and everywhere - and left untreated in the weather, it drinks in water, feeds fungi, and turns soft and grey within a season or two. Lovely indoors, doomed outside.
VISUAL: A pale pine board showing early signs of weather damage, the surface greying and roughening at the edges; clean neutral background, muted tones, no people.

[A] Red oak looks almost identical to white oak, but outdoors it betrays you. Its pores are wide open, like a bundle of tiny straws running through the board, and they wick water deep into the wood where rot takes hold. A beautiful indoor wood and a poor outdoor one.
VISUAL: A close study of red oak end grain showing the open pores as tiny dark holes, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] As a rule, most pale, fast-grown softwoods and most ordinary hardwoods have no real defence against the weather. They were never built for it. Use them outside unprotected and you are asking a wood to do a job its grain was never made to handle.
VISUAL: A plain untreated board resting outdoors on damp ground, the surface already darkening with moisture; muted overcast light, clean composition, no people.

[A] There is one honourable exception. Pressure-treated lumber takes ordinary softwood and forces preservatives deep into the grain under pressure - it cannot make pretty furniture, but for posts and frames in contact with the ground, it is built to last where untreated wood would rot away.
VISUAL: A greenish-tinted pressure-treated post standing in soil, sturdy and weatherproof, clean neutral background; even daylight, no people, no text.

## PART FOUR - MAKING IT LAST

[A] Choosing the right wood wins most of the battle, but a few habits win the rest. Wherever you can, keep the wood from sitting in standing water - raise furniture legs off wet ground, let rain run off rather than pool, and decay loses most of its grip before it can begin.
VISUAL: A wooden chair leg resting on a small stone foot lifting it clear of wet ground, clean and practical; soft daylight, neutral background, no people.

[A] If you finish outdoor wood, choose a finish made to flex and breathe with the weather, and accept that it will need refreshing. A penetrating outdoor oil renewed each year protects far better than a hard film that cracks, peels, and traps water underneath once the sun gets to it.
VISUAL: A wooden surface outdoors with a soft oiled sheen, water beading cleanly on top, rich grain showing through; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Or do what generations did before us - choose a naturally durable heartwood, build it well, and simply let it weather to a silver grey. Teak, cedar, and white oak need no finish at all to last. The wood was the protection all along.
VISUAL: A silver-grey weathered teak surface, dignified and sound despite its age, soft even light across the grain; clean neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] So before your next outdoor build, ask the only question that really matters. Is this a wood that defends itself, and am I using its protected heartwood? Get that right, and the weather becomes something your work survives, not something it surrenders to.
VISUAL: A finished outdoor wooden bench standing sound and solid in a garden, weathered but strong, calm and enduring; soft daylight, clean composition, no people.
