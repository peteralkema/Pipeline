## PART ONE - WALLS ARE NEVER SQUARE

[A] The first hidden trap is that walls are never as square as they look. Your seat may sit in a recess between two walls that appear perfectly parallel and square. Measure closely, though, and you will find they are not - walls bow, lean, and meet at angles that are never quite ninety degrees.
VISUAL: A window seat recess whose walls are subtly out of square; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So a cushion cut as a perfect rectangle will not fit a space that is not a perfect rectangle. It fits at one point and jams or gaps at another, because the seat opening is subtly wider at the front than the back, or on one side than the other. The rectangle meets a not-quite-rectangle.
VISUAL: A rectangular cushion meeting an out-of-square recess, jamming; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] This is why cushions that measured right on paper fail in reality. The single width you measured was only right at that one spot. A recess needs measuring in several places - front and back, both sides - because the real opening is a slightly irregular shape, not the clean rectangle it appears.
VISUAL: A recess measured at several points revealing its irregular shape; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So the first rule is to measure the real shape, not the assumed one. Measure the width at the front and at the back, and the depth on both sides. If they differ, your space is out of square, and your cushion must be made to that real, slightly irregular shape to fit cleanly.
VISUAL: A cushion made to the real irregular shape of a recess; clean interior, warm light, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - THE ALLOWANCE TRAP

[A] The second trap is the allowance - how much smaller than the seat a cushion should be. Cut a cushion to the exact size of the seat and it will not fit. A cushion has thickness and softness, and it needs a little room, or it wedges in, buckles up, and bulges at the edges.
VISUAL: A cushion cut to exact size buckling because it has no allowance; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] But leave too much allowance and it slides and gaps. So there is a narrow sweet spot - a cushion slightly smaller than the seat, enough to drop in cleanly, but not so much that it rattles with gaps. Judging that gap is an art few explain.
VISUAL: A cushion with the right allowance, dropping in cleanly; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] The thicker and firmer the cushion, the more allowance it needs, because a fat firm cushion cannot squeeze into a tight space the way a thin soft one can. A deep cushion cut to the same allowance as a thin one will jam. Match the allowance to the cushion's thickness and firmness.
VISUAL: A thick cushion needing more allowance than a thin one; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So the allowance is a real measurement, not an afterthought - small enough for a clean look, large enough to drop in without buckling, and matched to the cushion's thickness. Get the allowance wrong and even a perfectly-shaped cushion fits badly. It is the gap between paper and reality that catches most people.
VISUAL: A cushion with a well-judged allowance fitting cleanly; clean interior, warm light, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - CORNERS AND CURVES

[A] The third trap lives in the corners. A cushion must turn the inside corners of a seat cleanly, and this is where most fail. A stiff square cushion cannot press neatly into a corner - it rounds off, leaving a gap, or bunches, sitting proud. Corners defeat more cushions than any straight edge.
VISUAL: A cushion failing to fit cleanly into a seat's inside corner; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] For an L-shaped or wrap-around seat, the problem multiplies. The cushion must turn the corner of the L, and a single cushion rarely does this well. Often the answer is separate cushions that meet at the corner, each fitting its own straight run, rather than one large cushion fighting the bend.
VISUAL: An L-shaped seat with separate cushions meeting at the corner; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Rounded and bay seats add curves that a straight-cut cushion cannot follow at all. A bay window's angled front, a seat with a curved edge - these need cushions shaped to the curve, not rectangles pressed against them. A cushion must follow the true edge of the seat, curves and all.
VISUAL: A cushion shaped to follow the curved front of a bay seat; clean interior, warm light, no people.

[A] So plan for the corners and curves from the start. Decide whether one cushion or several will fit your seat's shape, and have each made to the real edges it must follow. The corners and curves are where a cushion fits or fails, and where careful planning saves the whole seat.
VISUAL: A cushion planned to fit a seat's corners and curves cleanly; clean interior, soft light, no people, no text.

## PART FOUR - GETTING IT RIGHT

[A] So how do you get a cushion that fits the first time? Measure the real shape - width at front and back, depth on both sides, the true angles of the corners. Make a card template of the actual seat if the shape is complex, and have the cushion made to it.
VISUAL: A card template made of a seat's real shape for the cushion; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Decide the allowance and the layout deliberately - how much smaller than the seat, and whether one cushion or several. Match the allowance to the cushion's thickness, and split a complex or corner seat into separate cushions that each fit a simple run. Plan the fit before you order, not after.
VISUAL: A cushion layout planned deliberately before ordering; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] And where the shape is tricky, have the cushion custom-made to your template rather than forcing a standard size to fit. A window seat is rarely a standard shape, and a cushion made to the real seat will always fit where a bought-to-guess one fails. The template is the secret to a first-time fit.
VISUAL: A custom cushion made to a template fitting perfectly; clean interior, warm light, no people.
