## PART ONE - MEASURING AND MARKING

[A] The first tool is the one beginners most underestimate - something to measure and mark accurately. It is not glamorous, but it decides everything. Woodworking lives and dies on accuracy, and accuracy starts with a clean, precise line drawn exactly where the cut should go.
VISUAL: A measuring tool and a marking line drawn crisply across a clean board, precise and deliberate; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] At the very least you want a good tape measure and a square - a simple tool that lets you draw a line dead perpendicular to an edge and check that a corner is a true right angle. A square is the quiet guardian of every accurate project. Without it, nothing comes out true.
VISUAL: A square resting on a board with a perfectly perpendicular line marked against it, clean and precise; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] The old carpenter's rule says it all - measure twice, cut once. The cheapest, most important habit in woodworking costs nothing but care. Mark accurately, check it, and half your mistakes vanish before the saw ever touches the wood. This humble first tool prevents more ruined boards than any other.
VISUAL: A board with careful measurement marks drawn on it, clean and exact, ready to cut; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

## PART TWO - A WAY TO CUT

[A] The second tool is a way to cut wood to size, and this is where beginners overspend the most, agonising over expensive machines. You do not need them to start. You need one good saw you can control - and a sharp handsaw or a simple circular saw will take you a remarkably long way.
VISUAL: A clean handsaw resting beside a board with a marked cut line, simple and ready; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] A handsaw is cheap, quiet, needs no power, and teaches you to cut straight by feel - a skill that makes you better at everything else. A circular saw cuts faster and handles bigger boards. Either one lets you do the fundamental job - turn a long board into the pieces your project needs.
VISUAL: A circular saw resting on a board beside a freshly cut clean edge, practical and ready; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] Whichever you choose, the rule is the same - one good saw beats three cheap ones. A sharp, well-made saw cuts cleanly and safely; a dull, flimsy one fights you and tears the wood. Spend your money on quality here, learn to use it well, and you can build almost anything.
VISUAL: A single quality saw resting cleanly on a workbench, well-made and sharp; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - DRILLING AND JOINING

[A] The third tool is a drill - and it's the one tool here where a power version truly earns its keep from day one. A cordless drill bores clean holes for screws and dowels, drives the screws home, and does in seconds what would take real effort by hand.
VISUAL: A cordless drill resting beside a board with neat drilled holes, practical and ready; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] With a drill you can join wood simply and strongly - pilot holes to stop splitting, screws to pull pieces tight, dowels to align them. It turns separate boards into a solid, assembled thing. For a beginner, no single tool unlocks more real projects than a good drill.
VISUAL: Two boards joined cleanly with screws through neatly drilled holes, solid and tight; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] Buy a drill with a little power and a second battery, so it never dies mid-job, and it will outwork every other tool you own. It is the one place a beginner can reach for power early and never regret it. A good drill repays its cost on the very first project.
VISUAL: A sturdy cordless drill resting on a clean workbench, capable and ready; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART FOUR - SHAPING, HOLDING, AND THE HONEST TRUTH

[A] The fourth tool is a small set of chisels. A sharp chisel cleans up a joint, pares away waste, chops a recess, and does the fine shaping that separates rough work from clean work. You do not need many - a few good chisels, kept sharp, handle nearly everything a beginner meets.
VISUAL: A small set of woodworking chisels laid out neatly on a clean surface, sharp and orderly; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] But the secret with chisels is sharpness, not quantity. A sharp chisel is a joy and a dull one is dangerous, slipping and tearing the wood. Learning to keep an edge keen is worth more than owning a dozen blades. Three sharp chisels beat ten dull ones every single time.
VISUAL: A single keen chisel edge catching the light, clean and sharp, resting on a board; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] And the fifth tool is the one everyone forgets and everyone runs short of - clamps. Glue needs pressure to hold while it dries, and clamps are the extra hands that squeeze a joint tight and hold it true. The old workshop joke is that you can never own too many, and it's entirely true.
VISUAL: Several clamps holding two glued boards tightly together while they set, practical and firm; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] So there is the honest list - something to measure and mark, a saw, a drill, a few sharp chisels, and clamps. Add sandpaper to smooth your work, and you can build real, lasting projects. Everything else on that overwhelming wall can wait until a project actually demands it.
VISUAL: The five core tools arranged cleanly together on a workbench, modest and complete; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] Start with a few good tools, learn them deeply, and let your kit grow only when a real need appears. That is how every master began - not with a full wall, but with a small box and the skill to use it. Next time, we'll follow a tree's whole journey into a finished board.
