## PART ONE - THE CUSHION SURPRISE

[A] The first hidden cost is the one that shocks people most - the cushion. Everyone treats it as a cheap afterthought, but a good window seat cushion, made to fit an odd-shaped seat in quality fabric with proper filling, can cost a startling amount - sometimes as much as all the wood combined.
VISUAL: A quality window seat cushion, a surprising cost; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] The reason is that a window seat is rarely a standard size, so the cushion must be custom-made - and custom upholstery, cut and filled and covered to your exact shape, is genuinely expensive. The bigger, thicker, and more custom the cushion, and the finer the fabric, the more it climbs.
VISUAL: A custom-made cushion driving up the cost; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] And the cushion isn't optional. It is half the comfort of the seat, so you can't simply skip it to save money. A window seat without a good cushion is a hard bench nobody sits on. So this is a cost you must bear, and one you should budget for properly from the very start.
VISUAL: A window seat needing its expensive cushion to work; clean interior, warm light, no people.

[A] So budget for the cushion as a major cost, not a cheap extra - often a real fraction of the project. It's the most common budget surprise in a window seat, catching out people who planned for the wood and forgot that the soft part they sit on costs real money too.
VISUAL: A cushion budgeted as the major cost it is; clean interior, soft light, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - THE COSTS IN THE WALL

[A] The second set of hidden costs lurks in the wall behind the seat. Building a window seat often means dealing with what is already there - and what is there can be expensive to move. The most common surprise is a radiator sitting exactly where the seat must go.
VISUAL: A radiator sitting where a window seat must go; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Move or reroute a radiator, and you have added a plumber's bill to your woodworking project - a real and often large cost that has nothing to do with the seat itself. Skirting boards, electrical sockets, vents, and pipes in the way all bring the same kind of hidden expense to relocate.
VISUAL: A window seat build blocked by pipes and sockets; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] There is also the cost of dealing with the window and wall themselves. An old draughty window may need sealing; a cold outside wall may need insulating so the seat is not freezing. These are costs of doing the seat properly, easy to overlook until the seat is cold and you wish you had.
VISUAL: A window seat needing wall and window work; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So check the wall before you budget. A radiator, sockets, pipes, or a draughty cold window in the seat's path can all add hidden costs that dwarf the wood. Knowing what lurks behind the seat before you start is the difference between a smooth project and a cascade of expensive surprises.

## PART THREE - THE COST OF SPACE

[A] The third hidden cost is subtler - the cost of the space itself. A window seat takes room, and that room was doing something before. Building the seat may mean losing storage, floor space, or a use the spot had, and that loss is a real cost, even if no money changes hands.

[A] Sometimes building a window seat means giving up more storage than it provides. If you rip out a cupboard or lose usable floor to gain a seat with a small storage box, you may end up with less useful space than before. Weigh what the spot did against what the seat will do.
VISUAL: A window seat costing more storage than it gives; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] There is also the cost of what you cannot easily undo. A built-in window seat is permanent, so if you tire of it or it does not work, removing it costs money and leaves a scar. The permanence that makes a built-in lovely also makes a mistake expensive to reverse.
VISUAL: A permanent window seat, costly to undo; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So count the cost of the space and the permanence, not just the materials. Losing storage, floor, or a useful spot is a real price, and a built-in you may regret is dearer still. The truest cost includes what you give up to have it, not only what you spend to build it.
