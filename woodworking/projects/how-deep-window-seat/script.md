## PART ONE - THE RULE THAT NEVER FAILS

[A] Here is the rule, and it's beautifully simple. For a window seat you'll sit on upright, like a chair, the depth from front to back should be about the same as the depth of a normal chair seat - deep enough for your thighs to rest fully.
VISUAL: A window seat with its depth matching a comfortable chair seat, well proportioned; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] In plain terms, that's roughly the length of your thigh - from the back of your knee to your seat. Sit that deep and your legs are fully supported, your feet reach the floor, and your back rests against the wall or a cushion behind.
VISUAL: A window seat depth shown as matching the thigh from knee to seat, clean and diagrammatic; clean interior, warm light, no people.

[A] Why does this depth work so well? Because a seat must do two things at once - support your thighs and let your back rest. Too shallow, and your thighs hang off the front edge with no support. Too deep, and your back can never reach the wall, leaving you to slump or perch forward.
VISUAL: A well-proportioned seat supporting the thigh while allowing the back to rest against the wall; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So the rule that never fails is this - match a good chair. If it works beneath you in a dining chair or an armchair, it will work in a window seat. The human body has not changed, and the depth that has always made a chair comfortable makes a window seat comfortable too.
VISUAL: A window seat and a comfortable chair shown together sharing the same seat depth; clean interior, soft light, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - WHERE PEOPLE GO WRONG

[A] Now the two great mistakes, and why they happen. The first is building too shallow. It usually comes from trying to fit the seat onto the thin ledge of an existing windowsill, taking whatever narrow depth the sill offers rather than the depth the body needs. The result is a perch, not a seat.
VISUAL: A too-shallow window seat built onto a thin sill, a perch rather than a seat; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] A shallow seat pushes you to the very front edge, thighs unsupported, back nowhere near the wall. You cannot relax on it. You balance on it. Many a beautiful-looking window seat goes unused for exactly this reason - it was built to the width of the sill instead of the depth of a person.
VISUAL: A shallow window seat that forces perching on the front edge, uncomfortable; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] The second mistake is building too deep - usually from wanting the seat to double as a bed, or from a bay that projects far out. A deep seat leaves your back stranded far from any wall, so you must pile cushions behind you or sit marooned in the middle.
VISUAL: A too-deep window seat leaving the back far from the wall, cushions needed to fill the gap; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] A too-deep seat isn't useless, but it must be handled deliberately - with a bank of cushions along the back wall to bring the resting point forward to where a person actually sits. Deep is only comfortable if you fill the extra depth behind. Left bare, it strands you. Cushioned well, it can work beautifully.
VISUAL: A deep window seat made comfortable with a bank of cushions filling the back depth; clean interior, warm light, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - CHANGING THE RULE ON PURPOSE

[A] The rule gives you the everyday depth. But sometimes you want to break it on purpose - and the trick is to know why. If the seat is meant not for upright sitting but for curling up sideways, knees drawn to your chest, you want it deeper than a chair.
VISUAL: A deeper window seat sized for curling up sideways with knees drawn in; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] For that curled-up reading nook, add depth beyond the chair rule and fill the back with cushions. Now the extra depth is a feature, not a fault - it gives you room to tuck your feet up and lean into the corner, wrapped rather than perched. The rule bends, but only because the use changed.
VISUAL: A deep cushioned reading nook made for curling up, the extra depth a feature; clean interior, warm light, no people.

[A] And if you want a true daybed - a seat wide enough to lie down on - you go deeper still, to about the width of a single bed, so a whole person can stretch out along it. This is the deepest a window seat goes.
VISUAL: A daybed window seat as deep as a single bed, room to lie down fully; clean interior, soft daylight, no people.

[A] So the depth follows the use. To sit upright, match a chair. To curl up, go deeper and cushion the back. To lie down, go as deep as a bed. Decide first how you will use the seat, and the right depth follows from that choice rather than from the window or the wall.

## PART FOUR - GETTING IT RIGHT

[A] So before you build, do the simplest test there is. Sit in a chair you find comfortable and notice how deep its seat is, how your thighs rest and your back leans. That depth is your target. Carry it to the window, and you carry a lifetime of comfortable seating with it.
VISUAL: A comfortable chair beside a window seat being built to match its depth; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Then decide your use honestly. Upright seat, curl-up nook, or lie-down daybed - each wants a different depth, and only you know which you truly want. Build for the way you will really use it, not the deepest the bay allows or the shallowest the sill offers. The use sets the depth, every time.
