## PART ONE - THE TWO WAYS WOOD CARRIES LOAD

[A] Wood carries weight in two completely different ways, and they have completely different strengths. The first is compression - squeezing. Push down on the end of an upright board, along the direction of the grain, and you're compressing those long fibres end to end, like pressing on a bundle of drinking straws stood on end.
VISUAL: An upright wooden post shown with the grain running vertically, a downward force pressing along its length, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] In compression, wood is tremendously strong. Those fibres running the length of the board resist being crushed beautifully, which is why an upright post can carry an enormous load. This is the wood doing what a tree trunk did its whole life - holding weight straight down through its length.
VISUAL: A sturdy vertical wooden post bearing a heavy load straight down, solid and unflinching, clean composition; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The second way is bending - and this is where wood gets weak. Lay a board flat across a span and load the middle, and you are no longer squeezing along the grain. You are trying to bend it, stretching the fibres on the bottom and squashing them on top.
VISUAL: A horizontal board spanning a gap with a load in the centre, the board curving downward, the bending clearly shown; clean diagrammatic style, warm tones, no people.

[A] And bending is where wood breaks. As the board bows, the bottom fibres are pulled apart until, somewhere along their length, they tear - and the whole board fails in a sudden crack. The same fibres that were unbreakable in compression are now being asked to stretch, and stretching is their weakness.
VISUAL: A bent wooden board at the moment fibres begin to tear along the underside, the strain visible; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

## PART TWO - WHY SPAN CHANGES EVERYTHING

[A] So bending is the enemy, and the single biggest thing that controls bending is span - the distance between the supports. This is the number that quietly decides everything, and most people never think about it.
VISUAL: A board resting on two supports with a clear gap between them, the span emphasised as the key distance; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The longer the span, the weaker the board becomes - and not gently. Double the gap between supports, and you do not halve the strength. You cut it far more steeply than that. A board that easily holds a load across a short gap can collapse under the same load across a long one.
VISUAL: Two identical boards on a clean surface, one spanning a short gap holding firm, one spanning a long gap sagging deeply; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] This is why a shelf that holds your books over a short span will sag into a smile if you stretch it across a wide one. Nothing changed but the distance between the brackets - and distance, in bending, is everything.
VISUAL: A wooden shelf sagging visibly in the middle across a wide span, books implied by the bow, clean composition; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] So the first rule of trusting wood is simple. Support it often. Every extra support you add shortens the span, and shortening the span strengthens the board far more than you would ever expect. Closer supports beat thicker wood almost every time.

## PART THREE - ORIENTATION AND DIRECTION

[A] There is one more trick the wood is hiding, and it is nearly free. Take a board spanning a gap and turn it on edge - tall and narrow instead of flat and wide. Suddenly it resists bending far better, carrying much more weight across the very same span.
VISUAL: A board shown on edge, tall and narrow, spanning a gap and staying straight under load, beside a flat one bowing; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] This is why the joists under a floor stand on edge, tall and thin, never laid flat. A board on edge puts its depth to work against the bend, and depth is what fights bending best. The same plank, simply turned, becomes a far stronger beam.
VISUAL: A row of wooden floor joists standing on edge, tall and parallel, supporting a floor above; clean architectural study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And never forget the direction of the grain. Wood is strong along its length and weak across it. Load it along the grain and it holds; ask it to carry weight that tries to split it across the grain, and it gives way far sooner.

## PART FOUR - KNOWING WHAT IT WILL HOLD

[A] So when someone asks how much a two-by-four can hold, the honest answer is a question back. How is it standing - upright or spanning? How far apart are its supports? Is it flat or on edge? Is the load pushing along the grain or across it? Answer those, and you can finally tell.

[A] For anything that truly matters - a loft floor, a heavy shelf, a structural beam - do not guess and do not trust a single board's word for it. Span tables and a builder's guidance exist precisely because the maths of bending is unforgiving, and wood gives little warning before it lets go.

[A] But for everyday work, carry these instincts and you will rarely go wrong. Keep spans short. Stand boards on edge when they must carry weight. Load along the grain, never across it. Do that, and a humble plank will hold far more than you ever expected.
