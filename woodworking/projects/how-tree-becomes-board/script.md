## COLD OPEN

[A] Run your hand across a smooth, flat board on your bench. It is dry, still, and stable - a quiet, dependable thing. But it was not always this. Not long ago it stood in a forest, a living tree, swaying in the wind and pulling tonnes of water up from the earth through its trunk.

[A] The journey from that living, water-filled tree to the dry, flat board in your hands is long, deliberate, and almost invisible to most woodworkers. And yet it explains nearly everything about how wood behaves - why it moves, why it must be dried, why grain runs the way it does.

[A] Because the board did not just appear. It was felled, sawn, and then dried with enormous patience, losing most of its water before it could ever be trusted. Every stable board you own carries that history inside it. Understand the journey, and you understand the wood.

[A] So let us follow that path from end to end - the felling, the sawmill, the long slow drying, and the final milling that turns a rough timber into the clean board you build with.

## PART ONE - THE LIVING TREE AND THE FELLING

[A] It begins with the tree itself, and the most important fact about it - it is full of water. A living tree is mostly moisture, drawing it up from its roots through millions of tiny tubes to feed its leaves. The wood is not dry and still; it is wet, swollen, and alive with sap.
VISUAL: A living tree trunk shown with a sense of water rising through it, lush and full; clean natural background, soft daylight, no people.

[A] When the tree is felled, that water is still inside it. A freshly cut log is heavy and damp, soaked through with moisture - what woodworkers call green wood. It cannot be used yet. Build with it now and it will shrink, twist, and crack as it dries in your finished work.
VISUAL: A freshly felled green log, damp and heavy, resting on the forest floor; warm tones, clean natural background, no people.

[A] So the felled log is cut to length and carried to the sawmill, still full of water, still a long way from being a board. The first half of the journey is over - the tree is down - but the wood inside isn't yet anything you could build with. The water must come out.
VISUAL: Logs stacked and waiting at the edge of a sawmill, damp and freshly cut; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

## PART TWO - THE SAWMILL

[A] At the sawmill, the log meets the blade, and this is where its grain and character are decided. How the sawyer chooses to cut the log determines what kind of boards come out - their grain pattern, their stability, even their beauty. One log can be broken down many different ways.
VISUAL: A log being sliced into boards at a sawmill, clean parallel cuts revealing the grain within; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] The simplest way is to slice straight through the log, board after board, like cutting a loaf. It is fast and wastes little, and gives boards with sweeping, lively grain. Most lumber is cut this way. But the sawyer can also cut toward the centre for straighter grain and greater stability.
VISUAL: A log shown sliced in straight parallel cuts, the boards falling away with sweeping grain; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] Whatever the method, the log emerges as rough boards - thick, fuzzy-surfaced, uneven, and still soaking wet. They look nothing like the clean lumber you buy. They are raw, green, and unstable, the grain finally exposed but the wood not yet ready. The shaping is done; the drying has not begun.
VISUAL: A stack of rough green boards with fuzzy uneven surfaces fresh from the saw; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

## PART THREE - THE LONG SLOW DRYING

[A] Now comes the longest and most important stage, the one that makes wood trustworthy - drying. All that water must leave the wood slowly and evenly, until the board reaches a settled moisture in balance with the air it will live in. Rush this, and the wood cracks and warps. Patience is everything.
VISUAL: Boards stacked with thin spacer sticks between them for airflow, drying slowly and evenly; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The traditional way is air-drying. The rough boards are stacked outdoors with thin sticks between each layer, letting air flow around every face, and left for months or even years. Slowly, gently, the water evaporates and the wood shrinks and settles into stability. It cannot be hurried; the wood sets the pace.
VISUAL: A tall outdoor stack of boards separated by spacer sticks, weathering and drying in open air; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] The faster, modern way is the kiln - a great oven that dries the boards in days or weeks under careful heat and humidity. It does in a short time what air-drying does in years, driving the moisture out evenly and bringing the wood to a stable, reliable dryness ready for the workshop.
VISUAL: Neat stacks of boards inside a drying kiln, warm and controlled, drying evenly; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Either way, the goal is the same - to take the wood from soaking green timber down to a settled, stable dryness, in balance with indoor air. Only then has the water that made it move and crack finally left. Only then is it truly ready to become furniture.
VISUAL: A stack of properly dried, stable boards resting calm and ready, the drying complete; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART FOUR - THE FINAL MILLING

[A] The dried boards are stable now, but still rough - fuzzy-faced, uneven, not yet the clean lumber you know. The last stage is milling, where the rough timber is finally surfaced. Machines plane the faces smooth and flat and straighten the edges, revealing the clean wood beneath the rough sawn skin.
VISUAL: A rough board being surfaced smooth, clean flat wood emerging from beneath the fuzzy sawn face; clean instructional study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Out of that milling comes the board you finally recognise - flat, smooth, straight-edged, and ready. It has travelled all the way from a living, water-filled tree in a forest to a stable, dependable plank on your bench, and every stage of that journey left its mark on the wood.
VISUAL: A clean, smooth, finished board resting on a workbench, flat and ready, the grain glowing; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] And now, when you pick up a board, you can read its whole history. The grain tells you how it was sawn. Its stability tells you how well it was dried. Its very stillness is the quiet result of all that water having long since left. The journey is written in the wood.
VISUAL: A finished board held in soft light, its grain and character clear, the story of its making visible; clean neutral background, warm tones, no people.
