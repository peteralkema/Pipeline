## PART ONE - HOW PLYWOOD IS BUILT

[A] To understand plywood's strength, you have to see how it's made - and it's clever. Plywood is built from thin sheets of wood called veneers, peeled from a log and stacked in layers, then glued together. But the trick is in how those layers are turned. Each layer is rotated against the one below it.
VISUAL: Thin wood veneers stacked in layers with each layer's grain running at right angles to the one below; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] So in a sheet of plywood, one layer's grain runs lengthwise, the next runs crosswise, the next lengthwise again - grain crossing grain, all the way up. This cross-graining is the whole secret. It means that in every direction across the sheet, some layer's strong lengthwise grain is always pointing that way.
VISUAL: A plywood edge showing alternating layers with grain crossing at right angles, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Now remember solid wood's great weakness - it is strong along the grain but weak across it, and it splits easily along its length. Plywood cancels that weakness out. Every layer's weak direction is backed by its neighbour's strong direction. The crossing grains brace each other, covering each other's faults.
VISUAL: A diagram-style comparison of solid wood weak across the grain beside plywood braced in every direction by crossing layers; clean study, warm tones, no people.

[A] That is the engineering at the heart of plywood. It takes wood's lopsided strength - mighty one way, feeble the other - and by crossing the layers, makes a material that is strong in every direction at once. It is wood's weakness, deliberately designed out.

## PART TWO - WHERE PLYWOOD WINS

[A] So where does this make plywood genuinely stronger? First, it barely splits. Solid wood cracks along its grain at the slightest provocation - a screw near an edge, a sharp knock. Plywood has no single grain line to split along, because every layer runs a different way. It holds together where solid wood cracks apart.
VISUAL: A plywood edge resisting a split while a solid board cracks along its grain beside it, the contrast clear; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] Second, it stays flat and still. Solid wood swells, shrinks, cups, and warps with every change in the seasons. Plywood barely moves - the crossing layers hold each other in check, so a sheet stays dimensionally stable through damp and dry alike. For a wide flat panel that must stay true, that stability is gold.
VISUAL: A flat plywood panel staying perfectly true beside a solid panel cupping, a clear comparison; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Third, it's strong and stiff across a wide sheet in every direction, not one. A solid wood panel is strong along its grain and weaker across it, but a sheet of good plywood resists bending and carries load evenly whichever way you turn it.

## PART THREE - WHERE SOLID WOOD WINS

[A] But solid wood is far from beaten, and in several ways it wins clearly. First, raw strength along the grain. A solid board loaded along its length is tremendously strong - a beam, a chair leg, or a rail carries weight in line with its grain better than plywood of the same size.
VISUAL: A solid wooden beam bearing a load along its grain, strong and unyielding; clean study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Second, it holds screws and joints far better, especially on its edges. Drive a screw into the edge of plywood and it grips the thin layers poorly and can peel them apart. Solid wood takes a screw deep into firm fibres and holds it fast. For strong traditional joinery, solid wood is the better partner.
VISUAL: A screw gripping firmly in solid wood beside one pulling weakly at a plywood edge, the difference clear; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] And third, beauty and repair. Solid wood is the same all the way through - you can shape it, carve it, round its edges, and sand it deeply, and it's still solid wood beneath. Plywood is only as deep as its thin top veneer; sand through that and the layers below show.

## PART FOUR - WHICH TO CHOOSE

[A] So the honest answer is that neither is simply stronger - they are strong in opposite ways. Plywood is stable, split-proof, and evenly strong across wide flat sheets. Solid wood is mighty along its grain, holds joints firmly, and can be shaped and repaired forever. The right choice depends entirely on the job.
VISUAL: A plywood sheet and a solid board side by side on a clean surface, each strong in its own way; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] So reach for plywood when you need a wide, flat, stable panel - a cabinet side, a shelf, a broad surface that must stay true and not warp. Its stability and even strength are exactly what big flat panels want, and it will outperform a solid panel that wants to cup.
VISUAL: A clean plywood cabinet panel resting flat and true on a clean surface; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And reach for solid wood when you need linear strength, strong joints, shaped edges, or lasting beauty - a table leg, a rail, a frame, a worktop you will shape and refinish for decades. Where the wood must carry load along its length or be worked deeply, solid wood is the truer choice.
