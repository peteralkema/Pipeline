## COLD OPEN

[A] Take two boards cut from the same tree, the same species, milled on the same day. Set them in the same room. Wait a season. One stays flat and true as the day it was cut. The other cups into a shallow trough, its edges lifting away from the bench. Same tree, opposite behaviour.

[A] How can wood from one tree act so differently? They drank the same water, grew in the same soil, dried in the same kiln. And yet one is rock-steady and the other moves like it has a will of its own.
VISUAL: A cross-section of a log showing growth rings, with two board positions marked within it, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The answer is not in the tree. It is in the saw. The single decision of how each board was cut from the log - at what angle to those growth rings - decided its entire character before it ever left the mill. One cut gives stability. The other gives movement.

[A] These two cuts have names - flat-sawn and quarter-sawn - and knowing the difference is the difference between a tabletop that stays flat for a lifetime and one that warps within a year. It is one of the most useful things a woodworker can learn to read.

[A] So let us look at how a log becomes a board, why the angle of the cut changes everything, and which one you should reach for.

## PART ONE - HOW THE CUT IS MADE

[A] Picture the end of a log - a series of growth rings, circle inside circle, like a target. Every board sawn from that log slices through those rings at some angle, and that angle is the whole story. It decides how the grain looks and how the board will move.
VISUAL: A log end shown as concentric rings like a target, clean and diagrammatic, evenly lit; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The most common cut is flat-sawn. The log is sliced straight through, board after board, like cutting a loaf of bread. The growth rings meet the face of each board at a low, shallow angle, sweeping across it. It is the fastest, cheapest way to break down a log, with the least waste.
VISUAL: A log shown sliced in straight parallel cuts across its width like a loaf of bread, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The other cut is quarter-sawn. Here the log is first split into quarters, then each quarter is sawn so the blade meets the growth rings nearly head-on, at a steep angle close to a right angle. It is slower, wastes more wood, and costs more - but what it produces is special.
VISUAL: A log shown quartered, then each quarter sliced so the cuts run toward the centre at a steep angle to the rings; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] So the difference is simply the angle at which the saw meets the rings. Shallow and sweeping gives you flat-sawn. Steep and head-on gives you quarter-sawn. That single angle, set at the mill, decides everything that follows.
VISUAL: Two board ends side by side, one with rings running shallow across the face, one with rings running steeply through it; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

## PART TWO - WHY STABILITY CHANGES

[A] Now the part that matters most. Wood moves with the seasons - swelling in damp air, shrinking in dry. And crucially, it moves most across the direction of its growth rings, and far less along them. Where the rings point decides which way the board will move.
VISUAL: A board end showing growth rings with the direction of greatest movement running across them, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] In a flat-sawn board, the rings run shallow across the wide face. So when the wood swells and shrinks, that movement happens straight across the width of the board - and because the rings curve, the two edges pull differently from the middle, and the board cups into a trough.
VISUAL: A flat-sawn board cupping into a shallow trough, the curved rings on its end driving the edges upward; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] In a quarter-sawn board, the rings run steeply through the thickness, almost straight up and down. So the seasonal movement happens through the thickness instead of across the width - a place where it barely shows. The board stays flat. It still moves, but it moves where it does no harm.
VISUAL: A quarter-sawn board staying flat, its steep vertical rings directing movement harmlessly through the thickness; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] That is the whole reason quarter-sawn is prized. It is dramatically more stable - it stays flat, resists cupping, and moves less in width through the changing seasons. For anything that must stay true, that stability is worth the extra cost and waste it takes to make.
VISUAL: A quarter-sawn board resting perfectly flat and true on a clean surface, straight-grained and steady; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - HOW THEY LOOK

[A] The two cuts do not just behave differently - they look completely different, and you can learn to tell them apart at a glance. Flat-sawn boards show sweeping, looping arches of grain, often called cathedral grain, like the pointed windows of a church marching down the board. It is lively and beautiful.
VISUAL: A flat-sawn board surface showing sweeping cathedral arches of grain looping down its length; clean study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Quarter-sawn boards look the opposite - calm, straight, parallel lines running steadily down the length, with no loops or arches. It is quieter, more formal grain. And in some woods, especially oak, the steep cut slices through the tree's internal rays and reveals a shimmering flake of figure called ray fleck.
VISUAL: A quarter-sawn oak board showing straight parallel grain with shimmering silvery ray-fleck patterns across it; clean study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] That ray fleck is unique to quarter-sawn wood - a flash of light-catching figure that simply cannot appear in a flat-sawn board. It is why quarter-sawn oak was the signature of fine furniture for generations. The stability came with a beauty you could not get any other way.
VISUAL: A close study of shimmering ray-fleck figure on quarter-sawn oak, the silvery flakes catching the light; clean neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] There is even a third cut worth knowing - rift-sawn, made at an angle between the two. It gives the straightest, most uniform grain of all, with no cathedral arches and no ray fleck, prized for table legs where a clean consistent line matters. The middle path between the two extremes.
VISUAL: A rift-sawn board showing perfectly straight, uniform, even grain lines down its length; clean study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART FOUR - WHICH TO CHOOSE

[A] So which do you reach for? It comes down to a simple trade. Flat-sawn is cheaper, more available, yields more boards from a log, and gives that lively cathedral grain - but it moves more and is prone to cupping. For most everyday work, it is perfectly good and perfectly economical.
VISUAL: A stack of flat-sawn boards with sweeping cathedral grain resting on a clean surface; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] Quarter-sawn costs more and is harder to find, but it stays flat, moves less, and carries that beautiful ray fleck. Reach for it where stability truly matters - a wide tabletop, a door panel, anything that must stay dead flat through years of changing seasons.
VISUAL: A quarter-sawn board lying flat and true on a clean surface, straight-grained and steady; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] And now you can read any board at the lumberyard before you buy it. Glance at the end grain. Rings running shallow across the face - flat-sawn, lively but restless. Rings running steeply up and down - quarter-sawn, calm and stable. The cut is written right there on the end of every board.
VISUAL: Two board ends held up for comparison, one with shallow rings, one with steep vertical rings, clearly different; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.
