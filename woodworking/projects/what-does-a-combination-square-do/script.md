## PART ONE - WHAT IT DOES

[A] A combination square is a steel rule with a sliding head that locks anywhere along it. The head has a square edge and an angled edge, so the tool checks and marks right angles and forty-five degree angles, measures, and gauges depths - many jobs in one compact, adjustable tool. Its versatility is the point.
VISUAL: A combination square marking a right angle; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] Its most constant job is checking and marking square. Woodworking depends on true right angles, and the combination square checks whether edges and cuts are square, and marks square lines across boards for cutting. This one job comes up in nearly every project, again and again, all the time.
VISUAL: A combination square marking a square line; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] But it does far more. It marks forty-five degree angles for mitres, measures and sets depths, gauges a consistent distance from an edge like a marking gauge, checks flatness, and acts as a short straightedge. This range of jobs, all in one tool, is why it stays in the hand through a whole project.
VISUAL: A combination square gauging a depth; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] So the combination square measures, marks, and checks squareness, angles, and depths - a whole handful of layout jobs in one adjustable tool. It is not a specialist but a versatile everyday workhorse, reached for constantly. For general measuring and marking, few tools earn their keep as often or as quietly.

## PART TWO - WHY ACCURACY DEPENDS ON IT

[A] Because the combination square is used to check and mark so much, accuracy depends heavily on it being truly accurate itself. If the square is not exactly square, every line you mark and every angle you check inherits that error, spreading it silently through all your work. A false square quietly ruins accuracy.
VISUAL: A combination square as the source of accuracy; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] This is why a good combination square matters so much more than its humble look suggests. A cheap, inaccurate one that is not quite square will make your joints not quite fit, no matter how carefully you work. The square is a reference standard, and a reference must be trustworthy to be worth anything.
VISUAL: An accurate square giving trustworthy results; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] It also anchors consistency across a project. Using one trusted square for all your marking means every line shares the same reference, so parts align. Mixing tools or trusting a false square introduces small mismatches that compound. The combination square is the single reference that keeps a whole project honest.
VISUAL: A combination square keeping a project consistent; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] So accuracy depends on the combination square because it is the reference so much work is checked and marked against. A true one quietly guarantees accuracy; a false one quietly destroys it. This is why experienced woodworkers insist on a genuinely accurate square - it is the standard everything else is measured by.

## PART THREE - CHOOSING A GOOD ONE

[A] Choosing a combination square comes down to accuracy above all. Because it is a reference tool, it must be genuinely square and well made - a cheap one that is out of true is worse than useless, spreading error everywhere. Here, more than with most tools, quality is worth paying for.
VISUAL: A well-made accurate combination square; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] You can test a square for accuracy by marking a line against a straight edge, flipping the square, and marking again - if the two lines match, it is true; if they splay apart, it is not. A good square passes this test; a poor one fails. Never trust a square you have not checked.
VISUAL: A combination square tested for true squareness; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] A quality combination square is not hugely expensive, and it lasts a lifetime, so it is a wise place to spend a little more. A single good one covers most layout needs - a common size with a clear, accurate rule serves for nearly everything a woodworker marks and checks.

[A] So choose accuracy over cheapness, test any square before trusting it, and buy one good one rather than several poor ones.
