## PART ONE - WHAT IT DOES

[A] A coping saw holds a thin, narrow blade under tension in a springy frame, and you cut by hand, guiding the blade around curves and shapes. Its thin blade can turn tight corners, so it cuts curves, shapes, and intricate lines by hand - the hand-tool answer to curved cutting. Turning tightly is its talent.
VISUAL: A coping saw turning a tight curve; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] Its first job is cutting curves without power. Where a band saw or jigsaw cuts curves with a motor, the coping saw does it by hand - slower, but cheap, quiet, portable, and precise for small work. For curved cuts in a hand-tool shop, or where no machine is handy, it is the simple answer.

[A] Its blade can also be threaded through a drilled hole to cut interior shapes, and rotated in the frame to cut in any direction. This lets it cut out enclosed shapes and work around awkward lines, making it surprisingly versatile for such a simple tool. Interior and awkward cuts are well within its reach.
VISUAL: A coping saw cutting an interior shape; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] So the coping saw cuts curves, shapes, and interior cut-outs by hand, with a thin blade that turns tightly and rotates in its frame. It is the hand-tool curve-cutter - slower than powered saws but cheap, quiet, and precise for small work. For curves without a machine, it does the job simply.

## PART TWO - THE COPING TRICK

[A] The coping saw's namesake job is coping - a clever way of joining moulding at inside corners that fits far better than cutting matching angles. Instead of two mitred angles meeting, one piece is cut to fit against the shaped profile of the other, and the coping saw makes that shaped cut.
VISUAL: A coping saw shaping a coped moulding joint; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] This matters because inside corners are rarely perfectly square, so mitred joints there tend to gap and open. A coped joint, where one moulding is cut to hug the profile of the other, fits tightly and hides small errors, staying closed even when the corner is not true. Coping beats mitring at inside corners.
VISUAL: A tight coped joint at an inside corner; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] The coping saw makes this profile-following cut, trimming along the shape of the moulding so it nests against its neighbour. This is a job with no simple power-tool equivalent, and it is why trim carpenters keep a coping saw in their kit. For neat inside corners in moulding, it is the tool.
VISUAL: A coping saw making a profile-following cut; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] So coping - cutting one moulding to fit the profile of another at inside corners - is the coping saw's signature trick, giving tighter, better-hiding joints than mitres. It is a job the saw does simply and nothing else replaces. For anyone fitting trim and moulding, this alone makes the coping saw worth owning.
