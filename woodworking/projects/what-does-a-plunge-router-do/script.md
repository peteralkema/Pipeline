## PART ONE - WHAT IT DOES

[A] A plunge router sits on a spring-loaded base that lets you push the spinning bit straight down into the wood to a set depth, then release it to spring back up. This means it can start a cut in the middle of a board, not only from the edge, which a fixed router cannot.
VISUAL: A plunge router plunging into the middle of a board; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] This plunging unlocks interior cuts. It can drop in to start a groove or recess anywhere on a surface, cut a slot that begins and ends in the middle of a board, or hollow out a shape - all starting from a plunge into the wood rather than an entry from the edge.
VISUAL: A plunge router cutting a stopped groove; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] It also excels at mortising and controlled-depth work. Plunging to an exact depth makes it ideal for cutting mortises, stopped grooves, and recesses of precise depth, dropping in and lifting out cleanly. This controlled vertical entry and exit is exactly what such joinery and recessing needs. Precision in depth is its strength.
VISUAL: A plunge router cutting a mortise to depth; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] So the plunge router does everything a router does, plus the crucial ability to plunge into the middle of a board for interior cuts, stopped grooves, mortises, and precise-depth recesses. That plunging action is its whole distinction, opening jobs that entry-from-the-edge routers cannot reach. Middle-of-the-board work is its speciality.

## PART TWO - WHY PLUNGING MATTERS

[A] To see why plunging matters, consider what a fixed router cannot do. A fixed-base router has its bit set at a fixed depth, sticking out constantly, so it must enter the wood from an edge. It cannot start a cut in the middle of a surface. Whole categories of cut are simply closed to it.
VISUAL: A fixed router unable to start mid-board; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] Interior work needs plunging. A groove that starts and stops in the middle of a board, a recess hollowed in a surface, a mortise cut into a face - all require entering the wood away from any edge, which only a plunge router can do safely and cleanly. For these, plunging is not optional.
VISUAL: A plunge router doing interior work; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] Plunging is also safer for starting cuts. Dropping the bit into the wood in a controlled, guided way is safer and more precise than tipping a spinning fixed router into the surface, which is awkward and risky. The plunge mechanism makes starting interior cuts controlled rather than dangerous. Safety and precision go together here.
VISUAL: A plunge router starting a cut safely; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] So plunging matters because it opens interior cuts and precise-depth work that fixed routers cannot do, and does so safely and cleanly. It is not a minor convenience but a genuine expansion of what a router can make. Whether you need it depends entirely on whether your work includes these interior jobs.

## PART THREE - PLUNGE VS FIXED BASE

[A] So how does it compare to a fixed-base router? The fixed base is simpler, steadier, and often preferred for edge work and table mounting - its constant depth makes it stable and predictable. The plunge base is more versatile, adding interior and depth work at the cost of a little simplicity.
VISUAL: A plunge router and fixed router compared; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] The fixed base wins for pure edge shaping and router-table use, where its steadiness and simplicity shine. The plunge base wins for interior cuts, mortising, and stopped grooves, where plunging is essential. Many woodworkers value each for its own work - fixed for edges, plunge for interiors. They cover different needs.
VISUAL: A fixed router for edges, plunge for interiors; clean workshop, warm light, no people.

[A] Some routers come as a combo, with one motor and both a fixed and a plunge base, giving both abilities in one purchase. This is often the best value for a first full-size router, covering edge work and interior work alike. It sidesteps the choice by giving you both bases to swap between.
VISUAL: A router with both fixed and plunge bases; clean workshop, soft light, no people.

[A] So the plunge and fixed bases suit interior and edge work respectively, and a combo offering both is often the wisest buy. The plunge router is not simply better than the fixed one - it is more versatile for interior jobs, and less simple for edges. Choose by the work you do, or get both.

## PART FOUR - DO YOU NEED ONE

[A] So do you need a plunge router? If your work includes interior cuts, mortising, stopped grooves, or recesses, the answer is close to yes - plunging is the only clean way to do these, and a fixed router cannot. For joinery and interior work, the plunge router is genuinely needed.

[A] If you only shape edges and cut from the edges inward, a fixed-base router covers you, and plunging adds little. The plunge router's value rises exactly with how much interior and depth work you do. For pure edge shaping, the simpler fixed base may suit you better. Match the base to your cuts.

[A] For a first full-size router, a combo with both bases is often the smartest choice, giving edge and interior ability together at good value. If buying a single base, choose plunge if you foresee interior work, fixed if you do not. The combo removes the dilemma for most beginners.
