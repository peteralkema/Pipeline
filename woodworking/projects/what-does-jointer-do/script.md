## PART ONE - THE ONE JOB A JOINTER DOES

[A] A jointer does one thing, and it does it better than anything else - it makes one face of a board perfectly flat. Not roughly flat. Dead flat, with no twist, no cup, and no bow. It takes a board that rocks and gives it a true, level reference face to build from.
VISUAL: A wooden board passing across a flat machine bed, one face emerging perfectly level and true; clean instructional study, warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] It works by shaving away the high spots in tiny, even passes. The board slides across a long flat bed, a cutter beneath removes a whisper of wood, and because the bed is perfectly flat, the face that comes off is perfectly flat too. Pass by pass, the twist and cup disappear.
VISUAL: A close study of a board surface becoming progressively flatter and smoother, the high spots shaved away; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] And a jointer does a second job almost for free - it makes one edge perfectly square to that flat face. Run the edge of the board against the machine's upright fence, and you get an edge at a true right angle to the face you just flattened. One flat face, one square edge.
VISUAL: A board with one face flat and one edge being made square against an upright fence, the right angle clear; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people, no hands.

[A] That flat face and square edge are the foundation of everything. They are the two true surfaces every other cut measures from. Without them, your boards meet at sloppy angles, your joints gap, and your assemblies twist. With them, everything downstream falls into square.
VISUAL: A flat, square board resting true on a bench, clean and precise, the two milled surfaces emphasised; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - WHY IT IS NOT A PLANER

[A] Here is the confusion that trips everyone up. A jointer and a planer look similar and sound similar, but they do opposite halves of the same job - and you cannot swap one for the other. Mix them up, and your boards come out flat-ish but never truly true.
VISUAL: Two similar woodworking machines shown side by side on a clean surface, calm and diagrammatic, presented as a comparison; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The jointer makes one face flat, with no reference - it creates flatness out of a twisted board. The planer does something different. It makes the second face perfectly parallel to the first, and brings the board to an even thickness all over. It references the flat face the jointer made.
VISUAL: A board shown with its top face being made parallel to its already-flat bottom face, the two faces becoming even and parallel; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people, no hands.

[A] So they work as a pair, in order. First the jointer gives you one flat face and one square edge. Then the planer makes the opposite face parallel and the board even in thickness. Skip the jointer and the planer just makes a twisted board into a thinner twisted board.
VISUAL: A board moving through two stages on a clean surface, first flattened on one face, then evened in thickness, a calm sequence; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] That last point is the trap. A planer alone cannot flatten a twisted board, because it simply copies whatever shape it is fed. Feed it a banana and you get a thinner banana. The jointer is the only machine that creates flatness from nothing. That is why it comes first.
VISUAL: A gently curved board shown emerging from a planer still curved but thinner, the flaw preserved; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART THREE - DO YOU ACTUALLY NEED ONE

[A] So here is the honest question. Do you need a jointer? And the honest answer is - it depends entirely on the wood you start with. If you buy your lumber already surfaced flat and square from the supplier, much of the jointer's job is already done for you.
VISUAL: A neatly stacked pile of pre-surfaced smooth boards, flat and even, resting on a clean surface; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] But if you buy rough lumber - cheaper, often better wood, straight from the mill - then every board arrives twisted, cupped, and rough, and you need a way to flatten and square it. For rough stock, a jointer earns its place faster than almost any other machine.
VISUAL: A stack of rough-sawn lumber with uneven, fuzzy surfaces and slight twists, raw and unmilled; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] And a jointer is not the only way to do its job - it is just the fastest. A hand plane, in skilled hands, flattens a face beautifully and quietly. A simple sled under a router or planer can flatten a board too. The job is essential; the machine is one option among several.

## PART FOUR - THE FOUNDATION OF EVERYTHING

[A] So strip away the confusion and it comes to this. A jointer creates a flat face and a square edge - the two true surfaces every accurate project is built upon. The planer then makes the board even. They are partners, in that order, and neither replaces the other.
VISUAL: A perfectly milled board resting true on a bench, flat-faced and square-edged, clean and precise; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] Whether you need the machine itself depends on your wood and your patience. Buy pre-surfaced boards, and you can begin without one. Buy rough lumber to save money and get better wood, and flattening becomes a job you cannot skip - by machine or by hand.

[A] But understand the principle even if you never own the machine. Flat and square come first. Everything you build inherits the trueness - or the twist - of the boards you start with. Get the foundation right, and the whole project falls into line behind it.
