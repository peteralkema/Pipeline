## PART ONE - THE ONE FORCE BEHIND IT ALL

[A] Every warp comes from a single cause - wood changing size as it gains or loses moisture, and changing it unevenly. Wood is full of moisture, and it constantly trades that moisture with the air, swelling when the air is damp and shrinking when the air is dry.
VISUAL: A wooden board resting in soft light with a faint suggestion of moisture moving between it and the air around it; clean neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] If a board gained or lost that moisture perfectly evenly, all over, all at once, it would simply get a little bigger or smaller and stay flat. The trouble is, it almost never does. One part dries faster than another - and the moment the change is uneven, the board is forced to bend.
VISUAL: A board shown drying unevenly, one face or edge paler and drier than the other; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Picture it simply. If the top of a board shrinks while the bottom has not yet, the top becomes shorter than the bottom - and a board that is shorter on one side than the other has no choice but to curl toward the shrinking side. That curl is the warp.
VISUAL: A board curling toward its drier, shrinking face, the uneven movement clearly shown; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] So every warp is the same story underneath. One part of the board moved more than another, and the board bent to absorb the difference. Master that one idea, and all the different shapes of warping suddenly make perfect sense.
VISUAL: A single board resting on a clean surface with one face subtly drier than the other, presented as a study; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - THE FOUR SHAPES OF WARP

[A] Warping comes in four shapes, and each one tells you exactly where the uneven movement happened. The first is cupping - the board curls across its width into a shallow trough or dome. This happens when one face dries faster than the other, usually driven by the curve of the growth rings.
VISUAL: A board cupped across its width into a shallow trough, the edges lifted; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] The second is bowing - the board curves along its length, end to end, like a drawn bow, while staying flat across its width. This comes from uneven drying down the length of the board, often from poor stacking that let one stretch dry faster than the rest.
VISUAL: A board bowed gently end to end along its length, the centre lifted clear of a flat surface; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] The third is twisting - the most dramatic, where the board winds into a slow propeller, the corners no longer sharing a plane. Twist usually comes from grain that ran at an angle through the tree, so the wood shrinks in a spiralling, uneven way as it dries.
VISUAL: A board twisted into a gentle propeller shape, its corners out of plane with each other; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And the fourth is crook - the board curves sideways along its edge, bending left or right while staying flat. Different shapes, but one shared cause beneath them all. Each is simply the map of where the wood moved unevenly. Read the shape, and you can read the history of the drying.
VISUAL: A board bent sideways along its edge in a long shallow curve, staying flat; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART THREE - WHY SOME BOARDS WARP MORE

[A] Some boards are far more prone to warping than others, and you can spot them before you buy. The biggest factor is how the board was cut from the log. A flat-sawn board, with curved rings running across its face, is the classic cupper - those rings pull the edges as it dries.
VISUAL: A flat-sawn board with curved rings on its end cupping as it dries, the rings driving the curl; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] Wood that was not properly dried is the other great offender. A board still carrying too much moisture deep inside is a warp waiting to happen - it will keep drying and moving long after you build with it. Wood needs to reach a settled, stable moisture before it can be trusted.
VISUAL: A board with a damp, darker core implied beneath a drier surface, resting on a clean surface; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And watch for wild, irregular grain, knots, and wood from near the very centre of the tree. All of them shrink unevenly and unpredictably, and all of them warp more than calm, straight-grained wood. The straighter and more even the grain, the flatter the board tends to stay.
VISUAL: A board with knots and wild swirling grain beside a calm straight-grained board, a comparison; clean study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART FOUR - HOW TO PREVENT IT

[A] So how do you stop it? The principle behind every method is the same - keep the wood drying or moving evenly, never letting one part change faster than another. Get that right and warping has nothing to grip.
VISUAL: A neat stack of boards resting evenly on a clean surface, flat and orderly; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

[A] When you store boards, do not stack them flat in a solid pile where the top breathes and the bottom cannot. Lay thin spacer sticks between each board so air flows evenly around every face. Even airflow means even drying, and even drying means flat boards. This one habit prevents more warps than any other.
VISUAL: A stack of boards separated by thin evenly-spaced spacer sticks letting air flow between every layer; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, no people.

[A] Let new wood settle in your workshop before you build with it. Wood brought from a different place is still adjusting to the moisture of its new home, and if you build too soon, it finishes adjusting inside your finished project. Give it time to reach balance first, and it will hold still afterward.
VISUAL: Boards resting in a workshop acclimating, calm and settling, soft even light; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And when you finish a piece, treat both faces the same - seal the underside of a tabletop just as you seal the top. If one face can breathe moisture and the other is sealed shut, they move differently, and the whole top cups. Balance the finish, and you balance the movement.
VISUAL: A tabletop shown with both its top and underside evenly finished, balanced and flat; clean diagrammatic study, warm tones, neutral background, no people.
