## COLD OPEN

[A] Walk through a genuinely old house - a cottage, a manor, a farmhouse that has stood for centuries - and you will notice something. Window seats everywhere. Tucked into bays, set into recesses, built beneath the stairs, as though no room was thought complete without one. They are almost a signature of age.

[A] It is easy to assume this was just taste - that people long ago simply liked window seats and built them for charm. But that's not why they are there. Old houses had window seats for reasons far more practical than pretty, reasons woven into the very way those homes were built and lived in.

[A] And understanding those reasons tells you something the photographs never do - why the window seat was almost inevitable in an old home, and why it grew rare in ours. The window seat was not an ornament added to old houses. It was a natural child of how they were made.

## PART ONE - THE THICK WALLS MADE THEM

[A] The first and biggest reason is the walls. Old houses were built with enormously thick walls - of stone, of brick, of solid mass - because that was how you made a building strong and lasting before modern methods.
VISUAL: A very thick old stone wall with a window cut through it forming a deep recess; clean historical interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Cut a window through a wall several feet thick and you do not get a flat opening. You get a deep recess - a hollow the depth of the wall, framing the glass at the far end. That recess was space, real usable space, created for free by the simple thickness of the wall.
VISUAL: A deep window recess formed by the thickness of an old wall, usable space; clean historical interior, warm light, no people.

[A] And what do you do with a deep recess at about sitting height? You put a seat in it. It was the most natural thing in the world - the wall had already carved out an alcove, and a bench fitted into it turned dead space into a place to sit.
VISUAL: A bench fitted naturally into a deep window recess in a thick wall; clean historical interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So in old houses the window seat was nearly automatic. Thick walls plus windows equalled deep recesses, and deep recesses at sitting height cried out for benches. The seat was not designed in. It was the obvious use of a space the wall had already made.

## PART TWO - LIGHT WAS PRECIOUS

[A] The second reason was light, and how scarce it was. Old houses were dim. Windows were smaller and fewer, glass was costly, and artificial light meant candles and firelight - feeble, flickering, and dear. In such a house, daylight was precious, and the brightest spot in any room was right at the window.
VISUAL: A dim old interior with the brightest light falling at a small window; clean historical interior, soft shaft of light, no people.

[A] So the window recess was not just a cosy nook - it was the best-lit place in the house. To do any fine work - reading, sewing, writing, detailed craft - you went to the window, where the daylight was. A seat there put you in the one spot bright enough to work by.
VISUAL: A window seat positioned in the brightest daylight of an old dim room, ideal for fine work; clean historical interior, warm light, no people.

[A] This made the window seat genuinely useful, not merely pleasant. It was where the light was, and light was life for any close task. A household that spent its days spinning, mending, reading, and writing needed that bright window spot, and a seat made it a place you could stay and work for hours.
VISUAL: A window seat as a practical working spot in the best light of an old home; clean historical interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So the window seat earned its place twice over - the wall made the recess, and the light made it worth sitting in. In a dim old house, the seat by the window was no luxury. It was where the useful daylight gathered, and where the household came to make the most of it.

## PART THREE - COLD ROOMS AND CLOSE LIVING

[A] The third reason was warmth, or the lack of it. Old houses were cold - heated by open fires that warmed one end of a room and left the rest chill. People lived close to whatever comfort they could find, and the window recess, oddly, offered a small refuge within the cold room.
VISUAL: An old room heated by a single fire, the window recess a nook within the cold; clean historical interior, soft firelight, no people.

[A] Set back into the thick wall, a window seat was a partly enclosed pocket - a little room within the room, shielded on three sides by stone. Draw a curtain across the recess, as many did, and you made a snug retreat, holding a pocket of warmth and privacy apart from the draughty hall.
VISUAL: A window recess with a curtain drawn across it forming a snug enclosed nook; clean historical interior, warm light, no people.

[A] And old houses were crowded, shared, and short on private space. The window seat gave one of the few spots where a person could withdraw a little - sit apart, read, think, or simply be slightly alone while remaining in the room. In a busy household, that small pocket of retreat was precious.
VISUAL: A window seat as a small pocket of retreat within a busy old household; clean historical interior, soft light, no people.

[A] So the window seat answered the life of the old house at every turn - a bright spot in a dim home, a warm nook in a cold one, a private pocket in a crowded one. It was not decoration. It was a practical answer to the real conditions of living in an old house.

## PART FOUR - WHAT CHANGED

[A] So why did the window seat grow rare? Because the conditions that created it disappeared, one by one. Walls grew thin, and the deep recess that cradled the seat vanished with them. A flat window in a thin wall offers no hollow to sit in. The seat lost the space that had made it inevitable.
VISUAL: A thin modern wall with a flat window and no recess for a seat; clean plain interior, soft light, no people.

[A] Light grew cheap and plentiful. Large windows and the flick of a switch meant no room was dim, and no one needed to chase daylight to a single bright spot. The window seat's role as the best-lit working place in the house simply melted away as light became something you no longer had to gather.
VISUAL: A bright modern room evenly lit, no need to seek out the window for light; clean interior, soft light, no people.

[A] And homes grew warm throughout, heated evenly, so the snug shielded nook lost its purpose too. The window seat had answered problems - dim light, cold rooms, thick walls, crowded living - and when those problems faded, the seat that solved them faded with them, from inevitable to optional.
VISUAL: An evenly warm modern home where the sheltered window nook is no longer needed; clean interior, warm light, no people.

[A] So when we build a window seat today, we choose on purpose what old houses got for free - recreating the cosy nook deliberately, chasing the charm the old house made by necessity. Next time, we will look at the kitchen nook window seat.
