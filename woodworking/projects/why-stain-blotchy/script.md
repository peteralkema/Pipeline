## PART ONE - WHY BLOTCHING HAPPENS

[A] Think of stain as coloured liquid that the wood drinks in. The colour you see is pigment soaking into the grain and lodging there. So how dark a patch turns depends entirely on one thing - how much stain that patch of wood drank.
VISUAL: A close study of stain soaking into wood grain, the liquid sinking into the surface and darkening it; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] And here is the problem. Within a single board, the wood is not equally thirsty everywhere. Some areas are dense and tight-grained, and they barely drink at all. Other areas are soft and open, and they gulp the stain down greedily. Same board, wildly different thirst.
VISUAL: A wooden surface showing alternating dense and soft grain bands, the difference in texture clearly visible; soft even light, neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] So when you flood the surface with stain, the thirsty soft patches drink deeply and turn dark, while the dense tight patches drink little and stay pale. The result is the blotch - dark islands in a pale sea, drawn by the wood's own uneven thirst.
VISUAL: A board with stain showing dark soaked patches beside pale resistant patches, the mottled pattern clear; warm-brown tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] Certain woods are notorious for it. Pine, cherry, birch, maple, and alder all have this uneven, thirsty grain, and all of them blotch badly if you stain them bare. They are not bad woods - they simply need one step of preparation that bare staining skips.
VISUAL: A small group of pale wood samples known for blotching, arranged on a clean surface, soft even light; warm tones, neutral background, no people, no text.

## PART TWO - THE END-GRAIN TRAP

[A] There is one place every wood drinks too much, no matter the species - the end grain. The ends of a board are where all those tiny tubes are cut open, exposed like the open mouths of a thousand straws, ready to suck stain deep inside.
VISUAL: A close study of board end grain showing the open pores as tiny tubes facing outward, clean and diagrammatic; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Brush stain onto end grain and it vanishes inward and turns almost black, far darker than the face of the same board. It is why the ends of a stained shelf or table so often look burnt and wrong against the rest. Same wood, same stain, a wide-open thirst.
VISUAL: A wooden board where the cut end has stained much darker than the face, the contrast clear; warm-brown tones, clean neutral background, no people.

[A] So the end grain needs the same defence as a blotchy wood, only more of it. The principle behind every fix is the same - you cannot change how thirsty the wood is, but you can give it a drink of something clear before the colour ever arrives.
VISUAL: A bare board with its end grain facing the viewer, clean and ready, resting on a neutral surface; soft daylight, warm tones, no people, no text.

## PART THREE - HOW TO FIX IT

[A] The classic fix is a pre-stain conditioner. It is a thin clear liquid you wipe on before staining, and it partly fills those thirsty open pores with something colourless. When the stain arrives, the greedy patches are already half-full, so they drink no more than the dense ones, and the colour lands even.
VISUAL: A clear conditioning liquid being wiped across a bare board, the surface darkening slightly as it soaks in evenly; warm tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] A wash coat does the same job. A very thin coat of clear sealer brushed on first, then lightly sanded, leaves the surface evenly primed so the stain sits more on top than soaking unevenly in. Either way, you are levelling the thirst before the colour ever touches the wood.
VISUAL: A bare wooden board with a faint even sheen of sealer across it, smooth and uniform, soft light; neutral background, warm tones, no people.

[A] Or change the stain itself. A gel stain is thick, almost like a pudding, so instead of soaking deep into the thirsty patches it sits on the surface and spreads its colour evenly across dense and soft alike. For a wood that blotches badly, a gel stain can fix the problem on its own.
VISUAL: A thick gel stain sitting on top of a wooden surface in an even layer rather than soaking in; warm-brown tones, clean neutral background, no people, no hands.

[A] And mind your sanding. Sanding too fine can polish the surface so smooth it will barely drink at all, fighting you in the other direction. For most stains, stopping at a medium smoothness lets the colour grip evenly. Smoother is not always better when colour is the goal.
VISUAL: A sanded wooden surface with a clean even matte texture, neither rough nor polished, soft light across the grain; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

## PART FOUR - THE EVEN FINISH

[A] So the whole secret is this. Blotching is not a mistake in your technique - it is the wood's uneven thirst made visible. You cannot scold the wood out of it. You can only feed it something clear first, so that when the colour comes, every part drinks its fair share.

[A] Always test on an offcut of the same wood first. Stain a scrap, see how it behaves, and you will know before you touch the real piece whether it needs conditioning, a gel stain, or nothing at all. A two-minute test saves a ruined project every time.
