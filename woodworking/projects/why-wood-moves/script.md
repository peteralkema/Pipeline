## PART ONE — WHY IT MOVES AT ALL

[A] Start with what wood really is. A tree spends its whole life pulling water up from its roots to its leaves, and it does this through millions of tiny tubes running the length of the trunk — long, thin straws, bundled together, stacked from the ground to the crown.
VISUAL: A stylised close view of vertical wood fibres like bundled tubes running top to bottom through a section of pale wood, clean and diagrammatic, evenly lit; warm tones, no people.

[A] When the tree is cut into boards, those tubes do not disappear. They are still there, running the length of every plank — and they behave like what they are. Like a bundle of straws. Like a sponge made of fibre. They take on water, and they give it back.
VISUAL: A length of board shown with its fibres running clearly along its length, the surface suggesting countless fine parallel tubes; clean instructional look, warm wood tone, no people.

[A] And the water never stops moving. The air around us always carries moisture — more in a humid summer, less in a dry heated winter. Wood is restless. It is always trying to match the air around it, pulling moisture in when the air is damp, releasing it when the air is dry.
VISUAL: A wooden board resting in a softly lit room, faint suggestion of moisture in the air around it, the wood the clear focal subject; calm, clean, neutral background, no people.

[A] When those fibres take on moisture, they swell — they grow fatter, like a dry rope thickening in the rain. When they dry out, they shrink back down. The board you built is not a dead thing. It is a bundle of fibres slowly fattening and thinning with every change in the weather.
VISUAL: A clean close study of wood fibres, the surface subtly fuller and rounded on one side, drier and tighter on the other, shown as a calm comparison; warm tones, no people, no text.

[A] This is the whole engine of the problem, and it is worth saying plainly. Wood moves because it is still, in a real sense, alive to the air — and the only question that matters for a woodworker is not whether it will move, but which way.
VISUAL: A single board centred on a clean surface, soft even light, the grain running clearly along its length, presented as the subject of study; warm natural tones, no people.

## PART TWO — THE DIRECTION RULE

[A] Here is the rule that saves your projects, and it is almost unfairly simple. Wood moves a lot across the grain — and almost not at all along it. The fibres run the length of the board. So the board barely changes in length. But across its width, it swells and shrinks enough to matter.
VISUAL: A board shown clearly from above with the grain running lengthwise, the long direction marked as stable, the across-the-width direction marked as the moving one; clean diagrammatic style, warm wood tones, no people.

[A] Picture that bundle of straws again. Push moisture into a bundle of straws and each straw grows a little fatter — so the bundle gets wider. But the straws do not get longer. That is wood exactly. It grows wide and stays long. Width moves. Length holds.
VISUAL: A clean diagrammatic bundle of tube-like fibres shown slightly fatter and wider but the same length, an arrow-free calm comparison of the two states; warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] Now the numbers, smaller than you fear and bigger than you think. Along its length, a board barely moves at all — nothing you would notice. But across its width, a wide board can swell and shrink by the better part of a finger's width between a damp summer and a dry winter.
VISUAL: A wide flat board shown alone on a clean surface, its width the clear emphasis of the composition, soft even light across the grain; warm natural tones, no people, no text.

[A] That is the number that wrecks tabletops. A solid top, glued up from several boards, is wide — and width is exactly where wood moves most. Across a broad top, that movement is enough force to split the wood or burst a joint. And it will not be denied.
VISUAL: A wide glued-up wooden tabletop seen from above, the seams between boards visible, the broad surface emphasised; clean, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] So the first rule of building with real wood is this. Never fight the width. The wood will win every time — it has more patience than you and it never stops pushing. You do not stop wood from moving. You give it somewhere to go.
VISUAL: A wide wooden panel resting on a clean surface, calm and solid, lit evenly to emphasise its breadth; warm wood tones, no people, no text.

## PART THREE — HOW IT DESTROYS THINGS

[A] Now you can see exactly why each disaster happens — and they are all the same disaster wearing different clothes. Take the split tabletop from the very beginning. A solid top is screwed down hard to a frame on every side, pinned so it cannot move.

[A] Then winter comes and the air dries. The top wants to shrink across its width — but it is screwed down tight and cannot. So the shrinking pulls it apart from inside, and the only way left to relieve it is to crack. He did not fight nature once. He fought it on every screw.

[A] The jamming drawer is the same story in summer. It was built in dry weather, a perfect sliding fit. Then the humid season arrives, the wood drinks in the damp air, and the drawer swells across its width — wider than the hole it must fit through. It does not slide. It wedges.

[A] The cracked panel is the third face of it. A solid panel is trapped inside a frame, glued or pinned at its edges so it cannot move. The panel shrinks in the dry season, the frame holds it fast — and the panel tears, because something has to give and the frame will not.

[A] Three different failures, one single cause, every time. The wood tried to move across its width, something rigid stopped it, and the wood broke free the only way it could. Once you see the pattern, you cannot unsee it — and you will spot the mistake in furniture everywhere you look.

## PART FOUR — HOW TO BUILD WITH IT

[A] So here is how real furniture survives, the methods passed down precisely because builders learned these failures the hard way. The principle behind every one of them is the same. Hold the wood where it is stable, and let it slide where it moves.
VISUAL: A wide wooden panel resting calmly on a clean surface, the long grain direction the clear emphasis, soft even light; warm natural tones, no people, no text.

[A] For a solid tabletop, you never screw it down tight all around. You fasten it in a way that grips firmly but still lets the top slide across its width as it swells and shrinks. The top is held — but free to breathe. That single change prevents the crack entirely.
VISUAL: A wooden tabletop joined to its underframe with a fastening that allows side-to-side movement, the top sitting flat and unstrained; clean instructional view, warm tones, no people.

[A] For a panel inside a frame, you never glue the panel in. You let it float — sitting loose in a groove, free to shrink and swell without ever being pinned. The frame stays square, the panel moves quietly inside it, and nothing tears. This is why old cabinet doors outlast everything around them.
VISUAL: A solid wooden panel resting loose within a grooved frame, a small visible gap at its edges leaving room to move; clean composition, warm wood tones, no people.

[A] For a drawer, you leave a little room across its width — a deliberate small gap so the wood can swell in summer without wedging. A drawer fitted too perfectly in dry weather is one that jams in the wet. A touch of room is not sloppiness. It is wisdom.
VISUAL: A wooden drawer sitting in its opening with a small even gap along each side, calm and well fitted; clean instructional look, warm tones, neutral background, no people.

[A] And when you glue boards into a wide top, you can turn the movement against itself — alternating them so the way each one wants to cup is balanced by its neighbour, the whole top pulling itself flat instead of bowing. You are not stopping the movement. You are setting it to work.
VISUAL: A glued-up wooden top seen from the end grain, the growth rings of neighbouring boards curving in alternating directions across the seams; clean diagrammatic style, warm tones, no people.

[A] That is the whole secret, and it is less about skill than about respect. The wood is going to move. The good builder does not fight that — he plans for it, leaves room for it, and lets the wood do quietly what it was always going to do anyway.
VISUAL: A finished wooden piece resting in soft daylight, solid and calm and whole, the grain running clearly through it; warm natural tones, clean background, no people.
